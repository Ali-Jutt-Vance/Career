# Phase 0 — Chapter 4: The Business Track — Clients, Revenue, Independence

> *"Sales is not something you do to someone. It is something you do for someone."* — Zig Ziglar

---

## Chapter Overview

You want two things that most advice treats as alternatives: a senior job and your own business. They are not alternatives. Run correctly, they are the same asset compounding in two directions.

Client work produces portfolio evidence that job applications need. Job interviews produce technical sharpness that client work needs. Client revenue removes the desperation that makes you accept the first mediocre offer. A strong job offer gives you a floor that lets you raise your rates. The engineers who do best over a five-year horizon almost always have both, because the combination gives them options, and options are what compensation is actually made of.

This chapter defines the business track: what it is, what the realistic numbers are, and what happens on the days the plan says *"3 proposals."*

## What the business actually is

Not an agency. Not a startup. Not yet.

The business you are starting in these 137 days is a **productised service practice**: you solve one specific, expensive, recurring problem for one specific type of client, at a price that reflects the value rather than the hours.

That is deliberately narrow. The instinct is to stay broad — "I can build anything" — because narrowing feels like turning away money. In practice, broad positioning is the single most common reason freelance profiles get no responses. A client with a broken Postgres query does not hire a "full-stack developer". They hire the person whose profile says *"I fix slow PostgreSQL queries for SaaS companies."*

### The positioning formula

> **I build ⟨specific outcome⟩ for ⟨specific client type⟩ so they can ⟨commercial result⟩.**

Weak, and typical:
- ~~"Full-stack developer with 3 years of experience in Node.js and React."~~
- ~~"I do backend development, APIs, databases, and cloud."~~

Strong, and rare:
- "I build production-ready Node.js APIs for early-stage SaaS founders so they can launch in weeks instead of months."
- "I fix slow PostgreSQL databases for SaaS companies so their app stops timing out under load."
- "I build RAG systems over company documents so support teams stop answering the same questions."

The strong versions are narrower, and they will earn more, because they are the only profile that looks like the answer to a specific problem. You can always take adjacent work once a client is talking to you. You cannot get them to talk to you at all with a generic profile.

Your positioning is drafted on **day 5** and refined continuously. It will change three or four times over the 137 days as the data comes in. That is the process working.

## The realistic numbers

Freelance marketing is full of dishonest arithmetic. Here is the honest version, for someone starting with no platform history and no reviews.

### The conversion funnel

| Stage | Realistic rate | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Proposals sent → viewed | 30–50% | Most are never opened |
| Viewed → replied | 10–20% | A reply is the real first win |
| Replied → call booked | 40–60% | Speed of response dominates here |
| Call → contract | 25–40% | Improves fast once you have a process |
| **Proposals → paid contract** | **1–3%** | **The number that actually matters** |

At 3 proposals per weekday, that is roughly **65 proposals a month**, producing **one to two contracts a month** once you are past the cold start. This is why the plan schedules proposals daily rather than in bursts — the maths only works at volume, and volume only happens as a habit.

### The realistic revenue curve

| Period | Weeks | Expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Cold start | 1–5 | £0. Profile building, positioning, learning to read job posts. Zero revenue is the correct outcome here. |
| First break | 6–10 | First small contract, $150–$600. The purpose is the review, not the money. |
| Traction | 11–15 | 1–2 contracts, $500–$2,000/month. Rates rise because you now have reviews. |
| Compounding | 16–20 | $1,500–$4,000/month possible. Repeat clients and referrals begin. |

The first contract is worth far more than its fee. A five-star review with a specific outcome in it changes your response rate on every subsequent proposal. This is why the plan tells you to take a small first contract and over-deliver on it rather than holding out for a good rate. You are buying a review.

**Do not expect $10K/month by January.** It is possible, but it is not the plan and treating it as the target will make you feel like a failure at $2K, which would be an excellent outcome from a standing start.

## Where the clients are

| Channel | Effort | Speed | Rate ceiling | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Upwork** | High volume of proposals | Slow start, compounds | Medium–high | From week 3, daily |
| **Fiverr** | Front-loaded (gig setup) | Passive once ranked | Low–medium | From week 1, then maintain |
| **LinkedIn (inbound)** | Consistent posting | Slow, then sudden | High | Build all 137 days |
| **Direct outreach** | Research per prospect | Medium | Highest | From week 10 |
| **Referrals** | Zero, once earned | Instant | Highest | After the first happy client |
| **Communities** | Genuine participation | Slow | High | Ongoing, low cost |

Upwork is the primary channel because it has volume and a review system that compounds. Fiverr is secondary — the gigs are set up once and then largely passive. LinkedIn is the long game: 137 days of building in public produces inbound interest by December, and it serves the job hunt simultaneously. Every technical post you write does double duty.

## The proposal, which is the entire skill

Most proposals are rejected in under ten seconds, because most proposals talk about the freelancer. The client is not interested in you. They are anxious about their problem and scanning for evidence that you have understood it.

### The structure that works

**Line 1 — their problem, restated more precisely than they wrote it.** This proves you read the post and understand the domain. It is the only line most clients read before deciding whether to continue.

**Line 2–3 — your specific approach.** Not "I have 3 years of experience" but "I'd start by running EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the three slowest queries — in my experience with this pattern it's usually a missing composite index on the tenant_id + created_at ordering."

**Line 4 — one piece of proof.** A link to the specific relevant project, with the specific relevant number. Not your whole portfolio.

**Line 5 — one question.** A question invites a reply and demonstrates that you are thinking about their situation rather than pasting a template. "Are you on RDS or self-hosted? That changes whether we can use pg_stat_statements directly."

**Line 6 — a clear, low-friction next step.** "Happy to do a free 20-minute look at the query plan before you commit to anything."

That is six lines. Under 150 words. Longer proposals do not perform better; they perform worse.

### The rules

- **Never reuse a proposal.** If you would not be embarrassed to have two clients compare your proposals side by side, it is too generic.
- **Reply within the hour.** Response speed correlates with winning more strongly than almost anything else, because the client is anxious and you are the first person who seemed to care.
- **Video proposals convert several times better than text.** From week 16 the plan makes these standard. A 90-second Loom in which you look at their problem is a different category of application.
- **If fifteen proposals produce zero replies, the problem is your positioning, not your luck.** Stop and rewrite the positioning before sending fifteen more.

## Pricing, briefly

The full treatment is in Phase 11. The three things that matter now:

**Hourly pricing punishes competence.** Getting faster reduces your income. It also anchors the client on your time rather than their outcome. Use it only for genuinely open-ended work.

**Fixed-price per outcome is the default.** "$800 to cut your API p99 below 200ms" is a conversation about their result. It also lets your speed benefit you rather than the client.

**Retainers are the goal.** Monthly recurring revenue is the difference between a series of jobs and a business. From week 18 the plan has you proposing a retainer to your best client — typically framed as ongoing maintenance, performance work, and priority availability for a fixed monthly fee.

Raise your rate every three closed contracts. You will feel it is too early every single time.

## Getting paid from Pakistan

This is an operational blocker that stops more people than any technical problem, and it needs to be solved in week 1, not when the first invoice is due.

- **Payoneer** — the most established route for Upwork and Fiverr withdrawals. Set it up in week 1, verify it, and test it with a small withdrawal before you need it.
- **Wise** — excellent for direct client invoicing in USD, EUR, or GBP.
- **Upwork direct-to-bank** — available in Pakistan, slower, sometimes cheaper.
- **Keep records from the first rupee.** Every invoice, every receipt. Foreign remittance for IT services has favourable tax treatment in Pakistan, but only if it is documented properly and received through banking channels. Talk to an accountant once revenue starts.
- **Register when it makes sense.** Sole proprietorship with an NTN is sufficient at the start; formalise further only when revenue justifies the overhead.

Do the boring administrative parts in week 1 while there is no pressure. Nothing kills momentum like closing your first client and then discovering you cannot receive the money.

## How the business track appears in the daily plan

**Weeks 1–2 — Setup.** Payment infrastructure. Upwork profile. Three Fiverr gigs. LinkedIn rebuilt. Positioning drafted. Twenty job posts studied to learn the language clients actually use.

**Weeks 3–9 — Volume and calibration.** Three proposals every weekday. Track everything: sent, viewed, replied. This period is about learning to read a job post and recognise a good-fit client quickly.

**Weeks 10–15 — Delivery and proof.** First contracts land. Over-deliver. Collect reviews. Add each project to the portfolio. Rates rise.

**Weeks 16–20 — Systems and recurrence.** Video proposals. Direct outreach. Contract and onboarding templates. The retainer conversation. January planning with a raised rate.

Thirty minutes on a weekday and a couple of hours at the weekend. It is deliberately the smallest of the four tracks by hours, because it is the one with the longest lag and the least value in front-loading. But it runs every single day, because pipelines that are fed intermittently produce nothing.

## The relationship between the business and the job

Some people worry these conflict. They do not, and the interaction is worth being explicit about.

**Client work is portfolio evidence.** "I built this for a client who paid for it" outranks "I built this to learn" in every hiring conversation that exists.

**Client work teaches commercial judgement.** Scope, cost, trade-offs under a real budget. This is exactly what separates senior engineers from strong mid-level ones, and it is very difficult to acquire inside an employed role.

**Client revenue is negotiating leverage.** An engineer with $2K a month of independent income negotiates differently. Not aggressively — just without fear. That difference is worth more than any interview technique.

**The job funds the business.** A senior salary buys the runway to grow the practice properly, rather than being forced to take whatever pays this month.

The order that works is: **land the job, keep the practice running underneath it.** Not one or the other.

## Practical Tasks

1. Set up Payoneer and Wise this week. Verify both. Test a withdrawal.
2. Write your positioning sentence using the formula. Then write two alternatives. You will test all three.
3. Study 20 job posts in your niche. For each, write one sentence on what the client is actually afraid of. This is the single most useful exercise in the whole business track.
4. Build the tracker: proposal date, client, job type, viewed, replied, called, closed, value. You cannot improve a funnel you are not measuring.
5. Draft your six-line proposal framework — as a framework with slots, never as a template to paste.

## Self Assessment

- Is my positioning narrow enough that it excludes most clients? If not, it is too broad.
- Can I state the realistic conversion rate from proposals to contracts, and does my daily volume produce a sensible outcome at that rate?
- Is my payment infrastructure working *today*, tested, before I need it?
- Am I measuring the funnel, or just sending and hoping?
- Do I understand why the first contract's review matters more than its fee?
