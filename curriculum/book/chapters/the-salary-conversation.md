# The Salary Conversation

> [!GOALS]
> - Recognise the four verbal habits that cost money: **hedging, justifying early, filling the silence and apologising**.
> - Use clear sentences for each moment: expectations, current salary, receiving an offer, countering, ranges and declining.
> - Set a tone that is **warm, certain, grateful and slow**.
> - Rehearse the sentences and the silence until they are automatic.
> - Deal with the feeling that asking is greedy or risky.

## Why this matters

{{ch:negotiating-offers}} covers the strategy of negotiation: research, ranges, anchoring and the whole package. This chapter covers the **delivery** — the exact words, the tone and, above all, what to do in the moments after you say a number.

Negotiation rarely fails because of strategy. Most people know they should ask for more. They fail in delivery: the number comes out with a hedge, an apology or a nervous extra sentence that gives away most of its value. These are speaking habits, and like other speaking habits they can be practised. In a second language, under pressure, practice matters even more.

## The simple idea

Stating a salary expectation is like placing a chess piece. You decide the move in advance, place the piece firmly, and take your hand off it. Hovering, sliding it back and forth or apologising for the move tells your opponent you are unsure — and invites them to push.

> [!ANALOGY]
> Saying your number is like placing a chess piece: decide in advance, place it firmly, and take your hand away.
>
> **Where the analogy breaks:** chess has a winner and a loser. A salary conversation is closer to two parties solving a problem together — they want to hire you, you want to join. Firmness is not hostility; the tone stays warm.

## The proper definition and key words

The **salary conversation** is the spoken or written exchange in which you and an employer agree on pay and other terms.

Key words:

- **Expectation** — the pay you are asking for, usually as a range.
- **Band** — the range a company has set for a role or level.
- **Counter-offer** — your response to an offer, asking for better terms.
- **Anchor** — the first number mentioned, which influences what follows.
- **Walk-away point** — the lowest offer you would accept ({{ch:negotiating-offers}}).

## Four habits that cost money

### Level 1 — Recognise them

1. **Hedging the number.** "I was thinking maybe around 280 or so, but I'm flexible." Everything after "280" lowered it; the listener now hears something like 250 as your real position. Say: **"I'm looking at 280 to 320."** Full stop.
2. **Justifying before you are challenged.** "I know that's a lot compared with my current salary, but…" You have raised the objection yourself and argued against your own number. Give your reasons only if asked.
3. **Filling the silence.** The most expensive habit. You say a number, there is a pause, and the discomfort makes you add "…but I understand if that's not possible." You have just negotiated against yourself. **The silence is the technique**: say the number and wait. The pause is them thinking — exactly what you want.
4. **Apologising.** "Sorry to ask, but…" Discussing pay is a normal part of a professional agreement. Apologising signals that you think the request is improper.

## The sentences

Rehearse each one aloud until it is boring. Under pressure you fall back on what you have rehearsed. The numbers below are illustrative monthly salaries in Pakistani rupees; use the figures from your own research ({{ch:the-job-market}}).

### Level 2 — When they ask your expectation

> "Based on the role and what I've researched for this level in Lahore, I'm looking at 280,000 to 320,000 a month. Is that within your band?"

Number, reason, a question back. Ending on a question moves the conversation forward and stops you filling the pause.

### Level 2 — When they ask your current salary

> "I'd rather not anchor on my current salary — my current role is at a small company and isn't representative of the market for this work. My expectation for this role is 280,000 to 320,000."

Not a refusal: an explanation and a redirection. Many recruiters accept it without pressing. (In some places it is illegal for employers to ask; where it isn't, you can still decline politely.)

### Level 2 — When they insist

> "I understand. I'd prefer not to share it, but I'm happy to be completely open about my expectation, which is 280,000 to 320,000."

Calm, not defensive, said once. Do not raise the tension.

### Level 3 — When you receive an offer

> "Thank you — I'm really pleased, and I'm very interested in the role. Could I take a day to look at the whole package and come back to you tomorrow?"

Do not accept in the same conversation. Asking for a short time to consider is normal and expected.

### Level 3 — The counter

> "Thank you again for the offer — I'm excited about the team. I was hoping we could get to 300,000. I'm bringing three years of production experience and two deployed applications in your stack, which I think puts me at the upper part of the band. Is that something you can do?"

One number, one reason, a question — then **stop talking**.

### Level 3 — When they say it's above their range

> "I understand. What is the range for this level? And is there a level where that number would fit?"

Two questions, no concession. Sometimes you were being considered at the wrong level, and asking is the only way to find out.

### Level 3 — When the base salary is fixed

> "I understand the band is fixed. Is there flexibility on the title, a signing bonus, or a written salary review after six months?"

Other parts of the package can often move when the base cannot ({{ch:negotiating-offers}}).

### Level 4 — Declining

> "Thank you very much for the offer and for everyone's time. I've decided to go in a different direction, but I really appreciated our conversations and would be glad to stay in touch."

Warm and brief; you may want to work with them in the future ({{ch:written-english}}).

## Tone

### Level 4 — Warm, certain, grateful, slow

- **Warm, not adversarial.** You want to work with these people, and they want you. The tone is two people solving a problem, not an argument.
- **Certain, not aggressive.** The difference is in the words around the number, not the volume. "I'm looking at 280 to 320" is certain. "I really need at least 280" sounds aggressive and slightly desperate.
- **Grateful without being deferential.** Thank them sincerely, once. Then discuss the number as the business matter it is.
- **Slow.** Nerves make people speak fast, and a number said fast sounds negotiable. Say it a little slower than feels natural.

### Level 4 — Phrases to drop and replace

| Drop | Use |
|---|---|
| "maybe around… or so" | "I'm looking at…" |
| "I know it's a lot, but…" | *(nothing — wait to be asked)* |
| "…but I'm flexible" | *(silence)* |
| "Sorry to ask…" | "Could we discuss the salary?" |
| "Whatever you think is fair." | "My expectation is…" |
| "I need at least…" | "I'm looking at… Is that within your band?" |

## Rehearsing

### Level 4 — Practice that works

- **Say each sentence aloud many times**, standing up, as if to a real person. They must be automatic, because under adrenaline you will not build sentences from scratch ({{ch:interview-under-pressure}}).
- **Practise the silence.** Say your counter, start a ten-second timer, and stay completely quiet until it ends. Ten seconds feels enormous at first; repeat until it doesn't.
- **Record yourself.** Listen for hedges, apologies and the extra sentence after the number. Remove each one and record again.
- **Rehearse several openings**, because you don't control which comes first:
  - They ask your expectation.
  - They ask your current salary.
  - They name a number first.
  - They name a number well below your target.
  - They push for a decision on the call.
- **Role-play with a friend** who plays a tough recruiter.

## When it feels wrong to ask

### Level 4 — The real obstacle

Imagine your current salary is far below the offer. Someone offers you much more than you earn today, and every instinct says: take it now, say thank you, don't risk a good thing.

Three things are worth knowing:

1. **A polite counter rarely risks the offer.** Companies expect negotiation and often leave room for it. The usual downside of asking is simply "no, this is our best offer" — and you can still accept.
2. **The gap compounds.** Raises are usually a percentage of your current salary, so a higher starting point carries forward year after year, and it shapes the anchor for your next job too. An illustration:

```ts
// Illustrative only: how a difference in starting monthly salary carries forward when
// future raises are a percentage of the current salary.
const offer = 230_000;      // monthly, PKR — the first number on the table
const countered = 260_000;  // monthly, PKR — after one polite counter
const yearlyRaise = 0.10;   // assume a 10% raise each year
const years = 4;

const fmt = (n: number) => Math.round(n).toLocaleString("en-US");
let a = offer, b = countered, totalGap = 0;
for (let year = 1; year <= years; year++) {
  const gap = (b - a) * 12;
  totalGap += gap;
  console.log(`year ${year}: ${fmt(a).padStart(7)} vs ${fmt(b).padStart(7)} a month -> ${fmt(gap)} more that year`);
  a *= 1 + yearlyRaise;
  b *= 1 + yearlyRaise;
}
console.log(`total over ${years} years: ${fmt(totalGap)}`);
```

**Output:**

```text
year 1: 230,000 vs 260,000 a month -> 360,000 more that year
year 2: 253,000 vs 286,000 a month -> 396,000 more that year
year 3: 278,300 vs 314,600 a month -> 435,600 more that year
year 4: 306,130 vs 346,060 a month -> 479,160 more that year
total over 4 years: 1,670,760
```

   One polite sentence, said once, is worth well over a year's worth of a typical raise in this example — before counting its effect on future jobs.

3. **They are not doing you a favour.** They have a role to fill, interviewed several people and chose you. That is a business decision, and so is your answer.

It helps to reframe it: you are not asking for a favour. You are telling them, politely and once, what it takes — and letting them decide.

## Real-world use cases

### The added sentence

- **Story**: a developer said "I'm looking at 300,000" — then, into the silence, "but I'm open to anything reasonable." The offer came at 250,000. In the next process, they practised the silence; the same sentence, followed by quiet, led to an offer at 290,000.

### The level question

- **Story**: told their number was above the band, a candidate asked whether there was a level where it fitted. The company re-interviewed them for the senior role and made a higher offer.

## Common mistakes

### Beginner: hedging and filling the silence

**Fix:** a plain number, then quiet.

### Beginner: accepting on the call

**Fix:** "Could I take a day to look at the whole package?"

### Intermediate: justifying before being asked

**Fix:** give reasons only when challenged.

### Intermediate: sharing current salary under mild pressure

**Fix:** redirect to your expectation, once, calmly.

### Intermediate: countering repeatedly or aggressively

**Fix:** counter once, with one reason, warmly.

## Debugging and troubleshooting

- **You freeze when asked about money**: the sentences aren't rehearsed enough — practise aloud again.
- **You speak fast**: slow down deliberately on the number.
- **They push for an immediate decision**: "I'm very interested; I'd like a day to review the full offer. Can I come back to you tomorrow?"
- **Their number is far below yours**: "Thank you. That's quite a bit below what I was expecting for this level. Is there flexibility?" — then silence.

## Security summary

- **Get the final offer in writing** before resigning from your current job ({{ch:negotiating-offers}}).
- **Beware fake job offers** that ask for fees, bank details or identity documents early, or pay "too good to be true" salaries. Verify the company and the recruiter through official channels.
- **Share salary documents** (such as payslips) only when there is a genuine need, through official channels, and remove unnecessary personal details.

## How a senior engineer thinks about this

- **Pay is a business conversation.** Calm and professional, not personal.
- **Say it once, then wait.** Silence does the work.
- **Know your walk-away point** before the conversation.
- **Relationships last longer than offers.** Stay warm whatever the outcome.

## Summary

The salary conversation is mostly about delivery. Avoid the four habits that cost money: **hedging the number**, **justifying before being challenged**, **filling the silence** and **apologising**. Say your number plainly and then **stay quiet** — the silence is the technique.

Prepare sentences for each moment: stating your expectation with a reason and a question back; redirecting questions about current salary; asking for a day when you receive an offer; **countering once** with one number and one reason; asking about the range and the level when you are told you're too high; and exploring title, bonus or a written review when the base is fixed. Keep the tone **warm, certain, grateful and slow**. Rehearse aloud, practise the silence with a timer, record yourself and role-play the openings. And remember that a polite counter rarely risks an offer, that the difference compounds over years, and that the company chose you for business reasons — so answer as a professional.

## Key terms

- **Expectation** — The pay you are asking for, usually as a range.
- **Band** — The pay range set for a role or level.
- **Counter-offer** — A request for better terms after an offer.
- **Anchor** — The first number mentioned, which shapes what follows.
- **Hedge** — A word that weakens your statement ("maybe", "or so").
- **Walk-away point** — The lowest offer you would accept.
- **Compounding** — Growth that builds on earlier growth, such as percentage raises.
