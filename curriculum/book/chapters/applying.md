# Applying for Jobs

> [!GOALS]
> - See a job search as a **funnel**, and understand why both **volume** and **targeting** matter.
> - Find roles where they actually appear: referrals, company pages, LinkedIn, local job boards and hiring posts.
> - Decide quickly whether to apply, without filtering yourself out unnecessarily.
> - Run a short, repeatable **application routine** with a specific message for each role.
> - **Track** every application with a next action, follow up correctly, and diagnose problems by stage.
> - Look after your motivation during a long search.

## Why this matters

Applying for jobs feels like sending messages into silence. Many applications get no reply at all, and it is easy to conclude that the market has rejected you, or to keep sending the same generic application everywhere and hope. Both responses waste the effort you put into your skills, projects and CV.

A job search is a process that can be managed like any other engineering problem: it has stages, measurable conversion between them, bottlenecks and fixes. This chapter shows how to run it deliberately — where to look, how to apply efficiently, what to write, how to track and follow up, and how to read your results — building on {{ch:the-job-market}} and {{ch:the-cv}}.

## The simple idea

A job search is a funnel. Many applications go in at the top; fewer become replies; fewer replies become calls; fewer calls become technical rounds; and a few become offers. You cannot control each stage completely, but you can control how much goes in, how well it is aimed, and how well you perform at each stage — and you can see where the funnel leaks.

> [!ANALOGY]
> A job search is like a funnel: many applications enter, fewer replies, calls and interviews follow, and a few offers come out — so you watch where it leaks and fix that stage.
>
> **Where the analogy breaks:** a funnel works the same every time. Hiring is noisy: the same application can succeed at one company and vanish at another for reasons you never learn. Judge your process by patterns across many applications, never by one result.

## The proper definition and key words

**Applying** is the process of identifying suitable roles, submitting tailored applications, and moving them through the hiring process until an offer is accepted.

Key words:

- **Funnel / pipeline** — the stages from application to offer.
- **Conversion rate** — the share moving from one stage to the next.
- **Cold application** — applying without any personal connection.
- **Referred application** — applying with a recommendation from an employee ({{ch:referrals-and-recruiters}}).
- **Targeting** — choosing roles that match your evidence and goals.
- **Tracker** — your record of applications, statuses and next actions.
- **Follow-up** — a short message after a period of silence.

## The funnel

### Level 1 — Why volume and targeting both matter

- **Volume**: cold applications convert to replies at low rates, so a handful of applications usually produces no interviews. A meaningful signal about your search appears only after dozens of well-aimed applications.
- **Targeting**: hundreds of badly aimed applications with a generic CV produce no more than a few good ones. The reply rate multiplies everything after it, and it depends on the **match** between role and evidence, the **quality** of your CV, and the **channel** (referrals convert far better than portals).

The strategy is steady, well-targeted volume, with as many applications as possible through referrals.

### Level 1 — Where the roles are

In rough order of how well they convert:

1. **Referrals** — an application recommended by someone inside the company is usually read by a person quickly ({{ch:referrals-and-recruiters}}).
2. **Company careers pages** — roles often appear there first; check your target companies regularly.
3. **"We're hiring" posts** by engineering managers and recruiters on LinkedIn — fewer people see them, so they convert well. Reply or message the author directly.
4. **LinkedIn Jobs** with saved searches for your role titles and stack.
5. **Local job boards** (in Pakistan, for example Rozee.pk) — higher volume, more variable quality.
6. **Recruiters and agencies** — some excellent, some will submit you to anything.

## Applying well

### Level 2 — Deciding whether to apply

Apply when most of these are true:

- The type of company matches your target ({{ch:the-job-market}}).
- The stack overlaps substantially with yours.
- The experience requested is close to yours, or not specified.
- The location works for you, or the role is genuinely remote.
- You can describe what the company builds.

**Do not filter yourself out** because you do not meet every listed requirement. Adverts are wish lists; companies regularly hire people who meet perhaps most of them. **Do** skip roles in clearly different disciplines (data engineering, mobile, embedded, research) and roles far beyond your current evidence.

### Level 2 — A short application routine

With a system, an application takes a few minutes:

1. **Read the advert once, properly**: note the main technologies and the problem the role is meant to solve.
2. **Tailor the CV**: reorder skills to match the advert's terms; adjust the summary's middle sentence; put the most relevant project bullet first ({{ch:the-cv}}).
3. **Write the message**, with one sentence specific to this company.
4. **Submit and log it** in your tracker.

If a form takes a long time, decide consciously whether the company is worth it.

### Level 2 — The message

Most candidates leave the message field empty or paste a generic paragraph. A short, specific message stands out:

> Hello — I'm applying for the Back-end Engineer role.
>
> I have three years of experience building and maintaining a PHP/AngularJS product as its only developer, and I recently moved to TypeScript, Node.js and PostgreSQL, with two deployed projects including a booking API with measured query optimisations.
>
> Your advert mentions query performance on a growing dataset — that's the part of my recent work I'd most like to discuss; my repository includes before-and-after query plans.
>
> Happy to walk through any of it. My CV is attached.

The third paragraph is the one that works: it shows you read the advert and connects it to evidence. Never send the same message unchanged to every company ({{ch:written-english}}).

## Managing the pipeline

### Level 3 — Tracking

Keep one tracker (a spreadsheet is enough) from your first application:

| Column | Why |
|---|---|
| Company and type | See whether you are aiming too high or too low |
| Role and link | You will need the advert again before interviews |
| Date applied | Drives follow-ups |
| Source | Which channels work |
| Referral contact | Who helped |
| Status | Applied, replied, screen, technical, final, offer, rejected, silent |
| Last action / next action | The most important columns |
| Notes | Names, questions asked, salary discussed |

A live process with **no next action** is quietly dying; reviewing that column regularly keeps the pipeline moving.

### Level 3 — Follow-ups

- **After about a week of silence**: one short, polite message to a named person or in reply to your application: "Following up on my application for the Back-end Engineer role. I'm still very interested and happy to send anything that would help. Thank you."
- **After a further period of silence**: mark it silent and move on; do not keep chasing.
- **After a rejection**: thank them briefly and ask for one specific piece of feedback; some will answer.
- **After an interview**: a short thank-you within a day, mentioning something specific you discussed.

### Level 3 — Diagnosing by stage

| What you see | Likely problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Many applications, almost no replies | CV or targeting | Outcome bullets, projects at the top, better-matched roles, more referrals |
| Replies, but calls do not get booked | Slow responses or weak first impression | Reply within hours; improve your LinkedIn about section |
| Screens that go nowhere | Introduction or spoken English | Practise the two-minute introduction aloud ({{ch:speaking-about-your-work}}) |
| Technical rounds failing | Coding without help, talking while coding | Practise problems aloud, unaided ({{ch:talking-about-code}}) |
| Final rounds failing | Behavioural answers, communication | Written STAR stories, mock interviews ({{ch:the-interview-conversation}}) |

"I'm not getting a job" is never the diagnosis; it is always a specific stage with a specific fix.

### Level 4 — Common interview questions about your search

- **"Why do you want to work here?"** One real sentence about what they build or something recent they did, plus one honest sentence connecting it to what you want.
- **"Are you interviewing elsewhere?"** Honest and calm: "I'm in conversation with a few companies at different stages." If you have an offer, say so — it can speed up the process.
- **"When can you start?"** Know your notice period before you are asked, and do not offer to break it; employers notice how you treat your current employer.

### Level 4 — The psychological side

A long search is mostly silence, which is hard. Three things help:

- **Measure what you control**: applications sent, practice done, projects improved — not replies, which you do not control.
- **Separate the two tracks**: your engineering skills improve visibly as you work; the search is invisible for a while and then moves quickly.
- **Expect a delay**: applications sent now produce interviews and offers later. Silence early in a search is not a forecast.
- **Keep applying until an offer is signed**: slowing down because one process looks promising is the most common self-inflicted problem ({{ch:how-hiring-works}}).

## From beginner to enterprise

**Level 1–2 — applying sensibly.** The funnel; where roles appear; deciding whether to apply; a quick routine with tailored CVs and specific messages.

**Level 3 — running a pipeline.** A tracker with next actions; polite follow-ups; diagnosis by stage.

**Level 4 — sustaining the search.** Handling common questions; protecting motivation; continuing until an offer is signed.

**Level 5 — later in your career.** Fewer, more targeted applications; most opportunities come through your network and reputation ({{ch:portfolio-and-brand}}).

**Level 6 — the hiring side.** Understanding pipelines as a hiring manager: response times, candidate experience and fair processes.

## Real-world use cases

### The specific sentence

- **Story**: two similar candidates applied for the same role. One left the message empty; the other wrote three sentences linking their query-optimisation project to the advert's mention of a growing database. The second received a call the same week.

### The tracker that revealed the leak

- **Story**: after a long search, an engineer's tracker showed many screens but no technical passes, all at companies with live coding rounds. Practising problems aloud with a friend for a few weeks changed the pattern.

## Common mistakes

### Beginner: a few applications, then giving up

**Fix:** judge after enough volume.

### Beginner: the same generic application everywhere

**Fix:** tailor the CV and write one specific sentence.

### Beginner: filtering yourself out on wish lists

**Fix:** apply when you meet most of the important requirements.

### Intermediate: no tracker

**Consequence:** missed follow-ups and no diagnosis. **Fix:** log everything with a next action.

### Intermediate: chasing silence repeatedly

**Fix:** one follow-up, then move on.

### Intermediate: stopping when one process looks good

**Fix:** continue until an offer is signed.

## Debugging and troubleshooting

- **No replies**: check targeting, the CV's top third and the share of referred applications.
- **Replies only from companies you do not want**: your CV and profile point the wrong way; adjust titles and projects.
- **Long silences after interviews**: follow up once; keep other processes moving.
- **Offers below expectation**: prepare negotiation and gather real ranges ({{ch:negotiating-offers}}).

## Security summary

- **Recruitment scams**: never pay fees to apply or for "training" to get a job; verify companies and recruiters.
- **Personal data**: share ID documents and bank details only after a genuine offer, through official channels.
- **Unusual interview requests** (installing unknown software, sending money, very unusual chat apps) are warning signs.

## How a senior engineer thinks about this

- **A search is a system.** Measure stages, find the bottleneck, fix it.
- **Quality and quantity together.** Well-aimed volume, with referrals first.
- **Control what you can.** Effort and preparation, not outcomes.
- **Persistence wins.** Most searches turn after a long quiet period.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 — Decide whether to apply

**Objective:** practise targeting.
**The advert:** "Back-end Engineer — 3–5 years; Node.js, PostgreSQL, AWS; experience with Kafka and Kubernetes a plus; Lahore, hybrid." You have three years with Node.js and PostgreSQL, some AWS, no Kafka or Kubernetes. Apply?

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

Yes. The core requirements (experience, Node.js, PostgreSQL, AWS) match; Kafka and Kubernetes are "a plus". Tailor the CV to put Node.js, PostgreSQL and AWS first, mention your queue experience (for example a job queue with retries and idempotent workers) as related to messaging, and in the message connect one of your projects to what they build. In interviews, be honest about Kafka and Kubernetes and show you understand the concepts ({{ch:message-brokers}}, {{ch:eks}}).

</details>

### Exercise 2 — Write the message

**Objective:** practise specific messages.
**What to do:** write a four-sentence application message for a fintech company whose advert stresses reliability and data correctness.

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

"Hello — I'm applying for the Back-end Engineer role. I have three years of back-end experience and recently built a NestJS and PostgreSQL service with database constraints, transactions and tests that reproduce race conditions. Your advert's focus on data correctness matches the part of my work I care most about — my project documents how I prevented double processing with idempotency keys and verified it by delivering messages twice. I'd be glad to walk through it; my CV is attached."

</details>

### Exercise 3 — Diagnose the funnel

**Objective:** practise diagnosis.
**The tracker:** 80 applications (10 referred), 9 replies, 7 screens, 1 technical round, 0 offers. Most screens ended with "we'll be in touch". What do you work on?

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

Replies and screens are reasonable for mostly cold applications, but screens rarely lead to technical rounds — the bottleneck is the **screen**: likely the introduction, the reason for leaving, salary expectations or spoken clarity. Practise the two-minute introduction and the "why are you leaving" answer aloud, record yourself, prepare the salary answer ({{ch:the-salary-conversation}}), and ask recruiters what the band is early. Also increase referred applications, which convert better at every stage.

</details>

## Summary

A job search is a **funnel** from applications to offers, and it needs both steady **volume** and good **targeting**. Roles convert best through **referrals**, then company careers pages, managers' hiring posts, LinkedIn Jobs, local boards and recruiters. Apply when most important requirements match — adverts are wish lists — and skip different disciplines and roles far beyond your evidence.

Use a short routine: read the advert, tailor the CV with its exact terms, write a short message with one sentence specific to the company, submit and **log** it. Keep a **tracker** with status and next action, follow up once after about a week of silence, thank interviewers, and ask for feedback after rejections. **Diagnose by stage** — no replies, no calls, failed screens, failed technical rounds, failed finals — and fix the specific bottleneck. Prepare answers about why this company, other processes and your start date; measure what you control; and keep applying until an offer is signed.

## Key terms

- **Job search funnel** — The stages from application to offer.
- **Conversion rate** — The proportion moving from one stage to the next.
- **Cold application** — An application without a personal connection.
- **Referred application** — An application recommended by an employee.
- **Targeting** — Choosing roles that match your evidence and goals.
- **Application tracker** — A record of applications, statuses and next actions.
- **Follow-up message** — A short, polite message after silence.
- **Bottleneck stage** — The stage where most of your applications stop.
