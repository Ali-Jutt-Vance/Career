# Speaking Under Pressure

> [!GOALS]
> - Understand what happens when your mind goes blank, and why it is worse in a second language.
> - Use a rehearsed **ten-second procedure** for the moment of blankness.
> - Recover mid-answer and between questions without losing the rest of the conversation.
> - Reduce pressure in advance through mock interviews, low-stakes practice and preparation.
> - Replace the fear of "being found out" with an accurate view of what interviewers are doing.

## Why this matters

At some point you will be asked something you know and find nothing there. This is a physical event more than an intellectual one: stress narrows attention, and the thing you knew a minute ago is suddenly out of reach. It happens to everyone, including experienced engineers in high-stakes meetings and incident calls.

The difference between people is not whether it happens, but whether they have a practised response for the next few seconds. Without one, silence turns into panic, panic into a poor answer, and one poor answer into a poor interview. With one, the moment passes and the conversation continues. This chapter gives you that response. It builds on {{ch:questions-you-cannot-answer}} and {{ch:small-talk}}.

## The simple idea

A rehearsed recovery procedure is like a fire drill. Nobody thinks clearly when the alarm goes off, so you practise the route in advance — and when the real alarm sounds, your feet know where to go even when your mind does not.

> [!ANALOGY]
> A blank-mind procedure is like a fire drill: practised in calm moments, so you can follow it without thinking when the alarm sounds.
>
> **Where the analogy breaks:** a fire drill ends when you leave the building. Here, you stay and keep talking — the procedure buys a few seconds and gets your thinking restarted, and then the conversation carries on as normal.

## The proper definition and key words

**Speaking under pressure** is communicating clearly while stressed — in interviews, presentations, difficult meetings or incidents.

Key words:

- **Going blank** — a temporary inability to retrieve something you know, caused by stress.
- **Working memory** — the limited mental space for holding and manipulating information right now.
- **Filler phrase** — a short, natural sentence that buys time ("Let me think about that for a moment").
- **Reset** — deliberately putting a bad moment behind you before the next one.

## What is happening

### Level 1 — Stress and working memory

Under stress, the body prepares for a physical threat: heart rate rises, breathing becomes shallow and attention narrows. Working memory — the space holding the question, your half-formed answer and the words to express it — shrinks. Things you know become temporarily hard to reach.

Two useful facts follow:

- **It is temporary and physical.** It usually passes if you do not feed it with panic. It says nothing about your ability; treating it as evidence ("I don't really know this") makes it worse.
- **It is worse in a second language.** Speaking English uses working memory that a native speaker does not spend. Under stress there is less to spare, so your English gets worse at exactly the moment you need it most. Knowing this lets you prepare for it instead of being surprised.

## The ten-second procedure

### Level 1 — Four steps

Practise this until it is automatic.

1. **Say something that buys time**, out loud, so the silence doesn't begin:

   > "Let me think about that for a moment."

   This is completely acceptable. Senior engineers say it all the time, and it turns dead air into visible thinking.

2. **Breathe out slowly, once.** A long exhale is a quick way to calm the body, and takes a few seconds.

3. **Say what you do know, out loud.** Speaking helps you retrieve memory in a way silent searching often does not — and the interviewer hears reasoning instead of silence:

   > "Right — this is about transaction isolation. I know read committed is the PostgreSQL default, and serializable is the strictest. What I'm reaching for is which level prevents non-repeatable reads…" ({{ch:transactions}})

   Often the answer arrives while you are speaking.

4. **If it doesn't come, name the gap and move on**, using the structure in {{ch:questions-you-cannot-answer}}. Do not sit in the silence.

### Level 2 — More time-buying phrases

| Situation | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Need a moment | "Good question — give me a second to structure this." |
| Need the question again | "Could you repeat the question? I want to make sure I answer the right thing." |
| Need to narrow it | "Do you mean in production, or in general?" |
| Starting to reason | "Let me think out loud for a moment." |

## Recovering mid-answer

### Level 2 — Four situations

| What happened | What to say |
|---|---|
| You lost the thread | "Sorry, I've lost my thread — let me start that again." Then restate the question and answer in one clear shape. |
| You're rambling | "Let me be more concise — the short answer is X." Interviewers are relieved when candidates do this. |
| You answered the wrong question | "I think I answered a different question — were you asking about Y?" Noticing it yourself is a good signal. |
| You said something wrong | "Actually, that's not right — let me correct it." Self-correction is viewed well ({{ch:questions-you-cannot-answer}}). |

## Recovering between questions

### Level 3 — Reset deliberately

After a bad answer, the danger is that the *next* answers go badly too, because your confidence has gone. Many interviews are lost not on the hard question but on the easy ones that follow it.

- **Reset on purpose.** One breath, and one sentence to yourself: *that question is finished.* Treat the next question as the first of the interview.
- **Don't review while you're still in the room.** Replaying the last answer while the next question is asked guarantees a worse answer. The review happens afterwards.
- **Assume it cost less than you think.** Candidates usually overestimate the damage of one weak answer; interviewers judge the whole conversation.

## Reducing pressure in advance

### Level 3 — Preparation that works

- **Mock interviews with real people.** Much nervousness is a response to novelty; it falls quickly with repetition. Practise with friends, colleagues, mentors or communities that run mock interviews ({{ch:technical-interview-guide}}).
- **Interview at lower-priority companies first.** Real interviews are better practice than any rehearsal; get the first few out of the way before the ones you care most about ({{ch:applying}}).
- **Rehearse the openings.** Much of the stress is concentrated at the start; an automatic introduction carries you through it ({{ch:speaking-about-your-work}}).
- **Warm up before the call.** Speak English aloud first, so you don't start cold ({{ch:small-talk}}).
- **Rest.** A good night's sleep before an interview is usually worth more than extra last-minute revision.
- **Prepare your edges.** Knowing where your knowledge stops means reaching that point calmly rather than in surprise.

### Level 4 — Practise the procedure itself

Practise *being stuck*, not just being right. Ask a friend to ask you questions slightly beyond your knowledge, and use the ten-second procedure every time. Record yourself answering hard questions and watch for long silences, rushed speech and apologies. The first few attempts feel uncomfortable; after that, being stuck becomes a familiar situation with a known response ({{ch:talking-about-code}}).

## The fear of being found out

### Level 4 — A more accurate picture

Many people — especially those who have learned with heavy help from tutorials or AI tools — walk into interviews believing the conversation is a process of *exposure*: at some point the interviewer will discover they "don't really know anything".

Two corrections:

1. **Interviewers are not trying to expose you.** They are trying to find out what you can do, so they can decide whether it matches what they need. A gap they find is information, not a verdict. Every candidate has gaps.
2. **Make sure there is nothing to expose.** Only claim what you can defend. If your CV lists a technology you cannot discuss at the depth it implies, either learn it or remove it ({{ch:the-cv}}). Build projects you understand deeply and can explain line by line ({{ch:projects-that-prove-skill}}). Once everything you claim is true, the fear has nothing to stand on.

## From beginner to enterprise

**Level 1 — understanding.** Going blank is physical, temporary and worse in a second language; the ten-second procedure.

**Level 2 — recovery phrases.** Time-buying, restarting, summarising, redirecting and correcting.

**Level 3 — resets and preparation.** Resetting between questions; mock interviews, low-stakes interviews, warm-ups and rest.

**Level 4 — confidence from truth.** Practising being stuck; claiming only what you can defend.

**Level 5 — pressure at work.** The same habits during incidents, executive meetings and difficult conversations ({{ch:observability-and-incidents}}).

## Real-world use cases

### The blank on a known topic

- **Story**: asked about the difference between processes and threads — something they knew well — a candidate went blank. They said "Let me think about that for a moment", breathed out, and started: "A process has its own memory space…" The rest followed. The interviewer noticed nothing unusual.

### The lost interview after one question

- **Story**: a candidate struggled with one system design question, then answered three easy questions badly while still thinking about it. After practising a deliberate reset, their next interview included a similar stumble — and strong answers afterwards.

## Common mistakes

### Beginner: silence when you go blank

**Fix:** "Let me think about that for a moment" — immediately.

### Beginner: searching silently

**Fix:** say what you do know, out loud.

### Intermediate: replaying a bad answer during the next question

**Fix:** reset; review later.

### Intermediate: practising only alone

**Fix:** mock interviews with real people and low-stakes real interviews.

### Intermediate: claiming more than you can defend

**Fix:** align your CV with what you can discuss deeply.

## Debugging and troubleshooting

- **You speak too fast when nervous**: deliberately slow down and pause between points; it sounds confident, not hesitant.
- **Your English breaks down under stress**: use shorter sentences and your prepared phrases; simple English is fine.
- **You can't stop thinking about a mistake**: a breath, "that question is finished", and full attention on the next one.
- **Nerves are overwhelming before every interview**: more practice with real people; the novelty response fades with repetition.

## Security summary

- **Pressure is a common tool in social engineering.** A caller demanding urgent access, credentials or payments relies on stress to skip verification. Slow down and verify through official channels, however urgent it sounds ({{ch:application-security}}).
- **In incidents, stress causes risky shortcuts** — disabling security controls or running unreviewed commands on production. Follow the incident process and get a second pair of eyes ({{ch:observability-and-incidents}}).

## How a senior engineer thinks about this

- **Everyone goes blank.** The procedure matters, not the event.
- **Speak to think.** Saying what you know restarts retrieval.
- **One moment is not the conversation.** Reset and continue.
- **Confidence comes from truth.** Claim what you can defend.

## Summary

Going blank under pressure is a physical, temporary event that happens to everyone, and it is worse in a second language because English uses working memory that stress takes away. The answer is not to be less nervous but to have a practised procedure: **say something that buys time** ("Let me think about that for a moment"), **breathe out** once, **say what you do know out loud**, and if the answer still doesn't come, **name the gap and move on**.

Recover mid-answer by restarting, summarising, checking you answered the right question or correcting yourself. Between questions, **reset deliberately** and don't review in the room — interviews are often lost on the easy questions after a hard one. Reduce pressure in advance with **mock interviews with real people**, lower-priority real interviews, rehearsed openings, warm-ups and rest. Finally, replace the fear of being found out with the truth: interviewers want to learn what you can do, and if you only claim what you can defend, there is nothing to expose.

## Key terms

- **Going blank** — Temporary inability to recall something you know, caused by stress.
- **Working memory** — Limited mental space for current information.
- **Filler phrase** — A natural sentence that buys thinking time.
- **Ten-second procedure** — Buy time, breathe, say what you know, name the gap.
- **Reset** — Deliberately putting a bad moment behind you.
- **Mock interview** — A practice interview with a real person.
