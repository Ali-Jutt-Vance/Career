# Negotiating Your Offer

> [!GOALS]
> - Understand **anchoring** and why naming your current salary first is so costly.
> - Research ranges and write three numbers before any conversation: **walk-away**, **target**, **ambitious**.
> - Handle the offer conversation: never accept on the spot, counter once with a reason, then stop talking.
> - Negotiate beyond base salary: title, bonuses, reviews, leave, remote days, budgets, start date.
> - Read an offer letter properly — gross and net, basic and allowances, benefits, bonuses, notice periods, bonds, probation.
> - Handle multiple offers, below-target offers and declining, professionally.

## Why this matters

A negotiation usually lasts minutes, yet it decides your salary for years: every future raise is a percentage of your starting point, and your next employer may ask what you earn now. Engineers who spend months preparing for interviews often spend minutes on the offer — and accept the first number because they are relieved and afraid of losing it.

Negotiating is normal and expected. Companies plan for it; a polite, well-reasoned counter almost never loses an offer. The skills are simple and learnable: research, preparation, a few sentences practised in advance, and the discipline to pause before deciding. This chapter covers the strategy; {{ch:the-salary-conversation}} covers the exact English phrases.

## The simple idea

Negotiating an offer is like selling a used car you know well. You research what similar cars sell for, decide in advance the lowest price you will accept and the price you hope for, ask a little above it, let the buyer make the first move if you can, and never decide while standing excitedly in the street — you sleep on it.

> [!ANALOGY]
> Negotiating an offer is like selling a car you know well: research the market, decide your lowest and hoped-for prices in advance, ask a little higher, and never decide on the spot.
>
> **Where the analogy breaks:** after selling a car, you never see the buyer again. After negotiating an offer, the other side becomes your manager and colleagues. Be firm about numbers and warm about the relationship — one well-reasoned counter, accepted graciously, is professional; aggressive, repeated haggling damages a relationship you are about to start.

## The proper definition and key words

**Salary negotiation** is the conversation in which a candidate and employer agree the terms of employment — pay, benefits, title and conditions — after the employer decides to make an offer.

Key words:

- **Anchor** — the first number named, which pulls the final result towards it.
- **Band / range** — the salary range for a role and level.
- **Walk-away number** — the lowest offer you would accept.
- **Target / ambitious number** — what you expect to agree / what you ask for first.
- **Counter-offer** — your response proposing different terms.
- **Total compensation** — salary plus bonuses, benefits, equity and allowances.
- **Gross / net** — before / after tax and deductions.
- **Basic salary / allowances** — the core salary / additional components (house rent, medical, transport).
- **Provident fund / gratuity** — retirement savings and end-of-service benefits common in Pakistan.
- **Bond** — a commitment to stay for a period, with a penalty for leaving early.
- **Probation** — an initial period with different notice terms.

## Anchors and research

### Level 1 — The current-salary problem

In Pakistan and many other markets, you will often be asked your **current salary** — on the application form or in the first call. If you earn well below the market for the role you are moving into, this number **anchors** the offer: an offer far below the role's band can look generous to the company and enormous to you, while still being much less than the role pays others.

**Do not lie** — some companies check payslips, and a discovered lie ends the offer. Instead, **answer a different question**:

> "I'd rather not anchor on my current number — my current role is at a small company and isn't representative of the market for this position. Based on the role and my research, I'm looking for something in the range of X to Y. What is the range for this role?"

It declines without refusing, explains truthfully, names your expectation and asks for theirs.

**If they insist**, in order of preference:

1. "I'm not comfortable sharing that, but I'm happy to be completely transparent about my expectation, which is X to Y."
2. If a form requires a number, enter your **expected** salary and clarify in the first call that it was your expectation.
3. If they absolutely require it, give it — then immediately reframe: "That reflects a small company rather than the market for this role; my expectation is X to Y."

### Level 2 — Research, then three numbers

Sources, most reliable first:

- **Internal recruiters**: ask for the band in the first call — many tell you ({{ch:referrals-and-recruiters}}).
- **People inside the company**: "roughly what should someone expect at this level?"
- **Your own interviews**: numbers quoted to you in other processes.
- **Salary sites and surveys**: useful for the general shape; data for some markets and levels is thin or old.

Then write three numbers **before** any offer conversation:

| Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Walk-away** | Below this, you decline and keep looking (unless the opportunity is exceptional for learning) |
| **Target** | What you realistically expect to agree |
| **Ambitious** | What you ask for first — justifiable, not absurd |

Writing them down prevents decisions made in the excitement of the moment.

## The conversation

### Level 2 — When they make the offer

**Never accept in the conversation where the number is first named.**

> "Thank you — I'm really pleased, and very interested in the role. Could I take a day to look at the whole package properly and come back to you tomorrow?"

This is normal and professional; it gives you time to read the letter and prepare a calm counter.

### Level 3 — The counter

The next day, by call or in writing: **appreciation, a specific number, a reason, then silence**.

> "Thank you again for the offer — I'm excited about the team. I was hoping we could get to [ambitious number]. I'm bringing three years of production experience and two deployed projects that cover the database and back-end work this role focuses on, and that figure is in line with what I've seen for similar roles."

Then **stop talking**. The silence after naming a number is uncomfortable — and it is where negotiations are won; people who fill it often argue against themselves. Expect a response somewhere between their offer and your number; that is a successful negotiation. **Counter once**, then accept graciously or decline; repeated rounds cost goodwill with your future manager.

### Level 3 — What else is negotiable

When the base salary is fixed by bands, these are often flexible:

- **Job title or level** — costs the company little and changes what you can apply for next.
- **Joining bonus** — often from a different budget.
- **Notice-period buyout** — if they want you to start sooner.
- **A written salary review** after six months with a stated figure — in the offer letter, not a verbal promise.
- **Leave, remote or hybrid days, equipment, training or certification budgets.**
- **Start date.**

> "I understand the band is fixed. Is there flexibility on the level, or a written review at six months?"

## The offer letter

### Level 3 — Read every line

- **Gross versus net**: offers are often quoted gross; calculate your net (after tax) before comparing.
- **Structure**: basic salary plus allowances. Some benefits (provident fund, gratuity, bonuses) are calculated on the **basic** part only, so two offers with the same total can differ in value.
- **Provident fund and gratuity**: whether the company contributes and at what rate — real compensation.
- **Bonuses**: guaranteed or discretionary? "Up to two months" that is fully discretionary is not two months.
- **Health insurance** and coverage for family.
- **Your notice period** in the new contract — it constrains your next move.
- **Bonds or training agreements**: length and penalty — a real cost to include when comparing ({{ch:the-job-market}}).
- **Probation**: length and notice terms during it.
- **Working model**: office days — contractual or just current practice?
- **Equity** (at some product companies and start-ups): type, vesting schedule, and the realistic chance it is ever worth money.

If anything is unclear, ask before signing; professionals ask about contracts.

## Harder situations

### Level 4 — Multiple offers

Several offers at once is the strongest position — another reason to keep applying until you sign ({{ch:applying}}). Be straightforward:

> "I want to be transparent — I have another offer at [amount] that I need to answer by Friday. Your team is my first choice. Is there room to close the gap?"

If a company you prefer is behind in its process: "I have an offer with a Friday deadline. I'd rather join you — is there any way to speed up your process?" Many will try.

**Never invent an offer.** Some companies ask to see it; being caught ends the process permanently.

### Level 4 — When the offer is below your walk-away

Sometimes accepting is still right — if it is a large improvement on your current situation, the engineering environment is genuinely strong (code review, senior engineers, modern practice) and there are no other realistic options. A year in a strong environment can be worth more than the money. Decline if the environment looks like what you are leaving, the bond is long, or other processes are close.

### Level 4 — Declining well

You may want this company later:

> "Thank you very much for the offer and for everyone's time. I've decided to go in a different direction, but I really valued the conversations and would be glad to stay in touch."

No long justification, no criticism.

### Level 4 — After accepting

- Get the **final offer in writing** before resigning from your current job.
- **Resign professionally**, serve your notice properly, and hand over well — people remember how you leave.
- Be careful with **counter-offers** from your current employer: the reasons you wanted to leave rarely disappear because the salary changes.

## From beginner to enterprise

**Level 1–2 — the basics.** Not anchoring on your current salary; research; three numbers; never accepting on the spot.

**Level 3 — negotiating.** A reasoned counter followed by silence; negotiating title, bonuses, reviews and benefits; reading the offer letter fully.

**Level 4 — complex situations.** Multiple offers, below-target offers, declining well, resigning professionally, handling counter-offers.

**Level 5 — senior negotiations.** Equity, levels, scope and team; negotiating role design as well as pay.

**Level 6 — the other side.** Making fair offers as a hiring manager; consistent bands; transparent processes.

## Real-world use cases

### The day that was worth it

- **Story**: offered a salary above their current pay, an engineer was tempted to accept immediately. Instead they asked for a day, checked that the offer was near the bottom of the band the recruiter had mentioned, and countered once with reasons. The company met them halfway and added a written six-month review.

### The basic-salary trap

- **Story**: two offers had the same total, but one had a much higher basic salary. Because provident fund contributions and gratuity were calculated on basic salary, the first offer was worth noticeably more over a few years.

## Common mistakes

### Beginner: naming the current salary first

**Fix:** redirect to your expectation.

### Beginner: accepting on the spot

**Fix:** ask for a day.

### Beginner: no numbers prepared

**Fix:** walk-away, target and ambitious, written down.

### Intermediate: filling the silence after your counter

**Fix:** say your number and reason, then wait.

### Intermediate: negotiating only base salary

**Fix:** consider title, bonuses, reviews and benefits.

### Intermediate: not reading the letter

**Fix:** check net pay, structure, benefits, bonuses, bonds and notice.

### Process: inventing competing offers

**Fix:** use only real offers.

## Debugging and troubleshooting

- **"That's above our range"**: "I understand — what is the range for this level? And is there a level where my number fits?" You may have been placed too low.
- **"Why do you think you're worth that when you earn much less now?"**: answer on value — the skills and evidence you bring and the market for this role — not your history.
- **Pressure to decide immediately**: a reasonable company allows a day or two; extreme pressure is a warning sign.
- **Verbal promises**: ask for them in writing.

## Security summary

- **Share payslips, ID and bank details** only with verified employers, after a real offer, through official channels.
- **Fake offers** asking for fees, equipment purchases or "processing charges" are scams.
- **Keep copies** of offer letters and contracts.

## How a senior engineer thinks about this

- **Preparation removes emotion.** Numbers decided in advance protect you.
- **One calm, reasoned counter is professional.**
- **Total value, not one number.** Structure, benefits, conditions and learning.
- **Relationships last longer than negotiations.** Firm on terms, warm in tone.

### Exercise 3 — Compare two offers

**Objective:** practise evaluating offers.
**The offers:** A — higher total salary, basic 50% of total, discretionary bonus "up to two months", two-year bond, five office days. B — slightly lower total, basic 70%, provident fund matched on basic, guaranteed one-month bonus, two remote days, no bond, strong code review culture. Which is better?

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

Compare **net** pay first, then add value: B's higher basic increases provident fund and gratuity; its bonus is guaranteed while A's may be zero; A's bond is a real cost and limits your options; B's remote days and engineering culture have real value for life and learning. Unless A's net salary is substantially higher, **B is likely better** — and you could ask A whether the bond is negotiable or B whether there is flexibility on base, using the other offer honestly.

</details>

## Summary

Negotiation decides your pay for years and is expected by employers. Avoid **anchoring** on your current salary: redirect to your researched expectation and ask for the range; never lie. Research from recruiters, insiders, your own interviews and surveys, and write a **walk-away**, **target** and **ambitious** number before any conversation.

When the offer comes, **never accept on the spot** — ask for a day. Counter **once**: appreciation, a specific number, a reason, then **silence**; expect a result between the two numbers and accept graciously. When base pay is fixed, negotiate **title, joining bonus, a written review, leave, remote days, budgets and start date**. Read the letter fully: **gross and net**, **basic and allowances**, **provident fund and gratuity**, bonus conditions, notice periods, **bonds**, probation, working model and equity. Use real competing offers transparently and never invent one; accept a lower offer when the learning environment is worth it; decline warmly; get everything in writing before resigning; and leave your current job professionally.

## Key terms

- **Salary negotiation** — Agreeing employment terms after an offer is made.
- **Anchoring** — The effect of the first number on the final agreement.
- **Salary band** — The pay range for a role and level.
- **Walk-away number** — The lowest acceptable offer.
- **Counter-offer** — A response proposing different terms.
- **Total compensation** — The full value of pay and benefits.
- **Gross / net salary** — Pay before / after tax and deductions.
- **Basic salary** — The core salary component on which some benefits are calculated.
- **Allowance** — An additional pay component such as house rent or medical.
- **Provident fund** — A retirement savings fund with employer contributions.
- **Gratuity** — An end-of-service benefit based on salary and years worked.
- **Bond** — A contractual commitment to stay, with a penalty for leaving early.
- **Probation period** — An initial period of employment with different terms.
- **Vesting** — Earning ownership of equity over time.
