# Phase 10 — Chapter 3: IELTS Writing — Task 1 and Task 2

---

## Chapter Overview

Writing is the lowest-scoring section for the majority of IELTS candidates worldwide, and it is where most people's overall band gets capped. It is also the most *mechanical* section: unlike Speaking, nothing happens in real time, and unlike Reading, the marking rubric is public. If you know exactly what the four criteria reward and you have practised producing it under a clock, Writing becomes an engineering problem — a specification, a fixed time budget, and a checklist.

Two structural facts drive everything in this chapter:

1. **Task 2 is worth twice as much as Task 1.** The Writing band is roughly one-third Task 1 and two-thirds Task 2. Spending 30 minutes perfecting a chart description and then rushing the essay is the single most expensive mistake in the section.
2. **Task 1 has one paragraph that is worth more than all the others.** Omitting the overview caps Task Achievement at Band 5. One sentence beginning "Overall," is the cheapest half-band in the entire exam.

**Topics:**
- The four Writing criteria, and what Band 6 / 7 / 8 look like on paper
- Academic Task 1: every chart type, with model answers and data-selection rules
- General Training Task 1: formal, semi-formal, and informal letters, with models
- Task 2: all five essay types, with full model essays and paragraph anatomy
- Planning in five minutes; managing 150 and 250 words without counting
- Cohesion, referencing, and paragraph development
- The 20-point self-check that catches most band-losing errors
- Technology and engineering topics, which recur constantly

## Beginner Theory

### The Task Specification

```
TASK 1                                      TASK 2
  Time    20 minutes (recommended)            Time    40 minutes (recommended)
  Length  150 words minimum                   Length  250 words minimum
  Weight  ~1/3 of the Writing band            Weight  ~2/3 of the Writing band
  Register  Formal, impersonal                Register  Formal, semi-personal
  Academic  Describe visual data              Both      Discursive essay
  GT        Write a letter

Both tasks are marked on FOUR criteria, each worth 25% of that task:
  Task Achievement (T1) / Task Response (T2)
  Coherence and Cohesion
  Lexical Resource
  Grammatical Range and Accuracy
```

There is no penalty for writing more than the minimum, but there is a penalty for writing less, and quality degrades past about 220 words (Task 1) and 340 words (Task 2) because you run out of checking time. Target **170–200 words for Task 1** and **280–320 words for Task 2**.

### What the Four Criteria Actually Reward

```
TASK ACHIEVEMENT (Task 1)
  Academic: Did you select and report the KEY features? Is there an overview?
            Are the figures accurate? Is it objective (no causes, no opinions)?
  GT:       Did you cover all three bullet points? Is the tone right for the
            recipient? Is the purpose clear from the first line?

TASK RESPONSE (Task 2)
  Did you answer the EXACT question asked, all parts of it?
  Is your position clear, and the same position, from start to finish?
  Are the main ideas developed — reason, explanation, example, consequence?
  Is anything off-topic, unsupported, or merely asserted?

COHERENCE AND COHESION
  Is there a logical progression from paragraph to paragraph?
  Does each paragraph have one central topic, announced in a topic sentence?
  Are cohesive devices varied and natural rather than mechanical?
  Is referencing (this, these, such, the latter) used to avoid repetition?

LEXICAL RESOURCE
  Range: enough vocabulary for flexibility and precision on this topic
  Collocation: words that actually go together in English
  Word formation: right form of the right word
  Spelling: errors are counted here
  Repetition of key nouns is the classic Band 6 signal

GRAMMATICAL RANGE AND ACCURACY
  Range: complex sentences — subordination, relative clauses, conditionals,
         passives, participle clauses, noun clauses
  Accuracy: what proportion of sentences are error-free
  Punctuation: comma splices and missing full stops are counted here
```

### Band 6 vs 7 vs 8 on Paper

| Criterion | Band 6 | Band 7 | Band 8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task Response | Position present but unclear or repetitive; some ideas inadequately developed or irrelevant | Clear position throughout; main ideas extended and supported, though some may be over-generalised | All parts covered sufficiently; well-developed response with relevant, extended, well-supported ideas |
| Coherence | Information arranged coherently overall; cohesive devices used but mechanically or inaccurately; paragraphing may be inadequate | Logical progression throughout; range of cohesive devices with some under/over-use; clear central topic per paragraph | Sequenced logically; all aspects of cohesion well managed; paragraphing used sufficiently and appropriately |
| Lexical | Adequate range; attempts less common vocabulary with some inaccuracy; noticeable spelling/word-formation errors that don't impede | Sufficient range for flexibility and precision; some less common items with occasional inaccuracy; few errors | Wide range used fluently and precisely; occasional inaccuracy in word choice/collocation only |
| Grammar | Mix of simple and complex forms; errors in grammar and punctuation occur but rarely reduce communication | Variety of complex structures; **frequent error-free sentences**; good control with a few errors | Wide range; majority of sentences error-free; occasional non-systematic slips |

**Read the Band 7 grammar cell again: "frequent error-free sentences."** For most candidates stuck at 6.5, this is the binding constraint. Not vocabulary. Not ideas. Error density. Chapter 5 of this phase exists for exactly this reason.

### Mental Model: You Are Producing Evidence for a Checklist

An examiner spends about 10 minutes on both of your tasks combined. They are not reading for pleasure or for insight; they are scanning for evidence against four criteria. This tells you how to write:

```
Make the evidence findable:
  Overview paragraph starts with "Overall," → Task Achievement box ticked in 1 second
  Thesis is the last sentence of paragraph 1 → Task Response found immediately
  Topic sentence opens each body paragraph → Coherence visible at a glance
  One clear example per body paragraph → "supported ideas" evidenced
  Deliberate complex structures → Grammatical Range evidenced

Do NOT bury your best work. A brilliant thesis in the middle of paragraph 3
scores lower than an adequate thesis at the end of paragraph 1, because the
examiner is looking for it in the standard place.
```

This is why templates help *structurally* and hurt *lexically*. Use a fixed architecture; write the actual sentences fresh.

## Academic Task 1 — Describing Visual Data

### The Seven Task Types

```
LINE GRAPH        change over time                    → trends, comparisons
BAR CHART         comparison across categories        → highest/lowest, groups
                  (or over time, if years on the x-axis)
PIE CHART(S)      proportions of a whole              → shares, dominance, shifts
TABLE             exact figures, many categories      → select! never list everything
PROCESS DIAGRAM   how something is made or works      → stages, passive, sequencing
MAP               changes to a place over time        → location language, passives
MIXED             two charts together                 → describe each, then link
```

### Universal Four-Paragraph Structure

```
PARAGRAPH 1 — INTRODUCTION (1 sentence, sometimes 2)
  Paraphrase the question: what is shown, where, when, in what units.
  NEVER copy the question wording — copied text is excluded from your word count.

PARAGRAPH 2 — OVERVIEW (2 sentences)
  The 2 biggest-picture facts. NO specific numbers here.
  Begin with "Overall," or "It is clear that" — make it unmissable.
  This paragraph is the difference between Band 5 and Band 7 on Task Achievement.

PARAGRAPH 3 — DETAIL 1 (3–4 sentences, with figures)
  The most significant group / trend / category, with supporting data.

PARAGRAPH 4 — DETAIL 2 (3–4 sentences, with figures)
  The remaining significant features, with data and comparisons.
```

**What goes in the overview vs the body:**

```
OVERVIEW  (no numbers)                      BODY  (numbers)
  the general direction of all series         exact values at start and end
  which is highest / lowest overall           the size of each change
  the biggest change overall                  specific years and turning points
  whether a gap widened or narrowed           comparisons with figures
  the most striking single fact                peaks, troughs, crossover points
```

### Data Selection — The Skill Nobody Teaches

Task Achievement rewards **selecting** key features, not transcribing everything. A table with 24 cells does not want 24 sentences.

```
Select and report:
  ✓ the highest and lowest values
  ✓ the biggest change (rise or fall)
  ✓ any crossover point (where one line overtakes another)
  ✓ anything anomalous (a series that behaves differently from the rest)
  ✓ start and end values for each series
  ✓ groupings (which categories behave similarly — describe them together)

Ignore:
  ✗ every intermediate year
  ✗ tiny fluctuations
  ✗ causes ("this happened because the economy grew") — you cannot know
  ✗ your opinion ("this is a worrying trend") — Task 1 is objective
  ✗ predictions beyond the data
```

**Grouping is the highest-value technique.** If four of six countries rose and two fell, describe them as two groups rather than six separate sentences. That is what "selecting key features" means, and it also produces the comparative grammar the rubric rewards.

### Tense and Language Rules

```
TENSE
  Past data (years given, in the past)      → past simple
  Present/ongoing data (no dates, "now")    → present simple
  Future projections (years ahead)           → will / is projected to / is expected to
  Process diagrams                           → present simple PASSIVE
  Maps                                       → past simple passive (for the changes)

NEVER
  ✗ "I think", "in my opinion", "we can see that I believe"
  ✗ "the graph gives information about the fact that..."  (wordy)
  ✗ Causal claims: "usage rose because the internet became cheaper"
  ✓ Neutral correlation only: "the rise coincided with..."
```

### The Trend Language Bank

```
UPWARD                  DOWNWARD                FLAT / VOLATILE
verb   rise             fall                    remain stable / steady
       increase         decrease                stay constant
       grow             decline                 level off / plateau
       climb            drop                    hold steady
       surge  (sharp)   plummet  (sharp)        fluctuate
       soar   (sharp)   slump    (sharp)        oscillate
       peak (at)        dip / bottom out        show little change

noun   a rise           a fall                  stability
       an increase      a decrease              a plateau
       growth           a decline               fluctuation
       a surge          a slump                 little variation
       an upturn        a downturn

ADVERBS (with verbs)          ADJECTIVES (with nouns)
  dramatically                  a dramatic rise
  sharply                       a sharp fall
  steeply                       a steep increase
  significantly                 a significant decline
  considerably                  a considerable drop
  steadily                      a steady increase
  gradually                     a gradual rise
  slightly / marginally         a slight / marginal change

PREPOSITIONS — memorise these; they are a frequent error source
  rise   FROM 20%  TO 45%          (start and end)
  rise   BY 25 percentage points   (the size of the change)
  stand / stood  AT 45%            (a value at a point)
  peak   AT 68%  IN 2015           (a maximum, and when)
  a rise OF 25%  IN sales          (noun form)
  an increase IN the number OF users
  double / triple / halve          (no preposition: "figures doubled")

COMPARISON
  three times as high as · twice the figure for · slightly higher than
  the highest / the second highest · by far the largest · marginally below
  compared with / in contrast to / whereas / while
  X outstripped Y · X overtook Y in 2012 · the gap between X and Y widened
```

### Model Answer 1 — Line Graph

> **Task:** The line graph below shows the percentage of the population using the internet in three countries between 2000 and 2020.

| Year | UK | China | India |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 40% | 2% | 1% |
| 2005 | 70% | 9% | 2% |
| 2010 | 85% | 34% | 7% |
| 2015 | 92% | 50% | 15% |
| 2020 | 95% | 70% | 43% |

```
INTRODUCTION
  The line graph compares the proportion of people who had internet access in
  the United Kingdom, China and India over a twenty-year period beginning in 2000.

OVERVIEW
  Overall, internet use expanded substantially in all three countries, although
  the pace of that expansion differed markedly. The UK led throughout, but the
  gap between it and the two Asian countries narrowed considerably by the end
  of the period, with China growing fastest in absolute terms.

DETAIL 1
  In 2000, the UK was already well ahead, with 40% of its population online,
  compared with figures of just 2% for China and 1% for India. British usage
  then climbed steadily, reaching 85% in 2010 before levelling off at around
  95% by 2020 — a point at which further growth was clearly limited by
  saturation.

DETAIL 2
  China's trajectory was far steeper. From a negligible 2% in 2000, the figure
  rose to 34% in 2010 and 70% in 2020, an overall increase of 68 percentage
  points. India followed a similar shape but lagged roughly a decade behind:
  usage remained below 10% until 2010, after which it accelerated sharply to
  reach 43% by the end of the period. Despite this late surge, India still had
  by far the lowest level of internet penetration of the three countries.

                                                              [~185 words]
```

*Why this scores well:* the overview contains no figures but states both the shared trend and the differences; the body groups and compares rather than listing; prepositions are precise ("rose to 34%", "an increase of 68 percentage points"); complex structures appear naturally ("a point at which further growth was clearly limited", "after which it accelerated"); and it is objective throughout — no causes are invented.

### Model Answer 2 — Bar Chart with Categories

> **Task:** The chart shows the average hours per week spent on four activities by men and women in one country in 2022.

| Activity | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Paid work | 39 | 27 |
| Housework | 8 | 17 |
| Childcare | 5 | 12 |
| Leisure | 26 | 21 |

```
The bar chart compares how many hours per week men and women in one country
devoted to four different activities in 2022.

Overall, men spent considerably longer than women in paid employment and in
leisure, whereas women carried out substantially more unpaid domestic work
and childcare. The largest single gap between the sexes occurred in paid work,
and the total workload, paid and unpaid combined, was broadly similar for both.

Men worked 39 hours a week for pay, twelve hours more than the figure of 27
recorded for women. They also enjoyed around five hours more leisure time,
at 26 hours compared with 21. In both cases the difference was substantial
but not extreme.

The pattern was reversed for unpaid work. Women spent 17 hours on housework,
more than double the male figure of 8, and 12 hours on childcare against
just 5 for men. Taken together, women therefore performed 29 hours of unpaid
work each week, almost exactly twice the male total of 13 hours, which
largely offsets their shorter working week.

                                                              [~175 words]
```

*Note the final sentence:* combining categories to produce a derived comparison ("29 hours… twice the male total") is exactly the "selecting and comparing key features" that separates Band 7 from Band 6. It is analysis of the data, not an opinion about it.

### Model Answer 3 — Process Diagram

> **Task:** The diagram shows how plastic bottles are recycled.

```
The diagram illustrates the stages involved in recycling used plastic bottles
into new products.

Overall, the process is cyclical and comprises eight main stages, beginning
with the collection of discarded bottles and ending with the manufacture of
new items. Human intervention is required mainly at the start, while the
middle stages are largely mechanised.

At the first stage, used bottles are deposited by consumers in dedicated
recycling bins, from which they are collected by lorry and transported to a
processing facility. Here the bottles are sorted, either manually or by
automated scanners, so that non-recyclable material can be removed. The
remaining plastic is then washed to eliminate labels, adhesive and residue.

Once cleaned, the bottles are fed into a machine which shreds them into small
flakes. These flakes are subsequently melted at high temperature and extruded
into uniform pellets. In the final stage, the pellets are sold to
manufacturers, where they are moulded into new products such as containers,
textiles and packaging, at which point the cycle begins again.

                                                              [~170 words]
```

*Process language to reuse:* `comprises N stages` · `begins with … and ends with` · `at the first stage` · `from which` · `once cleaned` · `is then fed into` · `subsequently` · `at which point`. Note that almost every verb is a **present simple passive** — that is the defining grammar of process tasks, and using it consistently is easy Grammatical Range evidence.

### Model Answer 4 — Map

> **Task:** The maps show a coastal village in 1990 and the same village today.

```
The two maps compare the layout of a small coastal village called Denham as
it was in 1990 with its appearance at the present day.

Overall, the village has been transformed from a modest agricultural
settlement into a tourist destination. While the road network and the church
have been retained, most of the farmland has been built on, and several
facilities aimed at visitors have been added along the shoreline.

In 1990, the village consisted of a small cluster of houses in the north-west,
a church on the main road, and a single shop. The eastern half of the area was
entirely occupied by farmland, and the beach along the southern coast was
undeveloped and reached only by a footpath.

Substantial changes have since taken place. The farmland to the east has been
cleared and replaced by a large hotel complex, together with a car park
immediately to its west. The footpath leading to the beach has been widened
into a road, and a restaurant has been constructed at its southern end,
directly overlooking the sea. The original housing has expanded slightly
northwards, although the church remains untouched in its original position.

                                                              [~190 words]
```

*Map language to reuse:* `has been transformed into` · `has been retained` · `built on` · `cleared and replaced by` · `widened into` · `to the east of` · `immediately to its west` · `overlooking` · `remains untouched`. Maps are almost entirely **present perfect passive** (for 1990 → now) plus location prepositions.

### Task 1 Fifteen-Minute Writing Protocol

```
Minutes 0–2   READ AND DECODE
  What are the units? (%, thousands, hours) What are the dates?
  How many series/categories? Is it change-over-time or comparison?
  Circle: highest, lowest, biggest change, any crossover.

Minutes 2–4   PLAN
  Write your two overview facts as bullet points, in note form.
  Decide your grouping: which categories go in paragraph 3, which in 4?

Minutes 4–17  WRITE
  Introduction (1 sentence) → Overview (2 sentences) →
  Detail 1 (3–4 sentences) → Detail 2 (3–4 sentences)

Minutes 17–20 CHECK
  Is there an overview beginning "Overall,"?          ← non-negotiable
  Are all numbers copied correctly?
  Tense consistent? Prepositions right (from/to/by/at)?
  Any opinion or cause that should be deleted?
  At least 150 words?
```

## General Training Task 1 — Letters

If you are taking General Training (Canada Express Entry, most work visas), your Task 1 is a letter, not a chart.

### The Three Registers

```
FORMAL — to someone you don't know, by role
  Dear Sir or Madam,  →  Yours faithfully,
  Recipient: a manager, a company, a council, an airline
  No contractions. No idiom. No exclamation marks.
  Purpose stated in sentence 1: "I am writing to complain about..."

SEMI-FORMAL — to someone you know in a professional capacity
  Dear Mr Ahmed,  →  Yours sincerely,
  Recipient: your landlord, your manager by name, a course tutor
  Polite, direct; contractions still best avoided.

INFORMAL — to a friend or family member
  Dear Sana,  /  Hi Sana,   →   Best wishes, / All the best, / Take care,
  Contractions expected. Questions, exclamations, and idiom are appropriate.
  Open with a personal line: "It's been ages since we last spoke!"
```

**How to identify the register:** if the prompt gives you a name, it is at least semi-formal. If it says "a friend", it is informal. If it says "the manager" with no name, it is formal.

### Universal Letter Structure

```
Dear [appropriate greeting],

PARAGRAPH 1 — Purpose. One or two sentences. State why you are writing.
PARAGRAPH 2 — Bullet point 1, developed with detail.
PARAGRAPH 3 — Bullet point 2, developed with detail.
PARAGRAPH 4 — Bullet point 3, developed with detail.
PARAGRAPH 5 — Closing line: the action you want, or a warm sign-off.

[Appropriate closing],
[First name only for informal; full name for formal]

All three bullet points MUST be covered. Missing one caps Task Achievement.
```

### Model Letter 1 — Formal Complaint

> **Task:** You recently bought a laptop online which arrived damaged. Write to the company. Say what you bought and when, describe the problem, and say what you want them to do.

```
Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to report a problem with a laptop I purchased from your online
store on 14 March, order number KX-48211, and to request a replacement.

The item was advertised as new and was described as being dispatched in
sealed manufacturer's packaging. When the parcel arrived four days later,
however, the outer box was crushed at one corner and the internal packaging
had clearly been opened previously. On removing the laptop, I found a
noticeable crack running along the lower edge of the screen casing, and one
of the hinges does not close correctly.

I contacted your customer service line on 19 March and was assured that a
member of your returns team would respond within 48 hours. Ten days have now
passed and I have received no communication whatsoever, despite sending two
further emails to the address I was given.

Given that the machine was faulty on arrival and that I have made repeated
attempts to resolve this through the usual channels, I would like a
replacement unit dispatched at your expense, along with confirmation of the
date on which I can expect it. If a replacement is not available, I would
ask for a full refund instead.

I look forward to your prompt reply.

Yours faithfully,
Ammar Malik
                                                              [~200 words]
```

*Formal letter phrase bank:* `I am writing to report / request / enquire about / complain about` · `I would be grateful if you could` · `Given that…, I would like` · `despite repeated attempts` · `I look forward to your prompt reply` · `Yours faithfully`.

### Model Letter 2 — Semi-Formal Request

> **Task:** You want to take two weeks of unpaid leave to attend a training course. Write to your manager. Explain what the course is, why it is useful, and how your work will be covered.

```
Dear Mr Khan,

I am writing to ask whether it would be possible for me to take two weeks of
unpaid leave in September, from the 8th to the 19th, in order to attend a
professional training course.

The course is an intensive cloud architecture programme run by a certified
AWS training partner in Dubai. It covers the design of secure, scalable
systems and concludes with the Solutions Architect examination. As you know,
the platform migration we have planned for next year will depend heavily on
exactly these skills, and at present we rely on external consultants for
that expertise.

Regarding cover, I have discussed the period with Sara and she has agreed to
take over the deployment schedule and the on-call rota in my absence. I would
complete the reporting module before I leave, so that nothing time-critical
remains outstanding, and I would of course remain reachable by email for
anything urgent.

I appreciate that September is a busy month, and I am happy to discuss
alternative dates if this period is difficult. Please let me know if you
would like to talk it through in person.

Yours sincerely,
Ammar Malik
                                                              [~195 words]
```

### Model Letter 3 — Informal

> **Task:** A friend is thinking of moving to your city for work. Write to them. Say what you like about living there, warn them about one difficulty, and offer to help.

```
Dear Sana,

It was so good to hear from you last week — and what exciting news! I had no
idea you were even thinking about moving here, so of course I'm going to try
to talk you into it.

Honestly, the best thing about this city is how alive it feels. There's
always something on, the food is ridiculous in the best possible way, and
you're never more than ten minutes from someone selling excellent tea at
midnight. Work-wise it's genuinely booming too; half the people I know have
changed jobs in the last two years without ever leaving the city.

I should warn you about one thing, though: the traffic is every bit as bad as
people say, and the winter air quality can be rough if you're at all
sensitive to it. If you do come, pick somewhere close to your office — people
who commute across town end up regretting it within a month.

Anyway, you know you're welcome to stay with us while you look for a place,
for as long as you need. Send me the areas you're considering and I'll tell
you honestly what they're like.

Can't wait to have you here!

All the best,
Ammar
                                                              [~205 words]
```

*Note what makes this informal without becoming sloppy:* contractions, direct address, humour, an exclamation, phrasal verbs (*talk you into it*, *end up regretting*), and a warm close — but the grammar is still fully controlled, which is what is actually scored.

## Task 2 — The Essay

### The Five Question Types

Correctly identifying the type is the first mark you earn or lose, because each type has a different required response.

```
1. OPINION (agree/disagree)
   "...To what extent do you agree or disagree?"
   "Do you agree or disagree?"
   → Requires: ONE clear position, sustained throughout.

2. DISCUSSION (both views)
   "Discuss both these views and give your own opinion."
   → Requires: BOTH views presented fairly, PLUS your own position.
     Omitting your opinion is the classic failure here.

3. ADVANTAGES / DISADVANTAGES
   "What are the advantages and disadvantages of...?"
   "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"
   → Note the difference! The second version REQUIRES a verdict.

4. PROBLEM / SOLUTION (or cause/solution)
   "What problems does this cause and what solutions can you suggest?"
   "Why is this happening and what can be done about it?"
   → Requires: BOTH halves, balanced. Two problems, two matching solutions.

5. TWO-PART / DIRECT QUESTION
   "Why do people do X? Is this a positive or negative development?"
   → Requires: an answer to EACH question, usually one body paragraph each.
     Answering only the first half is a Band 5 Task Response, however good
     the English.
```

### Universal Four-Paragraph Architecture

```
PARAGRAPH 1 — INTRODUCTION (40–50 words, 2–3 sentences)
  Sentence 1: paraphrase the topic — set the context, no memorised filler
  Sentence 2: [optional] narrow the issue or note the debate
  Sentence 3: THESIS — your position / your roadmap. Always the last sentence.

PARAGRAPH 2 — BODY 1 (90–110 words)
  Topic sentence (the ONE idea of this paragraph)
  Explanation (why is that true? what's the mechanism?)
  Example (specific, concrete, plausible)
  Consequence / link back to the question

PARAGRAPH 3 — BODY 2 (90–110 words)
  Same anatomy, second idea — or the counterargument plus your rebuttal

PARAGRAPH 4 — CONCLUSION (35–45 words, 2 sentences)
  Restate the thesis in genuinely different words
  Add a final implication or recommendation — no new arguments
```

Two body paragraphs, developed deeply, beat three shallow ones every time. Depth is what "extended and supported" means in the Band 7 descriptor.

### Paragraph Anatomy — Worked in Detail

This is the most important pattern in the chapter. Learn it as four moves.

```
TOPIC        "The most compelling argument for remote work is economic."
             ↑ one idea, no hedging, announces the paragraph

EXPLAIN      "Employers who no longer need to house every employee can
             reduce their largest fixed cost, while employees avoid both the
             expense and the unpaid hours of commuting."
             ↑ the MECHANISM — why is the topic sentence true?

EXAMPLE      "A mid-sized software company in Karachi, for instance, might
             spend as much on office space as on the salaries of five
             engineers; redirecting even half of that towards hiring is a
             material change to what the business can build."
             ↑ specific, concrete, plausible. "for instance" signals it.

CONSEQUENCE  "The effect, therefore, is not simply convenience but a genuine
             reallocation of resources towards productive work."
             ↑ links the idea explicitly back to the question
```

**On examples:** they do not need to be true, verifiable, or statistical. They need to be *specific*. "Studies show that 78% of people prefer remote work" is a worse example than the paragraph above, because invented statistics are transparent and add nothing. Concrete hypothetical cases, drawn from your own field, are ideal — and as a software engineer you have an unusually rich supply of them.

### Introductions Without Clichés

```
✗ NEVER (examiners see these hundreds of times and discount them entirely)
  "In this modern era of rapid globalisation and technological advancement..."
  "Since the dawn of civilisation, humans have always..."
  "It is a controversial issue which has attracted much attention nowadays."
  "This essay will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of both sides."

✓ INSTEAD — paraphrase the actual topic in one specific sentence

  Question: "Some people believe that governments should invest in public
             transport rather than building new roads. To what extent do you
             agree?"

  Introduction:
  "As urban populations grow, most governments face a choice about where to
  spend limited transport budgets: on expanding road capacity, or on buses,
  trams and rail. While new roads bring short-term relief, I would argue
  that public transport represents the far better long-term investment,
  primarily because it addresses the cause of congestion rather than its
  symptoms."
                                                       [56 words, 2 sentences]

  Notice: the topic is paraphrased with real content, the position is
  explicit ("I would argue that"), and a reason is previewed ("primarily
  because"), which gives the whole essay direction.
```

### Model Essay 1 — Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

> **Task:** Some people believe that technology is making human beings less capable of thinking for themselves. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

```
Search engines answer questions in milliseconds and AI assistants now draft
text that once required real effort, so it is unsurprising that people worry
about the effect of these tools on our mental capacities. While I accept that
technology can encourage intellectual passivity in certain situations, I
disagree with the broader claim, because in most professional contexts these
tools remove routine cognitive work and free attention for harder thinking.

There is undeniably a version of this concern that holds up. When a tool
supplies an answer instantly, there is little incentive to reason towards it,
and the underlying skill can atrophy through disuse. Navigation is the
clearest case: drivers who follow a satellite navigation system for years
often report that they no longer build any mental map of their own city, and
become genuinely lost when the device fails. Where a technology substitutes
for a skill entirely, rather than supporting it, some loss of independent
capability seems inevitable.

The professional reality, however, is generally the opposite. In software
engineering, for example, no competent developer memorises the exact syntax
of every library, because that information is a keystroke away; what the
profession actually rewards is judgement about how a system should be
structured, which risks matter, and what should be built at all. Freed from
recall, engineers spend their attention on precisely the abstract,
consequential problems that most demand independent thought. The same
pattern holds in medicine and law, where reference tools have not replaced
diagnostic or interpretive reasoning but supported it.

On balance, then, the danger lies not in technology itself but in how it is
used: as a substitute for thinking it is corrosive, and as a support for
thinking it is transformative. The reasonable response is to teach people
which of the two they are doing, rather than to assume that the tools
themselves make us less capable.

                                                              [~305 words]
```

*What earns Band 8 here:* the position is stated in paragraph 1 and never wavers; the concession paragraph is genuinely argued rather than dismissed, then bounded ("Where a technology substitutes… rather than supporting it"); examples are concrete and from different domains; cohesion is achieved through meaning and referencing rather than a chain of "Firstly / Secondly / Moreover"; and the conclusion reframes the question ("not technology itself but how it is used") instead of merely repeating it.

### Model Essay 2 — Discussion (Both Views + Opinion)

> **Task:** Some people think children should start formal schooling at a very early age. Others believe they should begin at seven or older. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

```
The age at which formal education should begin varies widely between
countries, from four in the United Kingdom to seven in much of Scandinavia,
and each system is defended with considerable conviction. Having weighed both
positions, I believe a later start is preferable for most children, although
the quality of what precedes school matters more than the starting age itself.

Those who favour an early start point to the pace at which young children
acquire skills. Literacy and numeracy build cumulatively, so a child who
begins reading at four has three additional years of practice by the age of
seven, and early exposure to structured routines is also argued to help
children from disadvantaged homes, who may otherwise arrive at school already
behind their peers. On this view, delaying formal instruction wastes the years
in which the brain is most receptive.

The opposing case rests on a distinction between learning and schooling.
Advocates of a later start argue that play is not a lesser activity but the
mechanism by which young children develop language, self-regulation and
social competence, and that formal instruction imposed too early crowds it
out. The Finnish system is the standard illustration: children there begin
school at seven and consistently perform at or near the top of international
assessments, which suggests that the early years are better spent on
foundations that are not easily measured.

My own view aligns with the second position, though with a qualification. The
evidence that a very early start produces lasting academic advantage is weak,
while the risk of associating school with pressure and failure at the age of
four is real. What matters, however, is that the preceding years are rich in
language, play and attention; a later start combined with neglect would be
worse than either alternative. The starting age is therefore a secondary
question, and the quality of early childhood provision is the primary one.

                                                              [~310 words]
```

*Note the structure choice:* view 1, view 2, then a dedicated opinion paragraph woven into the conclusion. This is safe and clear. The alternative — declaring your position in paragraph 1 and aligning yourself explicitly at the end of body 2 — is equally acceptable. What is *not* acceptable is presenting both views and never stating your own, which is the most common Task Response failure on this question type.

### Model Essay 3 — Problem and Solution

> **Task:** An increasing number of people are becoming dependent on their smartphones. What problems does this cause, and what measures could be taken to address them?

```
Smartphone ownership has moved in under two decades from a novelty to a
near-universal condition, and with it a pattern of use that many people
themselves describe as compulsive. This dependence carries two significant
costs — fragmented attention and displaced sleep — both of which can be
addressed, though only partly through individual effort.

The first and most pervasive problem is the erosion of sustained attention.
Because notifications arrive continuously and each one carries a small
reward, users acquire the habit of checking their device every few minutes,
which makes any task requiring prolonged concentration substantially harder.
The cost is easy to underestimate: a knowledge worker interrupted every ten
minutes may put in a full day and produce very little of consequence,
because the deep work that produces real output never begins. The second
problem is physiological. Devices used late at night both delay sleep
directly and suppress the hormonal signals that initiate it, and chronic
short sleep is now firmly associated with impaired memory, poorer mood
regulation and long-term health harm.

Several measures would help, at different levels. Individually, the most
effective intervention is environmental rather than moral: charging the phone
outside the bedroom removes the temptation entirely, whereas relying on
willpower against a device engineered to be compelling rarely succeeds.
Institutionally, employers can protect concentration by establishing
meeting-free periods and by making it explicit that messages need not be
answered within minutes. Regulation has a role too, since the underlying
incentive is commercial: platforms whose revenue depends on time spent will
not voluntarily reduce it, so requirements around default notification
settings and features aimed at children are more likely to change behaviour
at scale than public awareness campaigns.

In conclusion, smartphone dependence damages both concentration and sleep,
and the most credible responses combine changes to personal environment with
workplace norms and regulatory pressure on the platforms themselves.

                                                              [~315 words]
```

*Structural note:* two problems in one paragraph, then solutions in the next, with each solution explicitly matched to a problem. The alternative structure — problem 1 + its solution in body 1, problem 2 + its solution in body 2 — is equally valid and slightly easier to keep balanced. Choose one and be consistent.

### Model Essay 4 — Advantages Outweigh Disadvantages

> **Task:** More and more people are working from home rather than in an office. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?

```
Remote work moved from a marginal arrangement to a mainstream one within a
few years, and organisations are still deciding how permanent the shift
should be. In my view the advantages do outweigh the drawbacks for most
knowledge-based roles, provided that employers actively compensate for what
is lost when people no longer share a building.

The benefits are substantial and fall to both parties. Employees reclaim the
commute, which in a large city can amount to ten hours a week of unpaid,
unproductive time, and gain the uninterrupted stretches that demanding work
requires. Employers, meanwhile, can recruit from a far wider pool: a company
in Lahore competing for a specialist it cannot find locally may hire in
another city or another country entirely, which raises the quality of the
team while reducing the cost of office space. Where output can be measured
directly, as with software or design, there is little reason to insist on
physical presence.

The disadvantages are real but largely manageable. The genuine loss is
informal communication — the unplanned conversation in which a junior
colleague overhears the answer to a problem they did not know how to ask
about — and this bears most heavily on new and inexperienced staff, whose
learning depends on exactly that ambient exposure. Isolation is a further
risk for those who live alone. Both, however, respond to deliberate design:
structured mentoring, written documentation of decisions, and periodic
in-person weeks recover much of what distance removes, whereas simply adding
video meetings tends to make matters worse.

On balance, therefore, the advantages prevail, but conditionally. Remote work
delivers time, focus and access to talent; it withholds the incidental
learning that organisations previously received for free. Companies that
recognise the second point and invest in replacing it will find the trade
strongly favourable, while those that ignore it will slowly lose the ability
to develop their own people.

                                                              [~315 words]
```

### Model Essay 5 — Two-Part Question

> **Task:** Many people now change careers several times during their working lives. Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?

```
A single career held from graduation to retirement, once the norm in most
industrialised economies, has become the exception. This change is driven
chiefly by the speed at which industries themselves now change, and on
balance I regard it as a positive development, albeit one that distributes
its benefits unevenly.

The principal cause is the shortening lifespan of the skills a job requires.
Entire occupational categories now appear and disappear within a single
working life: roles built around digital advertising or mobile applications
did not exist thirty years ago, while much routine clerical work has been
automated away. A worker whose specialism becomes obsolete has no option but
to move, and employers, facing the same volatility, offer far less job
security than they once did, which removes much of the incentive for loyalty.
Rising life expectancy compounds the effect, since a career spanning forty-five
years is simply less likely to remain viable in one form throughout.

Whether this is desirable depends largely on whether the change is chosen or
imposed, but I would argue the overall effect is favourable. Someone who
discovers at thirty that their work does not suit them is no longer expected
to endure it for another three decades, and the accumulated experience of
people who have worked in several fields tends to make organisations more
adaptable. The obvious counterweight is insecurity: retraining costs money
and time, and older workers displaced from declining industries frequently
land in worse positions than they left. That, however, is an argument for
better public provision of retraining rather than for a return to lifelong
single careers.

In conclusion, careers now change repeatedly because the skills economies
need change repeatedly, and this flexibility benefits both individuals and
employers — provided that societies invest in helping people make those
transitions rather than leaving the cost to fall on the workers concerned.

                                                              [~310 words]
```

### The Five-Minute Planning Method

```
Minute 1  DECODE THE QUESTION
  Underline: the TOPIC, the TASK WORDS, and any LIMITING words.
    "Some people believe universities should only offer courses that are
     useful for future careers. To what extent do you agree?"
     topic = purpose of universities · task = agree/disagree · limit = "only"
  That word "only" is where most candidates lose Task Response: the extreme
  version is easy to disagree with, and your essay must engage with it.

Minute 2  DECIDE YOUR POSITION
  Not the truest position — the one you can develop best in 300 words.
  Write it as one sentence. That sentence becomes your thesis.

Minutes 3–4  GENERATE TWO IDEAS AND ONE EXAMPLE EACH
  Idea 1: ____________  Example: ____________
  Idea 2: ____________  Example: ____________
  If you cannot produce an example, discard the idea and pick another. An
  idea you cannot exemplify will not develop into a paragraph.

Minute 5  ORDER AND CHECK
  Does my plan answer every part of the question? (Two-part questions!)
  Is my position consistent between the thesis and both body paragraphs?
  Then write. Do not deviate from the plan to chase a new idea at minute 25.
```

### Managing Word Count Without Counting

```
Counting words costs 90 seconds you cannot spare. Calibrate instead:

  1. Write one Task 2 essay by hand or on screen exactly as in the exam.
  2. Count the words once. Note how many LINES that took (paper) or how many
     lines fill the box (screen).
  3. Divide: e.g. 300 words = 30 lines → 10 words per line for you.
  4. From then on, count LINES. 250 words = 25 lines. Check once, at the end.

Typical calibration targets:
  Task 1: 170–200 words   Task 2: 280–320 words
Under-length is penalised directly. Massively over-length costs checking time
and usually adds repetition, which harms Lexical Resource.
```

## Cohesion, Referencing, and Paragraph Flow

### Cohesive Devices — Range Without Mechanical Overuse

```
ADDITION        Furthermore · Moreover · In addition · What is more ·
                Equally important · Beyond this
CONTRAST        However · Nevertheless · Nonetheless · By contrast ·
                Conversely · That said · Whereas · While
CAUSE           because · since · as · owing to · due to · given that
EFFECT          Consequently · As a result · Therefore · Thus · Hence ·
                which means that · with the result that
EXAMPLE         For instance · For example · To illustrate ·
                A case in point is · take X, for example
CONCESSION      Admittedly · While it is true that · Granted ·
                Although · Even if · There is some force in the view that
EMPHASIS        Indeed · In particular · Crucially · Above all
SEQUENCE        Initially · Subsequently · At this stage · Finally
SUMMARY         In conclusion · On balance · Overall · To sum up

Rules that matter:
  Vary them. Three "Moreover"s in one essay is flagged as mechanical.
  Not every sentence needs one. Band 7 descriptors penalise OVER-use as well
    as under-use. Roughly one per two or three sentences is natural.
  Punctuate correctly:
    ✓ "However, the cost is high."        ✗ "However the cost is high."
    ✓ "The cost is high; however, ..."    ✗ "The cost is high, however, ..."
       (comma splice — a Grammar error, not a Cohesion one)
```

### Referencing — the Invisible Half of Cohesion

Examiners look for referencing explicitly, and it simultaneously solves your repetition problem.

```
First mention:   "social media platforms"
Then:            "these platforms" · "they" · "such services" ·
                 "the platforms in question" · "the latter"

Devices to use:
  this / these / such / that                  → point back to a whole idea
  the former / the latter                     → two things just mentioned
  the latter approach / this tendency         → noun + demonstrative
  doing so / to do so                        → replaces a repeated verb phrase
  which / who / whose                        → relative clauses join ideas

Example without referencing (Band 6):
  "Governments should invest in public transport. Public transport reduces
   congestion. Public transport is also better for the environment."

Example with referencing (Band 7+):
  "Governments should invest in public transport, which reduces congestion
   more reliably than road-building does. Such systems are also
   substantially cleaner, and doing so signals a long-term commitment that
   drivers respond to."
```

### Making Paragraphs Flow Into Each Other

```
Weak (paragraphs sit side by side):
  Para 2 ends: "...so the economic case is strong."
  Para 3 opens: "Secondly, there are social benefits."

Strong (the second paragraph acknowledges the first):
  Para 2 ends: "...so the economic case is strong."
  Para 3 opens: "The social argument, though less easily quantified, is
                 arguably more important."

The pattern: reference the previous idea, then introduce the new one.
  "Beyond the financial dimension, ..."
  "This economic argument, however, is not the only consideration."
  "A similar pattern is visible in education."
```

## Common Writing Errors and Fixes

| Error | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No overview (T1) | Introduction then straight to figures | One sentence starting "Overall," — always |
| Causes in Task 1 | "Sales rose because of advertising" | Describe only: "Sales rose, coinciding with…" |
| Copying the question | Introduction reuses the exact prompt wording | Paraphrase; copied text is excluded from the word count |
| Unclear position | "There are advantages and disadvantages" as the thesis | Commit: "I would argue that X, primarily because Y" |
| Missing half the question | Two-part question, only part one answered | Underline every question mark in the prompt before planning |
| Underdeveloped ideas | Three assertions, no explanation | Topic → Explain → Example → Consequence, every paragraph |
| Repeating the key noun | "technology" eight times | Build a paraphrase set before you start writing |
| Memorised opening | "In this modern era of globalisation…" | Write a real, specific first sentence about the actual topic |
| Comma splice | "The cost is high, however it is worth it" | Semicolon or full stop before "however" |
| Article errors | "The technology has changed the society" | Chapter 5 — the single highest-frequency error category |
| Uncountable plurals | "researches", "informations", "advices" | These have no plural form; use "research", "information", "advice" |
| Overusing "very" | "very big problem" | Use precise adjectives: "acute", "substantial", "pressing" |
| Informal register | "gonna", "lots of", "kids", "etc." | "going to", "a great many", "children", finish the list |
| No paragraphing | One block of 300 words | Four to five paragraphs, one clear idea each |
| Conclusion adds new ideas | New argument in the last paragraph | Conclusion only restates and implies |
| Equal time on both tasks | 30 minutes each | 20 / 40. Task 2 is worth double |

## Self-Check Checklist — 20 Points, 5 Minutes

Run this at the end of each task in practice until it becomes automatic.

```
TASK 1
   1. Is there an overview paragraph? Does it begin "Overall," or equivalent?
   2. Does the overview avoid specific numbers?
   3. Did I paraphrase the prompt rather than copying it?
   4. Are all figures transcribed correctly?
   5. Is the tense right and consistent throughout?
   6. Prepositions: rise FROM/TO, BY, stood AT, peaked AT?
   7. Have I removed every cause, opinion, and prediction?
   8. Are there at least 150 words (about 15 lines for most handwriting)?

TASK 2
   9. Have I answered every part of the question, including both halves of a
      two-part prompt?
  10. Is my position stated in the introduction as the last sentence?
  11. Is that position the same in both body paragraphs and the conclusion?
  12. Does each body paragraph have a topic sentence with ONE idea?
  13. Does each body paragraph contain a specific example?
  14. Does the conclusion restate without introducing new arguments?
  15. Are there at least 250 words (about 25 lines)?

BOTH
  16. Articles: have I checked every singular countable noun for a/an/the?
  17. Agreement: does every verb match its subject in number?
  18. Plurals: is every countable plural marked, and no uncountable pluralised?
  19. Have I varied my linking words instead of repeating one?
  20. Have I checked the spelling of the topic's key vocabulary?
```

**The three-error rule for practice:** after self-checking, identify your three most frequent error types across the last five tasks and write them on a card. Check only for those three first on your next task. Error correction generalises far better when it is targeted than when it is diffuse.

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: What is the most common reason candidates lose marks in Writing Task 2?**
A: Task Response failures, which cost a quarter of the score for that task and can drag the whole Writing band down by a full band. The three specific patterns are: not answering the exact question asked, most often by responding to the general topic rather than the precise prompt; failing to take a clear, consistent position, which usually shows up as an introduction saying "there are advantages and disadvantages" and never resolving; and answering only half of a two-part question. The second most common problem is underdeveloped ideas — three or four assertions with no explanation, example, or consequence, which reads as a list of opinions rather than an argument. The fix for both is mechanical: spend five minutes planning, write the position as a single sentence before you start, and build every body paragraph as topic sentence, explanation, example, consequence.

**Q2: Why is the overview paragraph in Task 1 so important?**
A: Because the official band descriptors tie it directly to Task Achievement: a response with no clear overview cannot score above Band 5 on that criterion, regardless of how accurate and well-written the rest is. Since Task Achievement is 25% of the Task 1 score, one missing sentence can cost half a band overall. The overview should be two sentences, contain no specific figures, and state the biggest-picture facts — the general direction of all series, which is highest or lowest, and whether gaps widened or narrowed. Start it with the word "Overall," so it is impossible for the examiner to miss. It is the cheapest and most reliable half-band available in the entire exam, and it takes about forty seconds to write.

**Q3: Can I use personal examples and the word "I" in Task 2?**
A: Yes for "I", sparingly for personal examples. Stating your position requires first person — "I would argue that", "In my view" — and this is entirely appropriate; the register of an IELTS essay is formal but not impersonal in the way a scientific paper is. Personal anecdotes, on the other hand, are weak evidence and can make the essay read as informal if they dominate. The strong compromise is a professionally framed example: "A mid-sized software company, for instance, may spend as much on office space as on five engineers' salaries" gives you concreteness and specificity without narrating your life. For Task 1 the rule is absolute: never use "I think" or "in my opinion", because the task is objective description of data and any opinion is off-task.

**Q4: Do I need real statistics and facts in my essays?**
A: No, and inventing precise-looking statistics tends to hurt you. Examiners are not fact-checkers and there are no marks for factual accuracy, but a sentence like "studies show that 78% of employees prefer remote work" is transparently made up and contributes nothing to any criterion. What the rubric rewards is *development* — an idea explained and illustrated concretely. A well-constructed hypothetical case, described specifically, evidences everything a fabricated number does not: precise vocabulary, complex grammar, and a supported argument. As a software engineer you have a genuine advantage here, because you can draw specific, plausible examples from your own field for the technology, work, education, and automation topics that dominate the Task 2 question bank.

**Q5: How should I split the 60 minutes, and what if I run out of time?**
A: Twenty minutes on Task 1 and forty on Task 2, because Task 2 carries roughly double the weight. Write Task 1 first, since it is more formulaic and finishing it builds momentum, but treat the twenty-minute mark as a hard stop — an unfinished Task 1 costs less than a rushed Task 2. Within Task 2, allocate five minutes to planning, thirty to writing, and five to checking. If you find yourself at minute 55 with an unwritten conclusion, write two sentences of conclusion immediately, even if it means abandoning the second half of a body paragraph, because a missing conclusion is a visible structural failure under Coherence and Cohesion. The most common self-inflicted version of this problem is spending thirty minutes on Task 1 to make it perfect, which trades a small gain on the lighter task for a large loss on the heavier one.

## Practical Tasks

### Beginner (10 Tasks)
1. Write ten Task 1 overview paragraphs only, from ten different charts, each beginning "Overall," and containing no figures.
2. Write ten Task 1 introductions by paraphrasing ten prompts without reusing more than three consecutive words.
3. Copy out the preposition rules (from/to, by, at, of) and write fifteen sentences using them with real data.
4. Take one line graph and write the full four-paragraph answer untimed. Then check it against the model in this chapter.
5. Write one paragraph using the Topic → Explain → Example → Consequence pattern, and label each of the four moves in the margin.
6. Identify the essay type for twenty Task 2 prompts, and write the required response in one line for each.
7. Write five thesis statements — one for each essay type — each as a single sentence with a position and a reason.
8. Rewrite three cliché introductions ("In this modern era…") as specific, topic-focused openings.
9. Calibrate your word count: write one 300-word essay and work out your words per line.
10. Run the 20-point self-check on one of your own old essays and count how many points it fails.

### Intermediate (10 Tasks)
1. Write one complete Task 1 and one complete Task 2 under strict timing (20 and 40 minutes) on three separate days.
2. Write one answer for each of the seven Academic Task 1 types (line, bar, pie, table, process, map, mixed).
3. If taking General Training: write one formal, one semi-formal, and one informal letter, and have someone check the register.
4. Write one essay of each of the five Task 2 types.
5. Take an old essay and rewrite it using referencing (this, such, the latter, doing so) to remove every repeated key noun.
6. Take an old essay and rewrite it adding four deliberate complex structures: a relative clause, a conditional, a passive, and a participle clause.
7. Build paraphrase sets for ten common Task 2 topic nouns before writing anything on those topics.
8. Do a cohesion audit: highlight every linking word in one essay and check that none appears more than twice.
9. Write two essays on the same prompt, one arguing each side, and compare which developed more easily.
10. Compile your personal three-error card from five marked tasks and check only for those on the next five.

### Advanced (10 Tasks)
1. Complete four full Writing papers (both tasks, 60 minutes) in one week under exam conditions.
2. Get at least two of them marked by a qualified tutor or an examiner-marked service, criterion by criterion.
3. Self-mark five of your own essays against the official band descriptors and compare with the external marking; investigate every disagreement.
4. Rewrite one Band 6 essay into a Band 8 essay and annotate exactly which changes moved which criterion.
5. Write one essay under a 30-minute constraint to build speed, then one under the normal 40 to feel the difference.
6. Practise the recovery drill: with ten minutes left and no conclusion, write the conclusion first, then fill back.
7. Take five prompts with limiting words ("only", "always", "the best way") and write introductions that engage explicitly with the limit.
8. Write a Task 2 essay using no linking word more than once in the entire essay.
9. Analyse three official model answers at Band 8–9 and list every structure they use that you do not.
10. Sit a full Writing paper handwritten (or typed, matching your test format) with no dictionary, and audit your own error density: count error-free sentences as a percentage.

## Cheat Sheet

```
TIME AND LENGTH
  Task 1  20 min · 150+ words (target 170–200) · one third of the band
  Task 2  40 min · 250+ words (target 280–320) · two thirds of the band
  Task 2 internal split: 5 min plan · 30 min write · 5 min check

TASK 1 STRUCTURE (Academic)
  P1 Introduction — paraphrase the prompt, 1 sentence
  P2 Overview     — 2 sentences, NO numbers, begin "Overall,"    ← critical
  P3 Detail 1     — biggest trend/group, with figures
  P4 Detail 2     — remaining features, with figures + comparison

TASK 1 PREPOSITIONS
  rise FROM 20% TO 45% · rise BY 25 points · stood AT 45% · peaked AT 68% IN 2015
  an increase OF 20% IN the number OF users · figures doubled/halved

TASK 1 TENSES
  past dates → past simple · no dates → present simple · projections → will/is expected to
  process → present simple PASSIVE · maps → present perfect / past passive

GT TASK 1 LETTERS
  Formal      Dear Sir or Madam → Yours faithfully
  Semi-formal Dear Mr Khan     → Yours sincerely
  Informal    Dear Sana / Hi   → All the best / Take care
  Cover ALL THREE bullet points. State the purpose in sentence 1.

TASK 2 STRUCTURE
  P1 Context (1–2 sentences) + THESIS as the last sentence
  P2 Topic → Explain → Example → Consequence
  P3 Topic → Explain → Example → Consequence  (or counter + rebuttal)
  P4 Restate position differently + one implication. No new arguments.

TASK 2 TYPES AND WHAT THEY DEMAND
  Opinion            one position, sustained
  Discussion         both views fairly + YOUR opinion (never omit it)
  Adv/Disadv         both; if "outweigh", give a verdict
  Problem/Solution   both halves, matched, balanced
  Two-part question  answer EVERY question in the prompt

PARAGRAPH ANATOMY (the most important pattern in this chapter)
  TOPIC sentence · EXPLAIN the mechanism · EXAMPLE, specific · CONSEQUENCE, linked back

NEVER
  Task 1: causes, opinions, predictions, or a missing overview
```
