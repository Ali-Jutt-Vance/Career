# Phase 10 — Chapter 2: IELTS Speaking — Fluency, Vocabulary, and Band 7+

---

## Chapter Overview

IELTS Speaking is the section candidates fear most and the one that improves fastest, because almost all of the marks lost at Band 6–6.5 come from *behaviour*, not from language ability. Short answers, stopping early in Part 2, mentally translating from your first language, and reciting memorised paragraphs are habits. Habits can be replaced in three weeks.

You sit in a room with one trained examiner for 11–14 minutes. The conversation is recorded. The examiner follows a fixed frame and cannot coach you, hint at your score, or react to your opinions. They also cannot penalise your accent — Pakistani, Indian, Nigerian, Chinese, and Brazilian accents are all fully compatible with Band 9.

**Topics:**
- Exactly how the four Speaking criteria are applied, and what Band 6 vs 7 vs 8 sounds like
- Part 1: the answer shape that produces the right length automatically
- Part 2: the story-bank method — six adaptable stories cover almost every cue card
- Part 3: how to sound analytical instead of opinionated
- Fluency mechanics: discourse markers, thinking time, and repair
- Vocabulary: collocation, precision, and using idiom without sounding rehearsed
- Pronunciation: the five things actually scored, and daily drills
- Software-engineering topics that recur, with model answers

## Beginner Theory

### The Three Parts

```
PART 1 — Introduction and interview                        4–5 minutes
  Identity check, then 3 familiar topics, ~4 questions each
  Topics: work/study, home, hometown, then 2 rotating everyday topics
          (food, weather, transport, phones, music, shopping, sleep, hobbies)
  Purpose: settle you down and sample everyday language
  Answer length: 2–4 sentences. Not one. Not eight.

PART 2 — Long turn (cue card)                              3–4 minutes
  Examiner hands you a card, paper, and pencil
  1 minute preparation
  1–2 minutes of uninterrupted speech (aim to be stopped at 2:00)
  1–2 short rounding-off questions afterwards
  Purpose: can you organise extended monologue without support?

PART 3 — Two-way discussion                                4–5 minutes
  Abstract questions thematically linked to the Part 2 topic
  The examiner may challenge you, ask for clarification, or shift direction
  Answer length: 4–6 sentences with reasoning
  Purpose: can you discuss abstract ideas, speculate, compare, and justify?
```

The examiner is following a script for the questions but marking you continuously across all three parts on the same four criteria. There is no separate score per part. This matters: a strong Part 3 can compensate for a shaky Part 1, and a brilliant Part 2 cannot rescue monosyllabic answers everywhere else.

### The Four Criteria, In Plain Language

```
FLUENCY AND COHERENCE (25%)
  Can you keep going at a normal speaking rate without long pauses?
  Do your ideas connect in an order the listener can follow?
  Do you use discourse markers naturally ("actually", "the thing is", "mind you")?
  NOT scored: speed. Fast, panicked speech scores lower than measured speech.

LEXICAL RESOURCE (25%)
  Range: do you have words for a variety of topics, including less common ones?
  Precision: is "commute" used where "commute" is right, not just "travel"?
  Collocation: "heavy traffic", "make a decision", "a steep learning curve"
  Paraphrase: when you don't know a word, can you talk around it smoothly?

GRAMMATICAL RANGE AND ACCURACY (25%)
  Range: do complex forms appear — relative clauses, conditionals, perfect
         aspects, passives, participle clauses?
  Accuracy: what proportion of your sentences are error-free?
  NOT scored: using the longest possible sentences. Accuracy beats ambition.

PRONUNCIATION (25%)
  Intelligibility: can the examiner understand you without effort throughout?
  Word stress, sentence stress, connected speech, intonation, chunking
  NOT scored: your accent, or sounding British/American.
```

### What Each Band Actually Sounds Like

| Criterion | Band 6 | Band 7 | Band 8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluency | Willing to speak at length, but repetition, self-correction and hesitation are noticeable; some loss of coherence | Speaks at length without noticeable effort; some hesitation, mainly to find *ideas* not words; uses a range of connectives | Fluent with only rare repetition; hesitation is content-related; develops topics coherently and appropriately |
| Lexical | Enough vocabulary to discuss topics at length; meaning is clear despite inappropriate word choices; can paraphrase with some success | Flexible vocabulary to discuss a variety of topics; uses some less common and idiomatic items with occasional inaccuracy; paraphrases effectively | Wide resource used readily and precisely; uses less common and idiomatic language naturally; skilful paraphrase |
| Grammar | Mix of simple and complex forms, but complex ones are less accurate; errors are frequent though rarely block meaning | Range of complex structures with some flexibility; **frequently produces error-free sentences**; a few persistent errors | Wide range used flexibly; majority of sentences error-free; occasional slips only |
| Pronunciation | Mixed control of features; generally understood but individual words or sounds reduce clarity | Uses a range of pronunciation features with mixed control; generally easy to understand; L1 accent has limited effect | Easy to understand throughout; occasional lapses in individual sounds only |

**The Band 7 sentence to memorise: "hesitation to find ideas, not words."** A Band 7 candidate pausing to think "hmm, what's a good example of this…" is fine. A Band 6 candidate pausing because they are hunting for the English word for a concept is not. This is why the story bank in this chapter matters — pre-thought content removes the idea-hunting *and* the word-hunting at the same time.

### Mental Model: You Are Being Sampled, Not Interrogated

The examiner needs evidence for four criteria in under 14 minutes. Every answer is a chance to hand them evidence:

```
Question: "Do you like your job?"

Answer A (Band 5–6 evidence):
  "Yes, I like it. It is good job."
  → Evidence supplied: 2 simple present sentences, 1 article error. That's all.

Answer B (Band 7–8 evidence):
  "I do, genuinely — more than I expected to when I started. What I enjoy most
  is the problem-solving side of it: I'm a backend engineer, so most of my day
  is spent working out why a system is behaving in a way it shouldn't. If I'd
  ended up in a job that was purely repetitive, I think I'd have got bored
  very quickly."
  → Evidence supplied: emphatic 'do', comparative, cleft structure ("What I
    enjoy most is..."), colon-style elaboration, precise vocabulary (backend
    engineer, repetitive), third conditional ("If I'd ended up... I'd have
    got"), natural discourse ("genuinely", "very quickly"). Six criteria hits
    in twenty seconds.
```

You are not being asked to be interesting. You are being asked to demonstrate range. Deciding in advance which structures you will deploy is completely legitimate — memorising *sentences* is not.

## Part 1 — Introduction and Interview

### The Answer Shape

Use **Answer → Reason → Detail** (some teachers call it PEEL: Point, Explain, Example, Link). Three moves, 2–4 sentences, then stop and let the examiner ask the next question.

```
Q: "Do you use public transport often?"

Answer:  "Not as often as I probably should, to be honest."
Reason:  "I work from home three days a week, so I only really commute twice a week."
Detail:  "When I do, I take the metro rather than drive — it's about twenty minutes
          faster in rush hour and I can read on the way."
```

**Why stopping matters.** Part 1 has around 12 questions in 4–5 minutes. If you talk for 45 seconds per answer the examiner will interrupt you, which costs you nothing directly but wastes your best material on trivial topics and can dent your Fluency impression. Save the extended performance for Parts 2 and 3.

### Part 1 Topic Bank with Model Answers

```
WORK / STUDY  (asked in almost every test — prepare this properly)

Q: "Do you work or are you a student?"
A: "I work — I'm a software engineer at a company that builds logistics systems.
   I've been there for about two and a half years now."

Q: "What do you do exactly?"
A: "I work on the backend, which means the part of the system users never see.
   Day to day I build APIs, work with databases, and fix performance problems
   when something is running too slowly. It's less glamorous than the design
   side, but I find it more satisfying."

Q: "Why did you choose that job?"
A: "Partly by accident, partly by temperament. I studied computer science, so
   the technical route was natural, but what really pulled me in was that
   programming gives you immediate feedback — you find out within seconds
   whether your idea worked. I'm not sure I'd have the patience for a field
   where you wait years to see results."

Q: "Would you like to change your job in the future?"
A: "Change roles, yes; change field, probably not. I'd like to move towards
   architecture — designing how whole systems fit together rather than
   building individual pieces. That said, if the industry shifts dramatically
   because of AI, I'd want to be flexible about it."

HOMETOWN

Q: "Where are you from?"
A: "I'm from Lahore, in the eastern part of Pakistan. It's the country's
   second-largest city and easily its most food-obsessed."

Q: "What's it like living there?"
A: "It's intense, in a good way mostly. There's a real energy to the place —
   the markets are open late, there's always something happening — but the
   traffic and the air quality in winter can be genuinely difficult.
   I'd say I love it and complain about it in roughly equal measure."

Q: "Has it changed much since you were a child?"
A: "Enormously. Whole areas that were farmland when I was small are now
   housing schemes, and the ring road has changed how people move around
   completely. Some of that is progress; some of it has cost the city its
   green spaces."

TECHNOLOGY

Q: "Do you use your phone a lot?"
A: "Probably more than is healthy. Between work notifications, navigation and
   messaging family, it's rarely out of my hand. I've started leaving it in
   another room in the evening, which has helped more than I expected."

Q: "What apps do you use most?"
A: "Mostly practical ones — a messaging app for family, maps for getting
   around, and my banking app. For work I live in Slack and GitHub. I'm not
   really a heavy social media user; I find it more draining than enjoyable."

FOOD

Q: "What kind of food do you like?"
A: "I'm fairly traditional in my tastes — I'd choose home-cooked food over
   anything from a restaurant. My mother makes a lentil dish that I've tried
   and failed to reproduce about ten times."

Q: "Do you cook at home?"
A: "I do, though 'cook' might be generous. I can manage four or five dishes
   reliably and I rotate them shamelessly. Learning to cook properly is
   somewhere on my list of good intentions."

SLEEP / ROUTINE / WEATHER  (fast rotation topics — same shape works)

Q: "Do you get enough sleep?"
A: "Rarely, if I'm honest. I tend to do my best work late at night, which
   means I go to bed far too late and then regret it every morning. Six hours
   is typical, and I know that's not ideal."
```

### Building Your Own Part 1 Bank

```
Prepare CONTENT for these 14 topics — never full scripts:
  work/study · hometown · home · family · friends · free time · sports · food
  weather · transport · technology/phone · music · shopping · sleep

For each, write only:
  - 2 specific facts (a number, a place, a name)
  - 1 opinion with a reason
  - 1 contrast or change over time ("it used to be... now it's...")

That is enough raw material to answer any question on that topic in three
sentences. Specific facts are what stop you drifting into vague generalities,
which is the most common Band 6 pattern in Part 1.
```

## Part 2 — The Long Turn

### What Happens

```
Examiner: "Now I'm going to give you a topic and I'd like you to talk about
           it for one to two minutes. Before you talk you'll have one minute
           to think about what you're going to say. You can make notes if
           you wish. Here is your topic."

You get: a card, a pencil, and paper.
The card always follows this shape:

  Describe [something].
  You should say:
    - [prompt 1]
    - [prompt 2]
    - [prompt 3]
  and explain [the reflective final prompt].
```

You are not obliged to follow the bullet points, but they are a free structure — use them in order. The final "and explain…" line is the one candidates skip, and it is usually the most heavily weighted for development.

### The 60-Second Preparation Protocol

```
Seconds 0–10   Decide WHICH real experience you'll use. Do not invent something
               elaborate; a real memory produces detail automatically.
Seconds 10–50  Write 5–6 keywords ONLY, one per bullet plus two for the ending.
               Keywords, not sentences. Sentences make you read aloud, which
               destroys fluency and is audible on the recording.
Seconds 50–60  Decide your first sentence and your last sentence.

Example notes for "Describe a difficult problem you solved":
    API 8 sec → customers leaving
    thought server → actually N+1 queries
    slow query log → JOIN + index
    8s → 200ms, 40×
    lesson: method not guesswork
```

### The Structure That Fills Two Minutes

```
OPEN        "I'd like to talk about..."                          ~10 sec
CONTEXT     when, where, who — set the scene                     ~20 sec
DEVELOP     the bullets, in order, one chunk each                ~60 sec
CLIMAX      the result, the moment, the outcome                  ~15 sec
REFLECT     what it meant, what you learned, how you feel now    ~20 sec
```

The reflection section is your safety buffer. If you reach 1:40 and have nothing left, reflection can always be extended: *"Looking back, I think what stayed with me most was…"*, *"It's the kind of thing I still think about when…"*.

### Model Answer 1 — Describe a difficult problem you solved

> *You should say: what the problem was, how you discovered it, what steps you took, and how you felt afterwards.*

```
"I'd like to talk about a performance problem I solved at work last year,
because it's probably the most satisfying piece of debugging I've ever done.

The context is that I work as a backend engineer, and we had a web application
used by a few thousand customers a day. One of our main pages had become
painfully slow — it was taking around eight seconds to load, and we could see
in the analytics that people were abandoning it before it finished.

I discovered the scale of it almost by accident. I was looking at our
monitoring dashboard for something unrelated and noticed that response times
had roughly tripled over about two months, which meant it had been getting
worse gradually rather than breaking all at once. That's the dangerous kind of
problem, because nobody raises an alarm.

As for how I tackled it — I was quite methodical, mainly because I'd learned
the hard way that guessing wastes days. First I checked whether the servers
were overloaded, which they weren't. Then I looked at the database's slow query
log, and that's where the real culprit was: the code was running one query,
and then a separate query for every single row it got back. In our field we
call that an N-plus-one query problem. With a few hundred rows, that meant
hundreds of round trips to the database.

The fix itself was almost embarrassingly small — I rewrote it as a single
combined query and added an index on the column we were filtering by. Response
time dropped from eight seconds to about two hundred milliseconds, so
roughly a fortyfold improvement, and the abandonment rate on that page fell
within a day.

How did I feel? Genuinely thrilled, and also slightly annoyed at how simple it
turned out to be. But the thing I took away from it was about process rather
than technology: the reason I found it in three hours instead of three days was
that I measured before I changed anything. I've applied that to almost
everything since, including how I prepare for exams, funnily enough."
```

*Why this is Band 8 material:* varied tenses (past simple, past continuous, past perfect, present perfect), complex forms ("which meant it had been getting worse", "the reason I found it… was that"), precise vocabulary (*monitoring, abandonment rate, index*), natural discourse markers (*as for, the thing is, funnily enough*), a paraphrase of jargon for a non-technical listener, and a genuine reflection. It runs a little over two minutes, which is correct — you want to be interrupted.

### Model Answer 2 — Describe a skill you learned that took a long time

```
"The skill I'd like to talk about is touch typing, which sounds mundane but
has probably had more effect on my working life than anything else I've
deliberately learned.

I picked it up about six years ago, at the very start of my career. I was
programming for eight hours a day while still hunting for keys with two
fingers, and I remember watching a senior colleague type without ever looking
down and realising how much friction I was carrying around.

Learning it was genuinely frustrating. The strange thing about touch typing is
that you get dramatically worse before you get better — I went from about
forty words a minute using my own bad method to maybe fifteen once I forced
myself to use the correct fingers. That lasted a couple of weeks, and I very
nearly gave up. What got me through was a simple rule I set myself: fifteen
minutes of drills every morning before I opened any real work, no exceptions.

After roughly two months I was back to my original speed, and after six I was
at about ninety words a minute without looking at the keyboard at all.

What I value about it now isn't really the speed. It's that the mechanical
part of writing has disappeared, so there's no gap between thinking something
and it appearing on the screen. And more broadly, it taught me to be
suspicious of the phrase 'I'm already fine at this' — I was fine at typing,
and I was also losing an hour a day to it."
```

### The Story Bank Method

You cannot prepare for every cue card. You can prepare six flexible experiences that between them satisfy the overwhelming majority of them.

```
BUILD SIX STORIES. For each, write 6 keywords, never sentences.

1. A DIFFICULT PROBLEM YOU SOLVED (work or study)
   Covers: problem · challenge · achievement · decision · time you helped
           someone · something you're proud of · a skill you used ·
           a time you worked hard · a time technology helped you

2. A SKILL YOU LEARNED SLOWLY
   Covers: skill · something you learned · a course · a teacher who influenced
           you · a habit you changed · patience · a goal you achieved

3. A PERSON WHO INFLUENCED YOU
   Covers: person you admire · a mentor · an older person · a leader ·
           someone who taught you · a family member · a colleague

4. A PLACE THAT MATTERS TO YOU
   Covers: place you visited · a quiet place · a city · a building ·
           somewhere you'd like to return to · a favourite part of your town

5. AN OBJECT OR PIECE OF TECHNOLOGY YOU RELY ON
   Covers: useful object · a gift · something you bought · a piece of
           technology · an app · something you'd not want to lose

6. A TIME SOMETHING WENT WRONG (and what you did)
   Covers: a mistake · being late · a disagreement · a change of plan ·
           a promise you broke · a difficult decision · advice you received

ADAPTING ON THE SPOT: the first sentence does the adapting.
  Card: "Describe an achievement you are proud of"
  → "The achievement I'd like to describe is a problem I solved at work..."
  Card: "Describe a time you used technology to solve a problem"
  → "I'd like to talk about a time technology — specifically a database
     index — got me out of real trouble at work..."

The story is the same. The framing sentence and the reflection change.
```

**What you may memorise, and what you may not:**

```
✓ Memorise: the keywords of your six stories
✓ Memorise: technical vocabulary and collocations
✓ Memorise: opening frames ("The one I'd like to talk about is...")
✓ Memorise: reflection frames ("Looking back, what stays with me is...")

✗ Do NOT memorise: whole paragraphs
✗ Do NOT memorise: elaborate "high-level" sentences bolted onto any topic
    ("In this contemporary era of rapid globalisation..." — examiners hear
     this a dozen times a day and discount it entirely)

Memorised language is explicitly excluded from assessment. Worse, the shift
in rhythm between your recited sentences and your real ones is obvious on
the recording, and it drags Fluency down.
```

### Rounding-Off Questions

After your two minutes, the examiner asks one or two short questions on the same topic. These are not a trap and are not separately scored — answer in one or two sentences and stop.

```
Examiner: "Do you often solve problems like that?"
You:      "Fairly often, yes — it's most of what my job actually is,
           though rarely with results that dramatic."
```

## Part 3 — Two-Way Discussion

### What Changes

Part 3 moves from *your* experience to society, trends, causes, and speculation. The questions get longer, more abstract, and the examiner may push back. This is where Band 7 and Band 8 are separated, because abstract discussion demands exactly the structures the rubric rewards: conditionals, passives, hedged claims, comparatives, and cause-and-effect chains.

```
Question functions you will meet:

OPINION      "Do you think technology has made people less patient?"
COMPARE      "How is the way people work now different from 30 years ago?"
CAUSE        "Why do you think so many people change careers these days?"
SPECULATE    "What kind of jobs might disappear in the next twenty years?"
EVALUATE     "Is remote work better for employers or for employees?"
SOLUTION     "How could governments reduce the harm of misinformation?"
CHALLENGE    "But wouldn't that be expensive for small companies?"
```

### The Answer Engine: Opinion → Reason → Example → Qualify

```
Q: "Do you think artificial intelligence will create more jobs than it destroys?"

OPINION   "On balance I'd say yes, though I hold that view fairly loosely."
REASON    "The main reason is that every previous wave of automation has
           displaced specific tasks rather than eliminating work altogether —
           what changes is what humans are paid to do."
EXAMPLE   "In my own field, for instance, AI tools now write a lot of routine
           code, but that's created demand for people who can judge whether
           the output is safe to deploy, which barely existed as a role
           three years ago."
QUALIFY   "That said, I think the transition is what matters. Even if the
           total number of jobs holds up, a warehouse worker in their fifties
           can't simply retrain as an AI auditor, so the aggregate figures
           may hide a lot of individual damage."
```

That final qualifying move is the single highest-value habit in Part 3. It shows analytical thinking, and it naturally produces concessive and conditional grammar — *even if*, *that said*, *may hide*, *can't simply*.

### Hedging: How to Sound Educated Instead of Dogmatic

```
✗ "AI will take all jobs."
✓ "It seems likely that AI will reshape a great many jobs, though I'd
   hesitate to say it will eliminate them outright."

✗ "Social media is bad for children."
✓ "The evidence seems to point towards a link between heavy social media use
   and anxiety in teenagers, but I'm cautious about treating it as the sole
   cause — there's a lot else going on in that age group."

Hedging toolkit:
  It seems likely / it appears that / there's a strong case for
  I'd argue that / I'm inclined to think / I'd hesitate to say
  tends to / broadly speaking / by and large / to a certain extent
  arguably / conceivably / in most cases / with some exceptions
  That said / having said that / then again / mind you
```

### Handling a Question You Have No Ideas About

This will happen. The recovery is a skill, and it protects Fluency far better than silence.

```
STEP 1 — buy 3 seconds legitimately (never "um" for 5 seconds)
  "That's an interesting one — I've honestly never thought about it."
  "Hmm, let me think about that for a second."
  "I'm not sure I have a settled view, but off the top of my head..."

STEP 2 — narrow the question to something you can discuss
  "It probably depends on which country you mean. In Pakistan, for instance..."
  "I can only really speak about my own field, but there..."

STEP 3 — use one of the four universal angles
  ECONOMIC     cost, jobs, growth, who pays
  SOCIAL       relationships, community, inequality, culture
  PRACTICAL    time, convenience, infrastructure, whether it would actually work
  GENERATIONAL older people vs younger people; how it used to be

Worked example:
Q: "Should governments fund public art?"
A: "That's an interesting one — I've never thought about it directly. My
   instinct is yes, though I'd want it kept in proportion. Economically it's
   a tiny fraction of a national budget, and cities with strong cultural
   life do seem to attract both tourism and skilled workers. Socially, I
   think shared spaces matter more than we admit — a square with something
   beautiful in it gets used differently from an empty one. The practical
   objection, of course, is that when hospitals are underfunded, art looks
   like a luxury, and I can see why voters feel that way."
```

Note what happened: no knowledge about arts policy was required. Three angles, applied deliberately, produced a Band 8 answer.

### Recurring Part 3 Themes for Technology-Related Cards

```
Technology and society     surveillance, privacy, dependence, digital divide
Work                       remote work, job security, automation, work-life balance
Education                  online learning, whether degrees still matter, teachers vs AI
Communication              online vs face-to-face, social media, language change
Environment                energy use of data centres, e-waste, remote work emissions
Government                 regulation of tech companies, misinformation, digital ID
Change over time           how work/communication/shopping differ from 30 years ago
```

For each theme, prepare one statistic-shaped fact, one example from your own field, and one counterargument. Chapter 6 gives you the vocabulary banks for all of these.

### Two Model Part 3 Exchanges

```
Q: "Why do some people find it hard to work from home?"
A: "Several reasons, I think, and they're not all about discipline, which is
   the usual assumption. The obvious one is physical space — if you're sharing
   a two-bedroom flat with family, there simply isn't anywhere to concentrate.
   Then there's the social side: for people who live alone, the office is
   where most of their daily human contact happens, and losing that is
   genuinely isolating. And I'd add a third, which is that some kinds of work
   depend on incidental conversation. In software, a lot of problems get
   solved because someone overhears you complaining and says 'oh, we fixed
   that last year'. That doesn't happen on a scheduled video call."

Q (challenge): "But couldn't companies just organise more video meetings?"
A: "They could, and most have tried — but I'd argue that misunderstands the
   mechanism. The value of those hallway conversations comes precisely from
   the fact that nobody planned them. Once you put it in a calendar, you've
   already decided who's in the room and what the subject is, so the accident
   can't happen. If anything, my experience has been that adding meetings
   makes remote work worse rather than better, because it eats the
   uninterrupted time that is the main advantage of being at home."
```

## Fluency Mechanics

### Discourse Markers — the Load-Bearing Structure of Fluent Speech

```
STARTING / FRAMING
  "Well, I suppose the main thing is..."      "The way I see it..."
  "To be honest..."                            "Personally, I'd say..."
  "It's funny you should ask, because..."      "Actually, that's..."

ADDING
  "What's more..."      "On top of that..."     "And there's another point:"
  "Not only that, but..."                       "Plus, of course..."

CONTRASTING
  "That said..."        "Having said that..."   "Then again..."
  "Mind you..."         "On the other hand..."  "Whereas..."

EXEMPLIFYING
  "For instance..."     "Take my own job — ..." "A good example would be..."
  "I'm thinking of..."  "Say you had..."

REASONING
  "The reason being that..."   "Which means that..."   "So what happens is..."
  "That's largely because..."  "It follows that..."

CONCLUDING
  "So all in all..."    "Which is why I'd say..."  "Ultimately..."
  "But that's just my take on it."
```

Use 4–6 different ones across the test. Using the same one nine times ("and also… and also…") is what a Band 6 transcript looks like.

### Buying Thinking Time Legitimately

```
✗ Silence for 4 seconds
✗ "Um... um... aaah..."
✗ "Repeat please" (in Part 2, or repeatedly anywhere)

✓ "That's a good question, actually."
✓ "Let me think about that for a moment."
✓ "I've not been asked that before — my first thought is..."
✓ Restate the question in your own words:
   Q: "Do you think city life is becoming less attractive?"
   A: "Whether cities are losing their appeal, you mean? I'd say it varies
      enormously by city..."

Restating is the most useful of these: it fills 3 seconds, demonstrates
paraphrase (which is Lexical Resource evidence), and gives your brain the
gap it needs.
```

### Repair — Fixing an Error Without Losing Marks

```
Self-correction is normal at Band 7 and above. What matters is that it's
quick and confident.

✓ "He go— he went to the meeting instead of me."     (instant, no fuss)
✓ "It's very... well, 'convenient' is the word I want." (repair with a comment)
✗ "He go... he go... sorry... he... how do I say... he going..."  (spiral)

Rules:
  Correct once. Then continue.
  Never explain your error or apologise for your English.
  If you can't find a word, TALK AROUND IT — do not stop:
    "...that thing you use to — the tool that measures how fast the code
     runs, a profiler I think it's called."
  Paraphrasing under pressure is explicitly rewarded in the descriptors.
```

### Speaking at Length Without Rambling

```
The Band 6 failure mode is stopping too early.
The Band 6.5 failure mode is going on too long with no structure.

Fix both with signposting inside your answer:

  "There are two things I'd point to. The first is [X]... and the second,
   which I think matters more, is [Y]."

  "Short answer: yes. The longer answer is that..."

Signposting commits you to a length and tells the examiner you're organised.
It converts rambling into structure at zero linguistic cost.
```

## Vocabulary for Speaking

### Precision Beats Fanciness

```
The Band 7 descriptor rewards flexibility and precision — not obscurity.

✗ "I utilise public transportation to commute to my workplace on a
   quotidian basis."   (unnatural register; examiners mark this DOWN)
✓ "I take the metro to work most days."   (precise, natural, correct)

Precision upgrades that DO earn marks, because they say something more exact:
  travel → commute (specifically, to work, regularly)
  problem → bottleneck (specifically, the constraining part)
  big increase → surge / spike (specifically, sudden)
  tired → drained / worn out / running on empty
  interested in → drawn to / fascinated by / hooked on
  busy → snowed under / stretched thin / flat out
```

### Word Families — One Word Becomes Four

```
analyse    → analysis     → analytical    → analytically
develop    → development  → developmental → developmentally
communicate→ communication→ communicative → communicatively
innovate   → innovation   → innovative    → innovatively
rely       → reliance     → reliable      → reliably
sustain    → sustainability→ sustainable  → sustainably
compete    → competition  → competitive   → competitively

```

Getting the *form* wrong is a Lexical Resource error even when the word choice is right ("it was very *develop*"). Drill families, not isolated words.

### Idiom — Use Sparingly and Only When Natural

The descriptors reward idiomatic language at Band 7+, but a forced idiom costs more than a missing one.

```
Safe, genuinely common, and useful in Part 3:
  a double-edged sword          — has real benefits and real costs
  a mixed blessing              — same, milder
  the tip of the iceberg        — a small visible part of a bigger issue
  a step in the right direction — partial improvement
  keep up with the times        — stay current
  at the cutting edge           — most advanced
  easier said than done         — sounds simple, isn't
  in the long run               — over time
  a grey area                   — not clearly right or wrong
  a false economy               — saving money now, costing more later

Avoid entirely:
  "It's raining cats and dogs"  — no native speaker under 60 says this
  "Every coin has two sides"    — an IELTS cliché; examiners flinch
  Any idiom you have not heard a real person use
```

## Pronunciation

### The Five Things Actually Scored

```
1. INTELLIGIBILITY — the master criterion
   Can the examiner understand you without straining, for 14 minutes?
   Most common destroyers: dropped final consonants, mumbling, speaking too fast.
   Fix: slow down about 10%, and finish your words. Especially -ed, -s, -t, -k.

2. WORD STRESS — wrong stress genuinely blocks recognition
   comPUter   deVElop   phoTOGrapher   photoGRAPHic   PHOtograph
   deVELopment  techNOLogy  techNOLogical  ecoNOMic  ECOnomy
   Note how stress MOVES within a family. Learn the family, not the word.

3. SENTENCE STRESS — stress content words, weaken function words
   "I WORK as a backend ENGINEER at a LOGISTICS company."
   Flat, even stress on every word is the most common cause of a
   "monotone / hard to follow" impression.

4. CONNECTED SPEECH — natural linking, not sloppiness
   want to → "wanna"      going to → "gonna"      did you → "didja"
   a lot of → "a lotta"   next week → "neks week"
   Using these makes you MORE intelligible, not less professional. Robotic
   word-by-word separation is harder to follow than natural linking.

5. INTONATION AND CHUNKING
   Statements fall at the end.  Yes/no questions rise.
   Lists rise, rise, then fall: "Python↗, JavaScript↗, and SQL↘"
   Chunk into thought groups with tiny pauses, not word-by-word:
   "The main problem / was that the database / was being queried / far too often."
```

### Sounds That Commonly Need Attention for South Asian Speakers

Not errors of accent — accent is not scored. These are the specific contrasts that most often cause a *listener* to mishear a word:

```
/v/ vs /w/     very / wary        vine / wine        vet / wet
/θ/ vs /t/     three / tree       thin / tin         path / part
/ð/ vs /d/     they / day         then / den
/z/ vs /j/     zoo / — (final -s often becomes /s/: "jobs" → "jobss")
/p/ vs /f/     — check aspiration on p- at word start
Final clusters -sts, -kts, -lds:  "texts", "facts", "worlds", "asked"
                                   Slow these down deliberately.
-ed endings:   worked /t/ · planned /d/ · needed /ɪd/
               Dropping these also reads as a GRAMMAR error — double cost.
```

### Daily Pronunciation Drill (10 minutes)

```
Minute 1–3   SHADOWING
  Play 30 seconds of a TED talk or BBC news. Play it again and speak
  simultaneously with the speaker, copying rhythm and stress, not accent.
  Repeat the same 30 seconds five times. Same clip all week.

Minute 4–6   RECORD AND COMPARE
  Read one paragraph aloud, recorded. Then listen to a native reading
  similar material. Compare: where do they pause? Which words are loudest?

Minute 7–8   MINIMAL PAIRS
  Five pairs from the list above, ten repetitions each, exaggerated.

Minute 9–10  FREE SPEECH, RECORDED
  Talk for 90 seconds on any Part 1 topic. Then listen back once with a
  single question in mind: "would a stranger understand every word?"

Weekly check: dictate a 60-second answer into your phone's voice-to-text.
Every word it gets wrong is a word a human examiner may also mishear.
This is a crude test, but it is honest and it is free.
```

## Common Speaking Mistakes and Fixes

| Mistake | Why it costs marks | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One-word Part 1 answers | Supplies almost no evidence for any criterion | Answer → Reason → Detail, every time |
| Stopping at 50 seconds in Part 2 | Task not fulfilled; Fluency capped | Timed practice until 2 minutes feels short |
| Reciting memorised paragraphs | Memorised language is excluded from assessment | Prepare keywords and frames only |
| Translating from L1 in your head | Long pauses and L1 word order | Think in English chunks; see Chapter 5 |
| Speaking very fast | Reduces intelligibility, not a fluency plus | Slow 10%; chunk into thought groups |
| Using only present simple | Grammatical Range capped at 6 | Deliberately deploy 4 tenses per part |
| Repeating the question's words | Lexical Resource capped | Paraphrase the question as you begin |
| "Sorry, my English is bad" | Cannot help; primes a negative impression | Never comment on your own English |
| Answering "I don't know" | Zero evidence produced | Use the 3-step recovery protocol |
| Long silence to find a word | Directly penalised under Fluency | Talk around it; paraphrase out loud |
| Whispering / hand over mouth | Pronunciation is 25% and needs to be audible | Sit up, project, hands down |
| Arguing with the examiner's challenge | Not penalised, but wastes time | Concede partially, then re-justify |

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: How do I get Band 7 in IELTS Speaking?**
A: Band 7 requires four things simultaneously: speaking at length without noticeable effort, where hesitation comes from choosing ideas rather than hunting for words; a vocabulary flexible enough to discuss unfamiliar topics, including some less common and idiomatic items; a range of complex grammatical structures with frequently error-free sentences; and pronunciation that is easy to follow throughout. In practice the most common blocker is not language but behaviour — giving short answers. Every Part 1 answer needs two to four sentences, Part 2 must fill the full two minutes, and Part 3 answers need an opinion, a reason, an example, and ideally a qualification. The second most common blocker is grammatical range: candidates who speak entirely in present simple cannot pass 6.0 on that criterion no matter how fluent they sound, so deliberately deploying conditionals, perfect aspects, and relative clauses is worth practising as a conscious drill.

**Q2: Can I prepare answers for Part 2 cue cards in advance?**
A: You should prepare content, and you must not prepare sentences. Build a bank of six real experiences — a problem you solved, a skill you learned slowly, a person who influenced you, a place that matters to you, an object you rely on, and a time something went wrong — held as five or six keywords each. Between them these cover the large majority of cue cards, because the card's framing changes but your material doesn't: the same debugging story serves "describe a difficult problem", "describe an achievement", "describe a time you used technology", and "describe a time you worked hard". What you must not do is memorise full paragraphs, because memorised language is explicitly excluded from assessment and the change of rhythm when you switch into recitation is obvious on the recording. Memorise keywords, opening frames, and reflection frames; improvise the rest.

**Q3: Does my accent affect my score?**
A: No. The Pronunciation criterion assesses intelligibility, word and sentence stress, connected speech, and intonation — not how closely you resemble a British or American speaker. Examiners are trained on and regularly assessed against a wide range of accents, and Band 9 is fully achievable with a strong regional accent. What does cost marks is anything that makes the listener work: dropped final consonants, misplaced word stress that turns a known word into an unrecognisable one, flat sentence stress that makes long answers hard to follow, and speaking too quickly. The practical implication is that accent-reduction courses are largely a waste of preparation time, while ten minutes a day of shadowing, minimal-pair drills, and recorded self-review is among the highest-return activities available.

**Q4: What happens if I don't understand the examiner's question?**
A: In Parts 1 and 3 you may ask for clarification and it is not penalised: "Sorry, could you rephrase that?" or "Do you mean X or Y?" is entirely normal and is what a competent speaker does. Do it once or twice at most, because repeated requests start to read as a comprehension problem. In Part 2 you cannot ask about the content of the card, so if a word on it is unfamiliar, interpret it in whatever way lets you speak for two minutes — you are marked on your English, not on card interpretation. The one thing never to do is answer a question you did not understand by producing an unrelated prepared speech; examiners spot that instantly and it damages the Coherence element of the Fluency score.

**Q5: Is it better to give an honest opinion or the opinion I have better vocabulary for?**
A: Take the position you can develop best. The examiner is not assessing the truth, morality, or originality of your views, and there is no bonus for sincerity or penalty for adopting a convenient position. If you are asked whether governments should tax fast food and you have three fluent sentences on the public-health side and nothing on the liberty side, argue the public-health side. That said, genuinely held views tend to produce more specific detail and fewer pauses, which is why real personal examples usually outperform invented ones. The best of both worlds is what strong candidates do naturally: state the position you can defend, then acknowledge the other side in one qualifying sentence, which both signals analytical thinking and produces concessive grammar.

## Practical Tasks

### Beginner (10 Tasks)
1. Record yourself answering ten Part 1 questions using Answer → Reason → Detail. Check each answer is two to four sentences.
2. Write your Part 1 bank for all fourteen topics: two specific facts, one opinion with a reason, one change over time per topic.
3. Speak about your job for two minutes without stopping. Note the exact second you first run out of things to say.
4. Record five minutes of free speech and count your fillers ("um", "like", "you know").
5. Learn twenty collocations from the lists in this chapter and use each in a spoken sentence.
6. Shadow the same 30-second BBC or TED clip every day for one week.
7. Write out and rehearse three different opening frames for Part 2.
8. Describe your home, your hometown, and your daily routine for one minute each, recorded.
9. Practise rising and falling intonation on ten statements and ten questions.
10. Do the ten-minute pronunciation drill five days in a row.

### Intermediate (10 Tasks)
1. Build your full six-story bank, written as keywords only, and time each story to check it reaches two minutes.
2. Do five complete Part 2 cue cards with one minute of preparation and strict two-minute timing.
3. Adapt one story to three different cue cards, changing only the opening and reflection.
4. Record a five-minute Part 3 discussion on technology and society, then transcribe two of your answers.
5. Take ten strong dogmatic statements and rewrite each with hedging language, out loud.
6. Practise the three-step recovery protocol on five questions you know nothing about.
7. Apply the four universal angles (economic, social, practical, generational) to five unfamiliar Part 3 questions.
8. Count the different tenses you use in one recorded Part 2 answer; if fewer than four, redo it.
9. Do a full Speaking mock with a partner or tutor and get marked on all four criteria separately.
10. Dictate five recorded answers into voice-to-text and fix every word it mishears.

### Advanced (10 Tasks)
1. Complete three full mock Speaking tests with different partners and compare the four-criteria breakdowns.
2. Transcribe one full mock test and count: total error-free sentences, complex structures attempted, distinct discourse markers used.
3. Self-mark that transcript against the official band descriptors, criterion by criterion, and justify each band in writing.
4. Record the same cue card three times across a week and identify what improved and what plateaued.
5. Hold a ten-minute unscripted discussion in English on a topic you have no expertise in.
6. Practise the challenge response: have a partner push back on every opinion you give for five minutes.
7. Prepare and rehearse one statistic-shaped fact, one field example, and one counterargument for each of the seven recurring Part 3 themes.
8. Deliberately deploy five specific structures (third conditional, passive, cleft sentence, participle clause, present perfect continuous) in one Part 3 session.
9. Practise idiomatic language by inserting exactly two idioms into a ten-minute session — no more, and only where natural.
10. Get one recorded mock test marked by a certified IELTS examiner or an examiner-marked service, and compare with your own self-assessment.

## Self Assessment

```
FLUENCY AND COHERENCE
  1. Can I speak for a full two minutes on any of my six stories without notes?
  2. When I pause, am I looking for an idea or hunting for a word?
  3. How many different discourse markers did I use in my last mock test?
  4. Do my Part 3 answers reach four to six sentences without rambling?

LEXICAL RESOURCE
  5. Can I paraphrase the examiner's question rather than repeating its words?
  6. Do I have precise vocabulary for all seven recurring Part 3 themes?
  7. When I don't know a word, do I talk around it smoothly, or stop?
  8. Have I used any idiom that I have actually heard a real person use?

GRAMMATICAL RANGE AND ACCURACY
  9. In my last recorded answer, how many tenses appeared? (Target: 4+)
 10. Did I produce at least one conditional, one passive, and one relative clause?
 11. What proportion of my sentences were error-free? (Band 7 needs "frequent")
 12. Which two grammar errors do I make repeatedly? (See Chapter 5.)

PRONUNCIATION
 13. Would a stranger understand every word of my last recording?
 14. Am I finishing final consonants, especially -ed and -s?
 15. Is my sentence stress varied, or am I flat and even across every word?

BEHAVIOUR
 16. Did I ever apologise for my English? (Target: never.)
 17. Did I stop early in Part 2? (Target: never.)
 18. Did any part of my answer sound recited? (Target: none.)

Scoring: 16–18 confident yeses → you are speaking at Band 7+.
         11–15 → identify the weakest criterion and drill only that for a week.
         Under 11 → you need volume: 20 recorded minutes a day for two weeks.
```

## Cheat Sheet

```
FORMAT
  Part 1  4–5 min · 3 familiar topics · answer in 2–4 sentences
  Part 2  1 min prep + 1–2 min talk · fill the full 2 minutes
  Part 3  4–5 min · abstract discussion · 4–6 sentences per answer

CRITERIA (25% each)
  Fluency & Coherence · Lexical Resource · Grammar Range & Accuracy · Pronunciation

ANSWER SHAPES
  Part 1   Answer → Reason → Detail
  Part 2   Open → Context → Develop (bullets) → Climax → Reflect
  Part 3   Opinion → Reason → Example → Qualify

PART 2 PREP (60 seconds)
  0–10  choose a REAL experience
  10–50 five or six keywords only, never sentences
  50–60 decide your first and last sentence

THE SIX-STORY BANK
  1 a difficult problem you solved      4 a place that matters to you
  2 a skill you learned slowly          5 an object you rely on
  3 a person who influenced you         6 a time something went wrong

THINKING TIME, LEGITIMATELY
  "That's an interesting question." · "Let me think for a second."
  Restate the question in your own words — fills time AND shows paraphrase.

HEDGING
  It seems likely · I'd argue that · tends to · broadly speaking
  to a certain extent · that said · then again · arguably

UNKNOWN TOPIC? FOUR ANGLES
  Economic · Social · Practical · Generational

DELIBERATE GRAMMAR TO DEPLOY EACH TEST
  a conditional        "If I hadn't taken that job, I'd probably still be..."
  a passive            "It's often assumed that..."
  a relative clause    "...the colleague who first showed me..."
  present perfect      "I've been working there for two and a half years."
  a cleft sentence     "What I enjoy most is..."

PRONUNCIATION — WHAT'S SCORED
  Intelligibility · word stress · sentence stress · connected speech · intonation
  NOT your accent.

NEVER
  Recite memorised paragraphs · stop early in Part 2 · answer in one word
  Apologise for your English · go silent hunting for a word · speak too fast
```
