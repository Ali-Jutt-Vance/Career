# Phase 10 — Chapter 5: Grammar for Band 7+

---

## Chapter Overview

If you are stuck between 6.0 and 6.5 in Writing or Speaking, grammar is almost certainly the reason — not ideas, not vocabulary, not exam technique. The official Band 7 descriptor for Grammatical Range and Accuracy requires two things at once:

```
RANGE      "uses a variety of complex structures"
ACCURACY   "produces frequent error-free sentences"
```

Most candidates satisfy one and fail the other. Cautious writers produce clean, simple sentences and are capped at 6.0 for lack of range. Ambitious writers attempt elaborate structures they cannot control and are capped at 6.0 for error density. Band 7 is the intersection: real complex structures, mostly right.

This chapter is deliberately narrow. It does not attempt to teach English grammar — it targets the specific structures the rubric rewards and the specific error categories that IELTS candidates actually produce, with drills you can mark yourself. Articles alone account for a large share of the errors in a typical Band 6 essay, and articles can be brought under control in a week.

**Topics:**
- Measuring your own error density, which is the number the rubric cares about
- Articles: a decision procedure that works in real time
- Countable and uncountable nouns, and the list that causes most trouble
- Subject–verb agreement, including the four traps
- The eight tenses IELTS actually needs, and present perfect vs past simple
- Conditionals, including the mixed forms that signal Band 8
- The passive: where it earns marks and where it costs them
- Complex sentences: relative clauses, participle clauses, noun clauses, cleft sentences
- Punctuation: comma splices, semicolons, and "however"
- Prepositions and word formation
- First-language transfer patterns for Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi and Bengali speakers
- Sixty error-correction drills with answers, and a self-editing protocol

## Beginner Theory

### Error Density — the Number That Decides Your Band

```
Take any essay you have written. Count:
   S = total number of sentences
   E = number of sentences containing at least one grammatical error
        (an error, not a stylistic preference)

   Error-free rate = (S − E) / S

Rough calibration against the descriptors:
   under 40% error-free   → Band 5–5.5
   around 50%             → Band 6
   around 70%             → Band 6.5–7   ("frequent error-free sentences")
   around 85%             → Band 7.5–8
   over 90%               → Band 8+

This is the single most useful measurement in your preparation, and almost
nobody does it. Do it on your last three essays before reading further.
```

Two consequences follow immediately:

1. **Shorter sentences are not the answer.** Cutting complexity raises your error-free rate but destroys your Range score, and the two criteria are marked together.
2. **A small number of error *types* produces most of your errors.** In practice, five categories — articles, plurals, subject–verb agreement, tense consistency, and prepositions — account for the large majority of errors in Band 6 writing. Fixing five habits is achievable. "Improving my grammar" is not.

### Your Five-Error Card

```
Before working through this chapter, take three pieces of your own writing and
mark every error. Then tally them by category:

   articles (a / an / the / zero)              ____
   plurals and uncountable nouns               ____
   subject–verb agreement                      ____
   verb tense and tense consistency            ____
   prepositions                                ____
   word form (noun/verb/adjective confusion)   ____
   sentence structure (fragments, run-ons)     ____
   punctuation (comma splice, missing stops)   ____
   articles of comparison (than/as/more)       ____
   word order                                  ____

Write your top five on a card. In the final three minutes of every writing
task, check ONLY for those five. Targeted checking finds roughly three times
as many errors as general re-reading, because you are looking for something
specific rather than hoping to notice something.
```

## Articles — The Highest-Frequency Error Category

English articles are difficult for speakers of Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, Russian, Turkish, Japanese, Korean and Chinese, because those languages either lack articles entirely or use different machinery. The good news: the rules are finite.

### The Decision Procedure

Run this in order, on every noun. With practice it takes under a second.

```
1. Is the noun PLURAL or UNCOUNTABLE?
     → and are you speaking generally?          → NO ARTICLE
        "Computers have changed education."
        "Information is easy to find."
     → and is it a specific known set?          → THE
        "The computers in our office are old."

2. Is the noun SINGULAR COUNTABLE?
     → It MUST have an article or a determiner. There is no bare singular.
        ✗ "I work as engineer."      ✓ "I work as an engineer."
        ✗ "Government should act."   ✓ "The government should act."

3. Which one — A/AN or THE?
     → First mention, or one of many, or a category member  → A / AN
        "I read a study about this."
     → Already mentioned, or unique, or identifiable from context → THE
        "The study found that..."   (the one just mentioned)
        "The internet has transformed work."   (unique)
        "The main problem is cost."   ("main" makes it unique)

4. A or AN? Go by SOUND, not spelling.
        a university (yoo-)   an hour (silent h)   an MBA (em-)
        a European (yoo-)     an honest man        a one-off (wun-)
```

### The Rules That Catch Candidates Out

```
GENERALISATIONS — three correct patterns, one wrong one
  ✓ "Computers are essential to modern work."       (plural, no article)
  ✓ "The computer has transformed modern work."     (the + singular = the class)
  ✓ "A computer is a useful tool."                  (a + singular = any example)
  ✗ "Computer is essential to modern work."         (bare singular — always wrong)

  For IELTS Writing, the plural-with-no-article form is safest and most natural.

ABSTRACT AND UNCOUNTABLE NOUNS IN GENERAL STATEMENTS — no article
  ✓ "Education should be free."          ✗ "The education should be free."
  ✓ "Technology has advanced rapidly."   ✗ "The technology has advanced rapidly."
  ✓ "Pollution is a serious problem."    ✗ "The pollution is a serious problem."
  ✓ "Society expects more."              ✗ "The society expects more."
  BUT specific ones take THE:
  ✓ "The education provided in rural schools is inadequate."
  ✓ "The pollution in Lahore worsens every winter."

SUPERLATIVES, ORDINALS, AND UNIQUE MODIFIERS — always THE
  the best solution · the first stage · the main reason · the only option ·
  the same result · the following example · the government · the environment ·
  the internet · the media · the economy · the number of

COUNTRIES AND PLACES
  No article: Pakistan · China · India · Europe · Asia · London
  THE: the United Kingdom · the USA · the Netherlands · the Philippines ·
       the UAE · the Middle East · (plurals, unions, and republics take THE)

INSTITUTIONS — no article when referring to the activity
  ✓ "go to school / university / hospital / prison / bed / work"  (the activity)
  ✓ "go to the school to meet the teacher"                        (the building)

FIXED PHRASES worth memorising as units
  in the long run · on the whole · in the past · in the future · at the moment ·
  in the meantime · to a certain extent · as a result · on the other hand ·
  in general · in particular · at present · by far the largest ·
  the vast majority of · a great deal of · a number of · the number of
```

### The Two-Minute Article Check

```
In your final check, scan for every SINGULAR COUNTABLE noun and ask one
question: does it have a determiner (a/an/the/my/this/each/one)?

If not, you have found an error. This single pass typically catches half of
all article errors in a Band 6 essay, and it takes under two minutes.
```

## Countable and Uncountable Nouns

### The List That Causes Most IELTS Errors

These are **uncountable in English** and take no plural -s and no "a/an", even though their equivalents are countable in many other languages.

```
NEVER PLURAL — the classic IELTS error list
  information       ✗ informations      ✓ pieces of information
  research          ✗ researches        ✓ studies · research findings
  advice            ✗ advices           ✓ pieces of advice
  knowledge         ✗ knowledges
  equipment         ✗ equipments        ✓ pieces of equipment
  software          ✗ softwares         ✓ software packages · applications
  hardware          ✗ hardwares
  furniture         ✗ furnitures        ✓ items of furniture
  luggage           ✗ luggages
  traffic           ✗ traffics
  progress          ✗ progresses
  homework / work   ✗ homeworks · ✗ works (except artistic works)
  money             ✗ monies (in normal usage)
  training          ✗ trainings         ✓ training courses · training sessions
  feedback          ✗ feedbacks
  employment        ✗ employments
  transport / transportation
  pollution · access · evidence · staff · accommodation · behaviour ·
  literature · music · news (looks plural, IS singular: "the news is good")

BOTH, WITH DIFFERENT MEANINGS
  time      "I don't have time" (uncountable) / "three times" (countable)
  experience "he has experience" (general) / "an unforgettable experience" (event)
  work      "I have work to do" / "the works of Shakespeare"
  space     "not enough space" / "a parking space"
  business  "business is good" / "he runs three businesses"

QUANTIFIERS — match them to the noun type
  COUNTABLE ONLY      many · few · a few · fewer · several · a number of ·
                      each · every · both
  UNCOUNTABLE ONLY    much · little · a little · less · a great deal of ·
                      a large amount of
  EITHER              some · any · a lot of · plenty of · most · all · more

  ✗ "many informations"   ✓ "much information" / "a great deal of information"
  ✗ "less people"         ✓ "fewer people"      (people are countable)
  ✗ "amount of students"  ✓ "number of students"
```

### Number Agreement in Common IELTS Phrases

```
✓ "The number of students HAS increased."       (the number = singular)
✓ "A number of students HAVE complained."       (a number of = plural)
✓ "The majority of people BELIEVE..."           (plural)
✓ "Twenty per cent of the population OWNS..."   (population = singular)
✓ "Twenty per cent of people OWN..."            (people = plural)
✓ "One in five workers IS affected."            (one = singular)
✓ "Each of the countries HAS its own policy."   (each = singular)
✓ "Neither of the options IS satisfactory."     (neither = singular, formally)
✓ "The government HAS announced..."             (BrE also allows "have")
✓ "The data SHOW..." (formal) / "The data SHOWS..." (widely accepted)
```

## Subject–Verb Agreement — The Four Traps

```
TRAP 1 — A PHRASE BETWEEN SUBJECT AND VERB
  ✗ "The number of people who own smartphones have risen."
  ✓ "The number of people who own smartphones HAS risen."
     The subject is "the number", not "smartphones". Delete the middle to test:
     "The number ... has risen."

TRAP 2 — INDEFINITE PRONOUNS ARE SINGULAR
  everyone · everybody · someone · nobody · each · either · neither · none
  ✓ "Everyone HAS a responsibility."
  ✓ "Each of the solutions HAS drawbacks."
  Note: for the possessive, "their" is standard and correct:
  ✓ "Everyone should submit their own work."

TRAP 3 — "THERE IS / THERE ARE" FOLLOWS THE REAL SUBJECT
  ✗ "There is many reasons for this."
  ✓ "There ARE many reasons for this."
  ✓ "There IS a great deal of evidence."

TRAP 4 — COMPOUND AND CORRELATIVE SUBJECTS
  "A and B" → plural:   ✓ "Cost and complexity ARE the main barriers."
  "A as well as B" → agrees with A: ✓ "The cost, as well as the risks, IS rising."
  "Either A or B" → agrees with B:  ✓ "Either the manager or the engineers ARE wrong."
  "Not only A but also B" → agrees with B: ✓ "Not only the fee but also the taxes ARE..."
```

## Tenses — The Eight That Matter

You do not need all twelve English tenses for IELTS. You need these eight, used deliberately.

| Tense | Form | IELTS use |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | works | General truths, opinions, Task 2 argument, process descriptions |
| Present continuous | is working | Current trends, temporary situations ("more people are choosing…") |
| Present perfect | has worked | Change up to now, experience, recent developments |
| Present perfect continuous | has been working | Duration up to now ("has been rising for a decade") |
| Past simple | worked | Task 1 with dates, Speaking Part 2 stories, historical facts |
| Past continuous | was working | Background action in a story ("I was reviewing the logs when…") |
| Past perfect | had worked | An earlier past, inside a past narrative |
| Future forms | will / is going to / is expected to | Predictions, Task 1 projections, plans |

### Present Perfect vs Past Simple — the Single Most Common Tense Error

```
PAST SIMPLE — finished time, and the time is stated or implied
  ✓ "I graduated in 2019."
  ✓ "Internet usage rose sharply between 2000 and 2010."
  ✓ "Yesterday I fixed the deployment pipeline."
  Signals: in 2019 · last year · yesterday · ago · when I was a student ·
           between X and Y

PRESENT PERFECT — unfinished time, or a past event with present relevance
  ✓ "I have worked here for two and a half years."   (and still do)
  ✓ "Internet usage has risen sharply since 2000."   (and continues)
  ✓ "I have already fixed it."                       (so it is fixed now)
  Signals: since · for · already · yet · so far · recently · lately ·
           over the last decade · in recent years · ever · never

THE ERROR TO ELIMINATE
  ✗ "I am working here since 2021."        (present continuous + since)
  ✓ "I have been working here since 2021."
  ✗ "I have graduated in 2019."            (perfect + finished time)
  ✓ "I graduated in 2019."
  ✗ "Since ten years I live in Lahore."
  ✓ "I have lived in Lahore for ten years."

FOR vs SINCE
  FOR + a duration:  for ten years · for six months · for a long time
  SINCE + a point:   since 2015 · since I graduated · since last March
```

### Tense in Task 1 vs Task 2

```
TASK 1 with dates in the past          → past simple throughout
   "Sales rose from 20% in 2005 to 45% in 2015."
TASK 1 with no dates / present data    → present simple
   "The chart shows that men spend longer commuting than women."
TASK 1 projections                     → will / is expected to / is projected to
   "Consumption is expected to double by 2040."
TASK 1 process diagram                 → present simple PASSIVE
   "The bottles are washed and then shredded."
TASK 1 maps (1990 → today)             → present perfect passive
   "The farmland has been replaced by a hotel."

TASK 2 general argument                → present simple
   "Automation reduces the demand for routine labour."
TASK 2 trend statements                → present perfect / present continuous
   "The number of remote workers has grown considerably."
TASK 2 hypothetical consequences       → conditionals (see below)
   "If governments invested more in retraining, displacement would be less severe."
```

### Trend Verbs and Tense in Speaking Part 2

Speaking Part 2 stories are the easiest place to demonstrate tense range, because narratives naturally use four tenses:

```
"I had been working on the project for about a month     [past perfect continuous]
 when I noticed that response times were climbing.       [past simple + past cont.]
 I had assumed it was a server problem,                  [past perfect]
 but it turned out to be the database.                   [past simple]
 Since then I've always measured before changing anything." [present perfect]
```

Rehearse one story until this pattern is automatic. It is direct evidence for Grammatical Range, and it costs nothing.

## Conditionals — Range Evidence That Is Easy to Control

```
ZERO — general truth / cause and effect
  If + present simple, present simple
  "If a system is poorly designed, it becomes expensive to maintain."

FIRST — a real future possibility
  If + present simple, will + infinitive
  "If governments invest in retraining, workers will adapt more quickly."

SECOND — hypothetical present/future (the Task 2 workhorse)
  If + past simple, would + infinitive
  "If public transport were cheaper, fewer people would drive."
  Note: "were" for all persons in formal writing ("if I were", "if it were").

THIRD — hypothetical past (impossible now)
  If + past perfect, would have + past participle
  "If the risks had been identified earlier, the failure would have been avoided."

MIXED — past condition, present result (strong Band 8 signal)
  If + past perfect, would + infinitive
  "If I hadn't taken that course, I wouldn't be in this role today."

ALTERNATIVES TO "IF" — use these for range
  Unless taxes are raised, the deficit will grow.
  Provided that funding is available, the scheme could be expanded.
  Were governments to act now, the cost would be far lower.      [inversion]
  Had the warning been heeded, the damage would have been limited. [inversion]
  Otherwise · in that case · assuming that · as long as · on condition that
```

The two inverted forms ("Were governments to act…", "Had the warning been heeded…") are among the highest-value single structures in this chapter. They are formal, unmistakably complex, easy to memorise as patterns, and hard to get wrong once learned.

## The Passive Voice

### Where It Earns Marks

```
1. PROCESS DESCRIPTIONS (Task 1) — the default grammar
   "The plastic is shredded, melted, and then extruded into pellets."

2. MAPS (Task 1)
   "A car park has been constructed to the west of the hotel."

3. WHEN THE AGENT IS OBVIOUS, UNKNOWN, OR IRRELEVANT
   "Taxes were raised in 2019." · "The data was collected over five years."

4. ACADEMIC HEDGING (Task 2) — attributing a view without naming anyone
   "It is often argued that..." · "It is widely assumed that..." ·
   "This is frequently cited as evidence that..."
   These are excellent Task 2 sentence openers and demonstrate range.

5. FOCUS AND FLOW — putting the important information first
   "Three factors are commonly held responsible for this trend."
```

### Where It Costs Marks

```
✗ Overuse. An essay written entirely in the passive is heavy and often vaguer
  than the rubric rewards ("precision" is part of Lexical Resource).
✗ Passive with intransitive verbs — impossible:
  ✗ "The problem was happened." ✓ "The problem happened / occurred."
  ✗ "He was arrived late."      ✓ "He arrived late."
✗ Missing the auxiliary or wrong participle:
  ✗ "The report was wrote by the committee."  ✓ "was written"
  ✗ "The system is design to scale."          ✓ "is designed to scale"

Target: roughly 15–25% of your sentences in the passive for Task 2, and
most of them in a Task 1 process description.
```

## Complex Sentences — The Range Toolkit

Five structures. Deploy at least three in every essay and every Speaking part.

### 1. Relative Clauses

```
DEFINING — identifies which one; NO commas
  "The engineers who work on the payment system are on call this week."
  (only those engineers, not all engineers)
  "that" is acceptable for things, and for people in less formal registers.
  The relative pronoun can be omitted when it is the object:
  "The book (that) I read last week..."

NON-DEFINING — adds extra information; COMMAS required; never "that"
  "Remote work, which was once a marginal arrangement, is now mainstream."
  "My manager, who has been there for a decade, disagreed."

PREPOSITIONAL RELATIVES — formal and high-value
  "the period during which usage doubled"
  "a problem for which there is no simple solution"
  "the extent to which technology is responsible"
  "circumstances in which regulation becomes necessary"
  "a point at which further growth is impossible"

WHOSE — for possession, people or things
  "companies whose revenue depends on advertising"

COMMON ERRORS
  ✗ "The people which live there..."        ✓ who / that
  ✗ "The reason why is because..."          ✓ "The reason is that..."
  ✗ "Technology, that has changed..."       ✓ "which has changed" (non-defining)
  ✗ "The place where I live in..."          ✓ "where I live" / "which I live in"
```

### 2. Participle Clauses — the most efficient complexity available

```
PRESENT PARTICIPLE (-ing) — same subject, simultaneous or causal
  "Realising that the servers were fine, I turned to the database."
  "Automation reduces costs, freeing resources for other work."

PAST PARTICIPLE (-ed) — passive meaning
  "Faced with rising costs, many firms reduced office space."
  "Once cleaned, the bottles are shredded into flakes."

PERFECT PARTICIPLE — an earlier action
  "Having identified the bottleneck, the team rewrote the query."

THE ONE RULE: the subject of the participle must be the subject of the main
clause, or you produce a dangling modifier.
  ✗ "Having fixed the bug, the system ran faster."   (the system didn't fix it)
  ✓ "Having fixed the bug, we found the system ran faster."
```

### 3. Noun Clauses

```
  "What matters most is the quality of the training."
  "Whether the policy succeeds depends on funding."
  "That the metric is unreliable has been known for decades."
  "How societies respond to automation will determine its effects."

These make excellent topic sentences, because they front the idea and read
as unmistakably academic.
```

### 4. Cleft Sentences — emphasis with structure

```
  "It is the transition, rather than the technology, that causes hardship."
  "What concerns me most is the pace of the change."
  "It was only after 2010 that usage began to accelerate."
  "The reason this matters is that the cost falls on individuals."
```

### 5. Concessive and Comparative Subordination

```
CONCESSION
  "Although the initial cost is high, the long-term saving is substantial."
  "While remote work suits experienced staff, it disadvantages new recruits."
  "Despite the obvious benefits, adoption has been slow."
  "Even if the total number of jobs holds up, the transition remains painful."
  Note: Despite / In spite of + NOUN or -ING (never a full clause)
    ✗ "Despite it was expensive..."   ✓ "Despite being expensive..."
    ✓ "Despite the expense..."        ✓ "Although it was expensive..."

COMPARISON
  "twice as expensive as" · "three times the figure for" ·
  "significantly more effective than" · "the fewer the resources, the greater
  the inequality" · "not so much a technical problem as a political one"
```

### Sentence Variety in Practice

```
An essay of 300 words should contain roughly:
   4–6 simple sentences        (for emphasis and clarity — these matter!)
   6–8 complex sentences       (with subordination)
   2–3 compound-complex        (two main clauses plus subordination)
   1–2 short punchy sentences  ("The result is predictable.")

The most common Band 6 pattern is fifteen sentences of near-identical length
and structure. Varying length deliberately — a fourteen-word sentence after
a thirty-word one — is read as control, and it is easy to do on purpose.
```

## Punctuation

```
THE COMMA SPLICE — the most common punctuation error in IELTS writing
  ✗ "The cost is high, however it is justified."
  ✓ "The cost is high; however, it is justified."
  ✓ "The cost is high. However, it is justified."
  ✓ "The cost is high, but it is justified."
  Rule: however · therefore · moreover · nevertheless · consequently ·
        furthermore · thus  are ADVERBS, not conjunctions. They cannot join
        two clauses with only a comma.
  Real conjunctions that CAN, with a comma: and · but · so · or · yet · for

SEMICOLONS — join two closely related complete clauses
  ✓ "Task 1 rewards accuracy; Task 2 rewards argument."
  Use one or two per essay. They read as control. Do not overuse.

COLONS — introduce an explanation or a list
  ✓ "The reason is simple: the metric is easy to count."

COMMAS THAT MATTER FOR MEANING
  After an introductory element:
    "In many developing countries, access remains limited."
    "Although the cost is high, the benefit is clear."
  Around non-defining relative clauses:
    "Remote work, which was once rare, is now common."
  NOT before "that":
    ✗ "He argued, that the policy failed."   ✓ "He argued that the policy failed."
  NOT between subject and verb:
    ✗ "The number of people who work remotely, has increased."

APOSTROPHES
  the company's policy (one company) · the companies' policies (several) ·
  its = possessive · it's = it is · never "it's policy"
  Avoid contractions entirely in Writing; they are fine in Speaking.

CAPITALS
  Nationalities and languages: English, Pakistani, Urdu
  Not for general subjects: mathematics, engineering, medicine
```

## Prepositions

The hardest category to rule-learn, so learn them in collocations instead.

```
VERB + PREPOSITION
  depend ON · result IN · result FROM · lead TO · contribute TO · consist OF ·
  benefit FROM · suffer FROM · rely ON · focus ON · invest IN · deal WITH ·
  cope WITH · succeed IN · account FOR · apply FOR (a job) · apply TO (a case) ·
  differ FROM · agree WITH (a person) · agree ON (a plan) · comply WITH ·
  substitute X FOR Y · provide someone WITH something · blame X FOR Y

ADJECTIVE + PREPOSITION
  aware OF · capable OF · responsible FOR · dependent ON · similar TO ·
  different FROM · related TO · relevant TO · essential TO/FOR · beneficial TO ·
  detrimental TO · vulnerable TO · subject TO · consistent WITH · proficient IN

NOUN + PREPOSITION
  an increase IN · a decrease IN · a reason FOR · a solution TO · a cause OF ·
  an effect ON · an impact ON · an advantage OF · a demand FOR · access TO ·
  a lack OF · a decline IN · attitudes TOWARDS · the relationship BETWEEN ·
  a shortage OF · a contribution TO · a rise OF 20% IN the figure

NO PREPOSITION NEEDED (frequent over-correction error)
  ✗ "discuss about"    ✓ "discuss"
  ✗ "emphasise on"     ✓ "emphasise"
  ✗ "affect to"        ✓ "affect"
  ✗ "reach to"         ✓ "reach"
  ✗ "enter into a room" ✓ "enter a room"
  ✗ "explain me"       ✓ "explain to me"

TIME AND PLACE
  IN 2020 · IN March · IN the morning · IN the 1990s
  ON Monday · ON 5 March · ON the weekend (AmE) / AT the weekend (BrE)
  AT 6pm · AT night · AT the moment
  BY 2030 (deadline) · UNTIL 2030 (duration) · FROM 2010 TO 2020
  BETWEEN 2010 AND 2020 · DURING the period · OVER the last decade
```

## Word Formation

Wrong word form is scored as a Lexical Resource error and is extremely common. Learn families, not words.

```
VERB            NOUN (thing/idea)     NOUN (person)     ADJECTIVE        ADVERB
analyse         analysis              analyst           analytical       analytically
develop         development           developer         developmental    —
compete         competition           competitor        competitive      competitively
innovate        innovation            innovator         innovative       innovatively
rely            reliance              —                 reliable/reliant reliably
sustain         sustainability        —                 sustainable      sustainably
economise       economy/economics     economist         economic/economical economically
industrialise   industry              industrialist     industrial/industrious —
signify         significance          —                 significant      significantly
succeed         success               —                 successful       successfully
apply           application           applicant         applicable       —
maintain        maintenance           —                 maintainable     —
argue           argument              —                 arguable         arguably
benefit         benefit               beneficiary       beneficial       beneficially
require         requirement           —                 requisite        —
strengthen      strength              —                 strong           strongly
prove           proof                 —                 proven           —
choose          choice                —                 chosen           —
lose            loss                  loser             lost             —
grow            growth                —                 growing          increasingly

PAIRS THAT ARE COMMONLY CONFUSED
  economic (relating to the economy) vs economical (cheap to run)
  historic (important in history) vs historical (relating to the past)
  effect (noun) vs affect (verb) — "the effect of X" / "X affects Y"
  advice (noun) vs advise (verb)
  practice (noun, BrE) vs practise (verb, BrE)
  rise (intransitive: prices rise) vs raise (transitive: raise prices)
  lie (lie down) vs lay (lay something down)
  number (countable) vs amount (uncountable)
  fewer (countable) vs less (uncountable)
```

## First-Language Transfer Patterns

If your first language is Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, or another South Asian language, a predictable set of patterns transfers into English. None of them reflects ability — they are structural differences between the languages, and they are correctable once named. Recognising your own patterns here is usually worth half a band in both Writing and Speaking.

```
1. DROPPED OR ADDED ARTICLES  (no article system in the source language)
   ✗ "I am software engineer."           ✓ "I am a software engineer."
   ✗ "The technology is changing society." (as a generalisation)
                                          ✓ "Technology is changing society."
   ✗ "I went to the office by the car."  ✓ "...by car."
   → Run the two-minute article check on every piece of writing.

2. PROGRESSIVE WITH STATIVE VERBS
   ✗ "I am having two brothers."          ✓ "I have two brothers."
   ✗ "I am knowing him since 2015."       ✓ "I have known him since 2015."
   ✗ "I am understanding the problem."    ✓ "I understand the problem."
   Statives: have (possess) · know · understand · believe · want · need ·
             like · prefer · own · seem · mean · consist

3. "SINCE" + DURATION, AND PRESENT FOR PRESENT PERFECT
   ✗ "I am working here since three years."
   ✓ "I have been working here for three years."
   ✗ "Since ten years I live in Lahore."
   ✓ "I have lived in Lahore for ten years."

4. PLACEMENT OF "ONLY" AND "ALSO"
   ✗ "I only have completed two tasks."   ✓ "I have completed only two tasks."
   ✗ "I also went there yesterday only."  ✓ "I went there yesterday as well."
   Place "only" immediately before what it limits.

5. UNIVERSAL QUESTION TAG "ISN'T IT?"
   ✗ "You are coming tomorrow, isn't it?" ✓ "...aren't you?"
   ✗ "They finished the work, isn't it?"  ✓ "...didn't they?"
   The tag must match the auxiliary and subject of the main clause.

6. "DISCUSS ABOUT" AND OTHER ADDED PREPOSITIONS
   ✗ discuss about · ✗ emphasise on · ✗ reach to · ✗ return back ·
   ✗ revert back · ✗ enter into (a room) · ✗ order for
   → discuss · emphasise · reach · return · reply · enter · order

7. "DOUBT" AND OTHER SEMANTIC SHIFTS
   ✗ "I have a doubt about question three."  ✓ "I have a question about..."
   ✗ "Kindly do the needful."                ✓ "Please take the necessary action."
   ✗ "I will revert to you."                 ✓ "I will get back to you."
   ✗ "prepone the meeting"                   ✓ "bring the meeting forward"
   ✗ "out of station"                        ✓ "away / out of town"
   ✗ "cousin brother / sister"               ✓ "cousin"
   ✗ "passing out from university"           ✓ "graduating from university"
   ✗ "give me one more chance only"          ✓ "give me one more chance"
   These are perfectly normal in South Asian English but are marked as
   inappropriate word choice in IELTS, which uses international standard usage.

8. WORD ORDER IN INDIRECT QUESTIONS
   ✗ "Can you tell me where is the office?"
   ✓ "Can you tell me where the office is?"
   ✗ "I asked him what is the problem."
   ✓ "I asked him what the problem was."

9. SUBJECT REPETITION AND RESUMPTIVE PRONOUNS
   ✗ "The engineer who fixed the bug, he was very experienced."
   ✓ "The engineer who fixed the bug was very experienced."

10. "MUCH MORE BETTER" / DOUBLE COMPARATIVES
   ✗ "much more better" · ✗ "more easier" · ✗ "most cheapest"
   ✓ "much better" · "far easier" · "the cheapest"

11. FINAL CONSONANT AND -ED/-S DROPPING (Speaking, but scored as grammar too)
   "He work at a bank" heard instead of "He works at a bank"
   "I finish it yesterday" instead of "I finished it yesterday"
   → Deliberately over-articulate final -s and -ed for two weeks. It sounds
     exaggerated to you and normal to the examiner.

12. "THE SAME LIKE" AND OTHER COMPARATIVE PATTERNS
   ✗ "the same like before"    ✓ "the same as before"
   ✗ "different than"          ✓ "different from" (BrE standard)
   ✗ "married with"            ✓ "married to"
```

## The Self-Editing Protocol

```
FOR WRITING — the final three minutes, in this fixed order

  PASS 1 (60 seconds) — ARTICLES AND NOUNS
    Scan for every singular countable noun: does it have a determiner?
    Scan for -s on plurals; scan for -s wrongly added to uncountables.

  PASS 2 (45 seconds) — VERBS
    Every verb: does it agree with its subject?
    Is the tense right and consistent within each paragraph?
    Every past participle in a passive: is it the correct form?

  PASS 3 (45 seconds) — YOUR FIVE-ERROR CARD
    Check only for your own top five personal error types.

  PASS 4 (30 seconds) — PUNCTUATION
    Any comma before "however", "therefore", "moreover" joining two clauses?
    Does every sentence have a subject, a verb, and a full stop?

Do NOT re-read for meaning at this stage. Meaning problems cannot be fixed in
three minutes; mechanical errors can, and they are what the criterion counts.

FOR SPEAKING — you cannot edit, so pre-load instead
  Before the test, decide the specific structures you will deploy:
    one second conditional · one present perfect · one relative clause ·
    one passive hedge · one cleft sentence
  Rehearse each as a template with your own content until it is automatic.
  Self-correct once if you notice an error, then move on. Never explain it.
```

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: Why does grammar matter more than vocabulary for reaching Band 7?**
A: Because the Band 7 grammar descriptor sets a measurable threshold — "frequently produces error-free sentences" — while the vocabulary descriptor asks for sufficient range for flexibility and precision, which most candidates with a professional background already have. In practice, a candidate at 6.5 typically has enough words to discuss education, technology, and work, but produces errors in roughly half their sentences, mostly in a handful of categories: articles, plurals, subject–verb agreement, tense consistency, and prepositions. Those five categories are finite and rule-governed, so they respond to two or three weeks of targeted drilling in a way that vocabulary breadth does not. The efficient sequence is therefore accuracy first, then range of structures, then vocabulary refinement — the opposite of how most candidates prepare.

**Q2: How can I use complex sentences without making more mistakes?**
A: Use a small number of memorised *patterns* rather than improvising complexity. Five structures cover the requirement: a non-defining relative clause, a second or third conditional, a participle clause, a passive hedge such as "It is widely argued that", and a concessive clause with "although" or "while". Rehearse each until you can produce it with any content, and deploy three or four per essay. This gives you genuine range evidence with almost no added error risk, because you are reusing a form you have already got right dozens of times. The failure mode to avoid is the very long sentence with three subordinate clauses, which usually collapses into a run-on or loses its subject; two well-controlled complex sentences score better than one ambitious broken one. It also helps to keep some short simple sentences deliberately — variety of length is itself read as control.

**Q3: What are the most common grammar mistakes made by South Asian English speakers in IELTS?**
A: A predictable set of transfer patterns, none of which reflects ability. Article omission or over-use is the largest, because Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi and Bengali have no article system. Second is the progressive with stative verbs — "I am having two brothers", "I am knowing him since 2015". Third is "since" with a duration plus a present tense, where English requires the present perfect: "I have been working here for three years", not "I am working here since three years". Fourth is the universal question tag "isn't it?" in place of a matching tag. Fifth is added prepositions in "discuss about", "emphasise on", "reach to", and "return back". There is also a set of vocabulary items that are entirely normal in South Asian English but marked as inappropriate in IELTS, such as "do the needful", "revert to you", "prepone", "out of station", and "passing out from university". All of these are correctable in days once named, which is why identifying your own pattern list is worth more than general grammar study.

**Q4: Should I use the passive voice in IELTS Writing?**
A: Yes, selectively. The passive is the default grammar of Task 1 process diagrams — "the bottles are washed, then shredded" — and of map descriptions, where "a car park has been constructed" is the natural form. In Task 2 it earns marks in two ways: as an academic hedge that attributes a view without naming anyone ("It is widely assumed that…"), and as a way of putting the important information first. What costs marks is overuse, since precision is part of Lexical Resource and an entirely passive essay reads as vague, and mechanical errors such as passivising intransitive verbs — "the problem was happened" is impossible because "happen" takes no object. A reasonable target is that a fifth to a quarter of your Task 2 sentences use the passive, deliberately rather than by accident.

**Q5: How do I fix grammar mistakes I cannot even see in my own writing?**
A: Make the checking specific rather than general. First, measure: mark up three of your own essays, or have them marked, and tally errors by category to produce a personal top-five list. Then check only for those five, one category per pass, in the final three minutes of every task — scanning every singular noun for a determiner is a mechanical operation that finds errors re-reading for meaning never will. Second, read your work aloud when practising at home; ear and eye catch different problems, and dropped -s and -ed endings become audible. Third, use the same three-minute protocol every time so it becomes automatic under exam pressure. Finally, get at least one or two pieces marked by a qualified human, because there are error types you will systematically fail to notice by definition — if you could see them, you would not be making them.

## Practical Tasks

### Beginner (10 Tasks)

2. Tally your errors by category and write your personal five-error card.

7. Complete drill Set B and mark it.
8. Write ten sentences using for and since correctly.
9. Copy out the verb+preposition list and write a sentence for fifteen of them.
10. Identify which of the twelve transfer patterns apply to you and mark them on your card.

### Intermediate (10 Tasks)
1. Complete drill Set C and mark it, writing a one-line reason for each correction.
2. Write one paragraph containing all four conditional types, correctly labelled.
3. Rewrite an old essay adding one non-defining relative clause, one participle clause, one passive hedge, and one concessive clause.
4. Complete drill Set D, then compare your versions with the model answers.
5. Write ten sentences using prepositional relative clauses ("the extent to which…").
6. Take one of your essays and eliminate every comma splice.
7. Write five inverted conditionals ("Were…", "Had…") on Task 2 topics.
8. Write a Task 1 process description of an everyday process using only the passive.
9. Record yourself speaking for two minutes, transcribe it, and mark every grammar error.
10. Apply the three-minute self-editing protocol to five consecutive writing tasks and log how many errors each pass catches.

### Advanced (10 Tasks)
1. Re-measure your error-free sentence rate after two weeks of drilling and compare against the baseline.
2. Write five essays in which at least 80% of sentences are error-free, verified by careful self-marking.
3. Take a Band 6 model essay from Chapter 7 and rewrite it to Band 8, annotating every grammatical change.
4. Deliberately deploy all five range structures in a single Speaking Part 3 session, recorded and transcribed.
5. Write ten cleft sentences and ten noun-clause topic sentences on real Task 2 prompts.
6. Build a personal error-pattern log across ten marked tasks and confirm that your top five categories are shrinking.
7. Have two pieces of writing marked by a qualified tutor specifically for Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and compare with your self-assessment.
8. Practise the fourteen-then-thirty-word sentence-variety pattern across one full essay.
9. Transcribe one of your recorded mock Speaking tests in full and calculate the error-free sentence rate for speech.
10. Reduce your five-error card to a three-error card by verifiably eliminating two categories.

## Cheat Sheet

```
THE BAND 7 GRAMMAR BAR
  Range: a variety of complex structures
  Accuracy: FREQUENT error-free sentences (~70%+)
  You need BOTH. Simple-and-clean caps at 6.0; complex-and-broken caps at 6.0.

ARTICLE DECISION PROCEDURE
  plural/uncountable + general        → no article
  plural/uncountable + specific set   → the
  singular countable                  → MUST have a determiner
    first mention / one of many       → a / an
    already known / unique / modified → the
  a vs an by SOUND: a university · an hour · an MBA

NEVER PLURAL
  information · research · advice · knowledge · equipment · software ·
  furniture · traffic · progress · money · training · feedback · evidence ·
  staff · accommodation · behaviour · news (singular verb)

AGREEMENT
  THE number of X HAS ...        A number of X HAVE ...
  Everyone/each/neither HAS ...  There ARE many reasons ...
  Delete the middle phrase to test the subject.

TENSES
  since/for/already/yet/recently/over the last decade → present perfect
  in 2019/last year/ago/when I was                    → past simple
  Task 1 process → present simple passive
  Task 1 map     → present perfect passive
  Task 2 argument → present simple; hypotheticals → conditionals

CONDITIONALS
  0  if + present, present            general truth
  1  if + present, will               real future
  2  if + past, would                 hypothetical now      ← Task 2 workhorse
  3  if + past perfect, would have    hypothetical past
  mixed  if + past perfect, would     past cause, present result
  inverted  Were governments to act... / Had the warning been heeded...

THE FIVE RANGE STRUCTURES (deploy 3+ per essay)
  1 non-defining relative   "X, which was once rare, is now common."
  2 conditional             "If X were cheaper, Y would fall."
  3 participle clause       "Having identified the cause, we rewrote it."
  4 passive hedge           "It is widely argued that..."
  5 concession              "Although the cost is high, ..."
  Bonus: cleft "What matters most is..." / "It is X, not Y, that..."

PUNCTUATION
  however/therefore/moreover = ADVERBS → semicolon or full stop, never a comma
  and/but/so/or/yet = conjunctions → comma is fine
  Despite/In spite of + NOUN or -ING (never a clause)
  Although + full clause, and NO "but" afterwards

NO PREPOSITION
  discuss · emphasise · affect · reach · enter · return · request · answer

SOUTH ASIAN TRANSFER PATTERNS TO WATCH
  dropped articles · "I am having/knowing" · "since three years" ·
  "isn't it?" tag · "discuss about" · "do the needful" · "revert to you" ·
  "prepone" · "passing out from university" · indirect question word order ·
  dropped final -s and -ed

THREE-MINUTE EDIT (fixed order)
  1 articles + plurals   2 subject–verb + tense   3 your five-error card
  4 punctuation (comma splices, full stops)
```
