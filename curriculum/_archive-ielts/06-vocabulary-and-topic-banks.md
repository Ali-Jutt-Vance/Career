# Phase 10 — Chapter 6: Vocabulary and Topic Banks

---

## Chapter Overview

Lexical Resource is 25% of your Writing band and 25% of your Speaking band — half of the two subjective sections. It is also the criterion most commonly *mis-prepared*, because candidates learn long words instead of useful ones.

The descriptors reward three things, in this order:

```
1. PRECISION     the right word for this exact meaning
2. COLLOCATION   words that genuinely go together in English
3. RANGE         enough variety to avoid repetition and to paraphrase
```

Notice what is absent: rarity. "I utilise public transportation on a quotidian basis" is *worse* than "I take the metro most days", because the register is wrong and the collocations are unnatural. Examiners mark unnatural elevation down, and they do it often, because the internet is full of advice telling candidates to swap ordinary words for obscure ones.

This chapter gives you the vocabulary that actually appears in IELTS answers at Band 7–8: twelve topic banks covering the entire Task 2 and Speaking Part 3 question space, with the collocations and hedged claim patterns that make them usable in real sentences. Each bank ends with model sentences so you learn words in context rather than as lists.

**Topics:**
- How Lexical Resource is actually scored, and the three fatal patterns
- The learning system: collocation-first, three-uses rule, spaced review
- Paraphrasing — five techniques, with drills
- Twelve topic banks with collocations, claim patterns, and model sentences
- Words and phrases to eliminate from your English
- Phrasal verbs and idioms for Speaking
- The IELTS spelling list
- A four-week vocabulary schedule

## Beginner Theory

### How Lexical Resource Is Scored

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 6 | Adequate range for the topic; attempts less common vocabulary but with inaccuracy; noticeable errors in word choice, formation and spelling that do not impede communication |
| 7 | Sufficient range to allow flexibility and precision; uses less common and idiomatic items with some awareness of style and collocation; occasional errors in word choice or collocation |
| 8 | Wide resource used fluently and flexibly to convey precise meaning; skilful use of uncommon and idiomatic items; occasional inaccuracies in word choice and collocation only |

Read the Band 7 wording carefully: **"flexibility and precision"** and **"awareness of style and collocation"**. That is not a vocabulary size test. It is a test of whether you can say exactly what you mean, in the right register, with words that belong together.

### The Three Fatal Patterns

```
1. REPETITION OF THE KEY NOUN
   The single clearest Band 6 signal. If the prompt is about "technology",
   a Band 6 essay uses "technology" nine times. A Band 7 essay uses
   "technology", "digital tools", "these systems", "such innovations",
   "they", and "the tools in question".
   → FIX: build a paraphrase set of 4–5 alternatives BEFORE you start writing.

2. THESAURUS MISFIRES
   ✗ "Governments must ameliorate the pecuniary burden on households."
   ✗ "This engenders a plethora of predicaments."
   Words looked up but never seen in use produce wrong collocations and a
   register clash. Examiners read this as inaccuracy, not sophistication.
   → FIX: only use a word you have SEEN in at least two real sentences.

3. INFORMAL REGISTER IN WRITING
   ✗ "lots of people", "kids", "stuff", "a big deal", "gonna", "etc.",
     "nowadays" (overused), "&", contractions
   ✓ "a great many people", "children", "factors", "significant",
     "going to", finish the list, "in recent years", "and"
   → FIX: keep a personal banned-words list; check for it in your final pass.
```

### The Learning System That Works

```
COLLOCATION-FIRST — never learn a word alone
  ✗ "detrimental = harmful"
  ✓ "have a detrimental effect on" · "detrimental to health"
     Words are stored and retrieved in chunks. A word learned alone is a word
     you will use in the wrong collocation.

THE THREE-USES RULE — a word is not learned until you have used it three times
  1. In a written sentence of your own, on a real IELTS topic
  2. Out loud, in a spoken answer, recorded
  3. In a timed task, unprompted
  Recognition is not production. IELTS scores production.

SPACED REVIEW — the schedule that beats re-reading
  Learn 10–15 items today.
  Review: tomorrow · in 3 days · in 7 days · in 21 days.
  Five minutes per review, out loud, producing the collocation not just
  recognising it. Any app with spaced repetition works; paper cards work too.

DEPTH BEFORE BREADTH
  200 items you can produce accurately beat 2,000 you half-recognise.
  Twelve topic banks × 20 items = 240 items. That is enough for Band 8 if
  you can actually produce them.

HARVEST FROM YOUR OWN PRACTICE
  Every Reading passage and Listening transcript you review is a source of
  vocabulary in exactly the register the exam uses. Add three items per
  passage to your list. This beats generic word lists because the words come
  pre-collocated in real sentences.
```

## Paraphrasing — The Core Skill

Paraphrase is where Lexical Resource, Coherence, and Task Response all meet. You need it in the Task 1 introduction, the Task 2 introduction, every Speaking answer, and every Reading question.

### Five Techniques

```
1. SYNONYM SUBSTITUTION — but only where the collocation survives
   "children" → "young people" / "the young" / "school-age children"
   Careful: "big problem" → "large problem" ✗ · "major problem" ✓

2. CHANGE THE WORD CLASS (the most reliable technique)
   "Governments should regulate social media."
   → "Government regulation of social media is necessary."
   → "The regulation of social media by the state is..."
   verb → noun, adjective → noun, active → passive: the meaning is identical
   and the sentence is unmistakably yours.

3. CHANGE THE VOICE
   "Companies collect enormous quantities of personal data."
   → "Enormous quantities of personal data are collected by companies."

4. NEGATE THE OPPOSITE
   "It is important." → "It is far from trivial."
   "Cheap" → "not particularly expensive"
   "Most people agree" → "few would dispute"

5. CHANGE THE STRUCTURE
   "Because cities are expanding, farmland is disappearing."
   → "The expansion of cities has come at the cost of farmland."
   → "As cities expand, farmland disappears."
   → "Urban expansion is eroding agricultural land."
```

### Worked Paraphrase Drill

```
ORIGINAL PROMPT
  "Some people believe that governments should spend money on public
   transport rather than building new roads. To what extent do you agree?"

WEAK PARAPHRASE (too close — copied words are excluded from your count)
  "Some people think that governments should spend money on public transport
   instead of building new roads."

STRONG PARAPHRASE (word class changes + restructure)
  "As urban populations grow, transport budgets are increasingly contested:
   should limited public funds be directed towards buses and rail networks,
   or towards expanding road capacity?"

What changed: "some people believe" → dropped entirely (it adds nothing);
"governments spend money" → "public funds be directed"; "public transport"
→ "buses and rail networks"; "building new roads" → "expanding road
capacity"; and the whole thing was recast as a question, which is a
legitimate and effective opening move.
```

## The Twelve Topic Banks

These twelve topics cover the overwhelming majority of Task 2 prompts and Speaking Part 3 questions. Each bank has the same structure: core nouns, verbs and collocations, hedged claim patterns, and model sentences at Band 7–8.

### Bank 1 — Education

```
NOUNS
  curriculum · syllabus · pedagogy · rote learning · critical thinking ·
  vocational training · tuition fees · academic attainment · dropout rate ·
  class size · streaming (by ability) · standardised testing · literacy ·
  numeracy · lifelong learning · student debt · higher education ·
  extracurricular activities · teacher-pupil ratio · educational disparity

VERBS AND COLLOCATIONS
  acquire skills · foster creativity · instil discipline · broaden horizons ·
  narrow the curriculum · meet the needs of · fall behind · catch up ·
  drop out of school · pursue a degree · enrol in a course · assess progress ·
  place undue emphasis on · teach to the test · equip students with ·
  bridge the attainment gap · subsidise tuition · retain qualified teachers

CLAIM PATTERNS
  "Education is widely regarded as the most reliable route out of poverty."
  "An excessive focus on examinations tends to crowd out deeper learning."
  "Vocational routes are frequently undervalued relative to academic ones."

MODEL SENTENCES
  "A curriculum built around standardised testing rewards recall rather than
   reasoning, and schools respond rationally by teaching to the test."
  "Where tuition fees are high, the decision to pursue a degree becomes an
   economic calculation rather than an intellectual one, which arguably
   distorts subject choice at a national level."
  "Smaller class sizes are consistently popular with parents, though the
   evidence that they improve attainment is more mixed than is often assumed."
```

### Bank 5 — Health and Lifestyle

```
NOUNS
  public health · preventive care · a sedentary lifestyle · obesity ·

  a healthcare system · waiting times · health insurance · a subsidy ·
  a vaccination programme · chronic illness · self-medication · a health screening

  place a strain on health services · fund treatment · promote wellbeing ·

  suffer from insomnia · recover from illness

CLAIM PATTERNS
  "Prevention is generally more cost-effective than treatment."
  "Behaviour change is rarely achieved through information alone."

```
NOUNS
  deterrence · rehabilitation · recidivism · a custodial sentence ·

  cybercrime · fraud · petty crime · organised crime · the judiciary ·

VERBS AND COLLOCATIONS
  commit an offence · deter offenders · impose a sentence · serve a sentence ·
  reoffend · rehabilitate offenders · tackle the root causes of crime ·

CLAIM PATTERNS
  "Longer sentences show weaker deterrent effects than certainty of detection."
  "Crime correlates strongly with deprivation and opportunity."
  "Surveillance measures trade privacy for a security benefit that is hard to

## Precision Upgrades — Better Words That Say More

These are not "fancier synonyms". Each says something more *exact* than the word it replaces, which is what the rubric means by precision.

```
INSTEAD OF "GOOD"        INSTEAD OF "BAD"
  beneficial               detrimental
  effective                counterproductive
  advantageous             damaging
  sound (a sound argument) flawed
  compelling               unconvincing
  substantial              negligible

INSTEAD OF "BIG"         INSTEAD OF "IMPORTANT"
  substantial              crucial
  considerable             pivotal
  significant              central
  marked (a marked rise)   consequential
  acute (an acute problem) fundamental

INSTEAD OF "PROBLEM"     INSTEAD OF "MANY PEOPLE THINK"
  issue                    it is widely held that
  obstacle                 there is a broad consensus that
  drawback                 few would dispute that
  shortcoming              the prevailing view is that
  constraint               commentators frequently argue that
  bottleneck               (never: "many people think")

INSTEAD OF "SHOWS"       INSTEAD OF "CAUSES"
  illustrates              gives rise to
  indicates                results in
  demonstrates             contributes to
  reveals                  triggers
  suggests (weaker claim)  is a driver of
  highlights               leads to

INSTEAD OF "VERY"        INSTEAD OF "A LOT OF"
  particularly             a great deal of (uncountable)
  markedly                 a considerable number of (countable)
  strikingly               a substantial proportion of
  exceptionally            the vast majority of
  (or use a stronger adjective and delete the adverb entirely)
```

## Words and Phrases to Eliminate

```
BANNED IN WRITING — informal register
  lots of · a lot of (borderline; prefer "a great deal of") · kids · stuff ·
  things · guys · gonna · wanna · big · bad · nice · get (prefer obtain/receive) ·
  etc. · & · sort of · kind of · a bit · pretty (as in "pretty good")

BANNED — IELTS clichés examiners hear all day
  "In this modern era of globalisation and technology..."
  "Since the dawn of civilisation..."
  "It is a burning issue nowadays."
  "Every coin has two sides."
  "Last but not least..."
  "As we all know..."
  "This essay will discuss both sides and then give my opinion."
  "In a nutshell..."

BANNED — overclaiming without evidence
  "Studies have proven that 78%..."   (invented statistics fool nobody)
  "It is a fact that..."              (usually it isn't)
  "Everyone knows that..."
  "always" / "never" / "all people"   (hedge instead)

USE SPARINGLY — not wrong, just exhausted
  nowadays (once per essay maximum; "in recent years" is better)
  Moreover / Furthermore (vary them; twice each maximum)
  I think (prefer "I would argue" / "in my view")
```

## Phrasal Verbs and Idioms for Speaking

Phrasal verbs are *informal-neutral* — excellent in Speaking, acceptable in GT informal letters, and best avoided in formal Task 2.

```
PHRASAL VERBS FOR SPEAKING
  pick up (a skill) · take up (a hobby) · get into (a field) ·
  end up (doing) · come across (find by chance) · figure out · work out ·
  put up with (tolerate) · get on with (a person / a task) ·
  look into (investigate) · come up with (an idea) · take on (a task) ·
  catch up on (sleep, work) · cut down on (sugar, spending) ·
  keep up with (developments) · fall behind · turn out (to be) ·
  set aside (time) · run into (a problem) · get round to (finally doing)

MODEL USE
  "I got into programming almost by accident, and I ended up enjoying it far
   more than the subject I actually studied."
  "I try to set aside an hour in the evening to keep up with what's happening
   in the field, though I don't always get round to it."

IDIOMS — safe, genuinely common, useful in Part 3
  a double-edged sword · a mixed blessing · the tip of the iceberg ·
  a step in the right direction · a grey area · a false economy ·
  easier said than done · in the long run · at the cutting edge ·
  keep up with the times · a steep learning curve · food for thought ·
  the elephant in the room · bear the brunt of · a drop in the ocean

RULE: two idioms per Speaking test, maximum, and only where they fit
naturally. A forced idiom costs more than a missing one. Never use an idiom
you have not heard a real person say.
```

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: Is it better to use difficult words or simple words in IELTS?**
A: Precise words, which are frequently neither. The Lexical Resource descriptors reward flexibility, precision, and awareness of collocation and style — not rarity. "I take the metro most days" is a better sentence than "I utilise public transportation on a quotidian basis", because the second has a register clash and unnatural collocation, and examiners mark both as inaccuracy. Where an uncommon word genuinely says something more exact — "a marked rise" rather than "a big rise", "detrimental to health" rather than "bad for health" — it earns credit. The safe rule is to use a word only if you have seen it in at least two real sentences and know what it collocates with. A vocabulary of 250 items you can deploy accurately outperforms 2,000 you half-recognise, because the exam scores production, not recognition.

**Q2: How many words a day should I learn, and how should I learn them?**
A: Ten to fifteen items a day, where an "item" is a collocation rather than a single word — "have a detrimental effect on" rather than "detrimental". Learn them in topic clusters, because IELTS questions are organised by topic and clustered vocabulary is retrieved together under pressure. Then apply the three-uses rule: an item is not learned until you have written it in a sentence of your own, said it out loud in a recorded answer, and produced it unprompted in a timed task. Review on a spaced schedule — tomorrow, in three days, in a week, in three weeks — for about five minutes each time, always producing rather than merely reading. Fifteen items a day for four weeks is roughly 400 items, which is more than enough for Band 8 if you can actually produce them.

**Q3: How do I stop repeating the same words in my essays?**
A: Build a paraphrase set before you start writing. Take the prompt's key nouns and write four or five alternatives in the margin during your planning minutes — for "technology": digital tools, these systems, such innovations, the tools in question. Then use referencing to carry the idea forward without naming it again: this, these, such, the latter, doing so. Changing word class is the most reliable technique, because it never risks the wrong synonym: "governments should regulate social media" becomes "government regulation of social media". The habit to break is reaching for a thesaurus mid-sentence, which produces the wrong collocation; the habit to build is deciding your paraphrase set in advance, when you have time to check that it sounds right.

**Q4: Should I memorise idioms and phrasal verbs?**
A: A small number, and use them in the right section. Phrasal verbs are excellent in Speaking and in informal General Training letters, where they sound natural — "I picked up the skill", "I ended up specialising in it" — but they are too informal for most of a Task 2 essay. Idioms are explicitly credited in the Speaking descriptors at Band 7 and above, but the credit is for using them *naturally*, and a forced idiom is more damaging than a missing one. Learn about fifteen genuinely common ones, use at most two in a Speaking test, and never use one you have not heard a real person say. Avoid the textbook idioms that IELTS candidates recite — "every coin has two sides", "it's raining cats and dogs" — because examiners hear them constantly and they read as memorised rather than as evidence of range.

**Q5: Do I need the Academic Word List?**
A: It is useful but secondary. The AWL's 570 word families cover the register of academic Reading passages well, so working through it improves Reading comprehension and gives you a formal vocabulary for Task 2. However, the higher-return activity is harvesting from your own practice material: every Reading passage and Listening transcript you review supplies words already embedded in the exact register and collocations the exam uses, and every question you got wrong supplies a paraphrase pair worth logging. Two hundred items collected from your own practice, each with a collocation and three uses, will do more for your Writing and Speaking than the whole AWL memorised passively. Use the AWL as a checklist for gaps, not as your primary method.

## Practical Tasks

### Beginner (10 Tasks)
1. Build your personal banned-words list and check one old essay against it.
2. Complete the ten paraphrase drills and compare with the model answers.
3. Learn Bank 1 and Bank 2 (30 items) using the three-uses rule.
4. Rewrite one old essay replacing every instance of good, bad, big, important, problem, and shows with a precise alternative.
5. Write fifteen sentences using collocations from the Work and Employment bank.
6. Do a 30-word spelling test from the IELTS spelling list.
7. Write out five word families from Chapter 5 and use each form in a sentence.
8. Build paraphrase sets of five alternatives for ten common Task 2 key nouns.
9. Record a two-minute Speaking answer using six phrasal verbs naturally.
10. Set up a spaced-review system (app or cards) and enter your first 30 items.

### Intermediate (10 Tasks)
1. Complete all twelve topic banks over two weeks, with three uses per item.
2. Write one Task 2 essay per bank pair — six essays across six topics.
3. For each of the twelve banks, prepare one claim, one example from your own field, and one counterargument.
4. Harvest three vocabulary items with collocations from every Reading passage you practise, for two weeks.
5. Log a paraphrase pair for every Reading answer across three full papers.
6. Record twelve Speaking Part 3 answers, one per bank, using at least six bank items each.
7. Audit one of your essays for key-noun repetition and rewrite it using referencing.
8. Take five sentences you wrote with thesaurus words and check each collocation in a dictionary; fix what does not collocate.
9. Do the full spelling list as a dictation test.
10. Write one essay in which you deliberately use no adverb of degree ("very", "really", "extremely") at all.

### Advanced (10 Tasks)
1. Reach 250 items with three verified uses each, and confirm with a self-test that you can produce every one.
2. Write one Band 8 essay per topic bank — twelve essays — and check each for precision rather than rarity.
3. Have two essays marked specifically for Lexical Resource, and investigate every collocation the marker flags.
4. Take one of your own Band 6 essays and rewrite it at Band 8, annotating which changes were lexical and which were grammatical.
5. Build a personal collocation dictionary of 100 entries from your own harvested material.
6. Do a register audit: check one essay for every informal item and one Speaking transcript for every over-formal item.
7. Practise a Speaking test in which you deploy exactly two idioms, both natural, and have a listener guess which ones were deliberate.
8. Prepare a one-page "topic sheet" per bank containing five items, one claim pattern, one example, and one counterargument, for final revision.
9. Sit a full Writing paper and afterwards count the distinct content words you used; compare against an earlier paper.
10. Take twenty AWL families you do not use and integrate five of them into essays with correct collocation.
