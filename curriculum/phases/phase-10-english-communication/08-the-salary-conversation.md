# Phase 10 — Chapter 8: The Salary Conversation

> *"The words are the easy part. Staying quiet after them is the skill."*

---

## Chapter Overview

Phase 11 Chapter 6 covers the strategy of negotiation — the numbers, the anchoring, the offer letter. This chapter covers the *delivery*: the exact language, the tone, and the small verbal habits that give away money without you noticing.

It is in the communication phase deliberately, because negotiation failure is almost never a failure of strategy. Most people know they should ask for more. They fail at the point of speech — the number comes out lower than intended, or an apology is attached to it, or the silence afterwards gets filled.

Read this alongside Phase 11 Chapter 6, and rehearse the sentences aloud until they are boring.

## The four verbal habits that cost money

**1. Hedging the number.** *"I was thinking maybe around 280 or so, but I'm flexible."* Everything after "280" reduced it. The interviewer now hears 250 as your real position.

Say the number plainly: *"I'm looking at 280 to 320."* Full stop.

**2. Justifying before you are challenged.** *"I know that's a lot given my current salary, but…"* You have introduced the objection yourself and argued against your own position before anyone disagreed. State the number and let them respond.

**3. Filling the silence.** The most expensive habit. You name a number, there is a pause, and the discomfort makes you add *"but I understand if that's not possible"*. You have just negotiated against yourself in four seconds.

**The silence is the technique.** Say the number and wait, however long it takes. The pause is them thinking, which is exactly what you want.

**4. Apologising.** *"Sorry to ask, but…"* Discussing compensation is a normal part of a professional transaction. Nothing about it requires an apology, and apologising signals that you do not believe your own number.

## The sentences

Rehearse each aloud until it is dull. Under pressure you fall back on what is rehearsed.

### When they ask your expectation

> *"Based on the role and what I've researched for this level in Lahore, I'm looking at 280,000 to 320,000. Is that inside your band?"*

Number, reasoning, question back. Ending on a question moves the conversation forward and stops you from filling the pause.

### When they ask your current salary

> *"I'd rather not anchor on my current number — my current role is at a small company and it isn't representative of the market for this work. My expectation for this role is 280,000 to 320,000."*

Not a refusal, an explanation and a redirection. Most recruiters accept it without pressing.

### When they insist

> *"I understand. I'm not comfortable sharing it, but I'm happy to be completely transparent about my expectation, which is 280,000 to 320,000."*

Calm, not defensive, said once. Do not escalate the tone.

### When you receive an offer

> *"Thank you — I'm genuinely pleased, and I'm very interested in the role. Could I take a day to look at the whole package and come back to you tomorrow?"*

Never accept in the same conversation. Nobody has ever lost an offer to that sentence.

### The counter

> *"Thank you again for the offer, I'm excited about the team. I was hoping we could get to 300,000. I'm bringing three years of production experience plus two applications I've built and deployed end to end on Node, React, PostgreSQL and AWS. Is there room to move?"*

Then **stop talking**.

### When they say it is above their range

> *"I understand. What is the range for this level? And is there a level where that number would fit?"*

Two questions, no concession. Sometimes the answer is that you were being considered at the wrong level, and asking is the only way to discover it.

### When the base is genuinely fixed

> *"I understand the band is fixed. Is there flexibility on the title, or could we put a review at six months in writing?"*

### Declining

> *"Thank you very much for the offer and for the time everyone spent. I've decided to go in a different direction, but I really appreciated the conversations and I'd be glad to stay in touch."*

## Tone

**Warm, not adversarial.** This is not a confrontation. You want to work with these people and they want you to. The tone is closer to two people solving a problem than two people on opposite sides of one.

**Certain, not aggressive.** The difference is in the qualifiers, not the volume. "I'm looking at 280 to 320" is certain. "I really need at least 280" is aggressive and slightly desperate. "I was hoping for maybe 280" is neither.

**Grateful without being deferential.** Thank them, genuinely, once. Then talk about the number as a business matter, which is what it is.

**Slow.** Nervousness makes people speak quickly, and a number said quickly sounds negotiable. Say it slightly slower than feels natural.

## Rehearsing it

**Say each sentence aloud ten times.** Not read — said, standing up, as though to a person. They must be automatic, because under adrenaline you will not construct language well.

**Practise the silence with a timer.** Say your counter, start a ten-second timer, and stay completely quiet until it ends. Ten seconds of silence feels enormous. Do it until it does not.

**Record yourself.** Listen for hedges, apologies, and the sentence you added after the number. Delete each one and record again.

**Rehearse five openings**, because you do not control which one arrives:
- They ask your expectation first
- They ask your current salary first
- They name a number first
- They name a number well below your target
- They push for a decision on the call

## When it feels wrong to ask

This is the real obstacle and it deserves naming.

You are on 85,000. Someone offers 230,000. Every instinct says take it immediately, say thank you, and do not risk a good thing over greed.

Three things are worth knowing.

**Asking does not risk the offer.** A single polite counter has essentially never caused a written offer to be withdrawn. Companies expect it; many leave room specifically for it. The downside is that they say no.

**The gap compounds.** The difference between 230,000 and 280,000 is 600,000 rupees in the first year alone, and it also sets the base for your next raise and the anchor for your next job. One twenty-minute conversation changes several years.

**They are not doing you a favour.** They have a role that needs filling, they interviewed a number of people, and they chose you because you were the best available. That is a transaction between two parties, and both parties are allowed to state their position.

If it helps, reframe it: you are not asking for a favour. You are telling them what it takes, politely, once, and then letting them decide.

## Self Assessment

- Can I say my number without a hedge, an apology, or a sentence after it?
- Can I stay silent for ten seconds after naming it? Time yourself.
- Have I rehearsed all five openings, or only the one I expect?
- Do I know my walk-away number, and would I actually walk?
- When I last said a number aloud, did I speak faster than normal?

## Cheat Sheet

- **Four habits that cost money:** hedging the number · justifying before challenged · **filling the silence** · apologising.
- **Say the number, then stop.** The silence is the technique.
- **Expectation:** *"Based on the role and my research for this level, I'm looking at X to Y. Is that inside your band?"*
- **Current salary:** *"I'd rather not anchor on that — it isn't representative of the market. My expectation is X to Y."*
- **On receiving an offer:** *"Could I take a day and come back to you tomorrow?"* Never accept in the conversation.
- **Counter once,** with one reason, then silence.
- **Above their range?** *"What is the range for this level, and is there a level where that number fits?"*
- **Tone: warm, certain, grateful, slow.** Speaking quickly makes a number sound negotiable.
- **Rehearse the silence with a ten-second timer** until it stops feeling long.
- **A polite counter does not lose written offers.** The downside is that they say no.
