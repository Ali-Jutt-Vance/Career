# Phase 10 — Chapter 5: Small Talk and the First Five Minutes

> *"The decision is not made in the first five minutes. But the lens through which everything after it is read, is."*

---

## Chapter Overview

Interviews do not start with the first technical question. They start with two or three minutes of nothing in particular — how are you, did you find the office, how has your week been — and then the introduction, and only then the substance.

Those first five minutes are the most rehearsable part of the whole process and the part most candidates never practise, because it feels trivial. It is not trivial. It sets a frame. An interviewer who forms an early impression of a nervous, uncertain candidate reads a hesitant technical answer as confirmation; an interviewer who forms an impression of a competent, relaxed one reads the same hesitation as thoughtfulness.

## Why it is hard, specifically

Small talk in a second language is disproportionately difficult, and the reason is worth understanding: it is unstructured. Technical questions have a shape you can prepare. "So, how's your week been?" has no shape, arrives without warning, and is the moment your English is least warmed up.

There is also a cultural component. Direct, casual social exchange with someone senior is not the default register in many Pakistani workplaces, so it can feel presumptuous to respond casually to a director asking how your weekend was. In an interview with a Tier 2 company — particularly one working with foreign clients — a warm, brief, casual answer is exactly what is expected, and a stiff formal one reads as distance.

The fix is the same as for everything else in this phase: prepare the unstructured part so it stops being unstructured.

## The opening exchange

Four or five things get asked. Prepare a two-sentence answer to each. Not scripts — just enough that you are not generating language from nothing while nervous.

**"How are you?"** — *"I'm good, thanks — a bit of a busy week, but good. How about you?"* Return the question. This is the single most useful habit in the whole exchange, because it turns a test into a conversation.

**"How was your weekend?" / "How's your week going?"** — Have one small real detail ready. *"Good — I finally got the deployment pipeline working on a side project I've been building, so that felt like a win."* One detail, and it happens to open a technical door if they want it.

**"Did you find us okay?" / "How's the connection?"** — *"All good, thanks."* Do not elaborate. This one is a formality.

**"Tell me a bit about yourself before we start."** — The ninety-second introduction from Chapter 1. This is the real opening and it should be automatic.

**"Have you been busy with interviews?"** — *"A few conversations going on, yes."* Neither desperate nor boastful.

## Answering questions back

The most common mistake is answering and stopping. "How are you?" "Fine." Then silence, and the interviewer has to restart the conversation, which is work.

**Two sentences and a return question** is the whole formula. It costs nothing and it changes the register from interrogation to conversation.

> *"Good, thanks — busy week at work but I've had time to keep building in the evenings. How about you, how's your week been?"*

You will feel that asking a question back is presumptuous. It is not. It is standard, it is expected, and interviewers find it easier and more pleasant.

## Warming up before the call

Your English at the start of a call is measurably worse than your English fifteen minutes in, and interviews are often over before you warm up. This is entirely fixable.

**Speak English aloud for ten minutes before the call.** Read something aloud, explain your project to an empty room, deliver your introduction twice. Arrive already warm.

**Have the introduction fully automatic.** If the first substantial thing you say is fluent, the frame is set favourably before any hard question arrives.

**Do the practical checks fifteen minutes early:** audio, camera, lighting so your face is not in shadow, water within reach, the job advert and your notes open, notifications off, a quiet room, and a phone number in case the connection fails.

Being ten minutes early and warmed up removes the low-grade panic that otherwise occupies the first five minutes.

## Reading the register

Interviewers vary. Match them rather than applying one style to everyone.

**The formal interviewer** — straight into questions, minimal small talk. Match it. Do not force warmth; be efficient and precise.

**The casual interviewer** — chatty, tangents, personal. Match that too. Being stiff with a casual interviewer reads as difficult to work with, which is a real scoring criterion.

**The rushed interviewer** — clearly between meetings. Be concise, get to the point, do not fill silence.

Matching register is a social skill that engineers are frequently assumed not to have, and demonstrating it quietly does you a lot of good.

## Closing the conversation

The last two minutes matter more than the middle, because of how memory works — people recall openings and endings.

**Ask your three questions.** Chapter 3 covers them. Ask at least two.

**Ask about next steps.** *"What are the next steps, and when might I hear back?"* Practical, expected, and it gives you a date for your follow-up.

**Close warmly and specifically.** *"Thanks — this was genuinely interesting, especially the part about how you handle the migrations. I'd like to be considered."* Saying you want the job is not desperate. It is clear, and candidates omit it far more often than they should.

## When something goes wrong

**You cannot understand their accent.** Ask. *"Sorry, could you repeat that?"* Once is completely normal. If it continues, be direct: *"Would you mind speaking a little slower? I want to make sure I answer the right question."* Nobody minds, and answering the wrong question is far worse.

**You mishear a question.** Restate it before answering: *"So you're asking how I'd handle the concurrent updates?"* This catches the error in three seconds instead of three minutes.

**The connection drops.** Rejoin immediately, apologise once briefly, carry on. It happens constantly and nobody scores it.

**You go blank on your own introduction.** Slow down and start again. *"Let me start that again."* Perfectly acceptable, and far better than pushing on through a disintegrating answer.

## Self Assessment

- Do I have a two-sentence answer ready for "how's your week been", with one real detail?
- Do I return the question, or answer and stop?
- Do I speak English aloud for ten minutes before a call?
- Can I match a casual interviewer without being stiff?
- Do I say, at the end, that I want the job?

## Cheat Sheet

- **The first five minutes set the lens** through which everything after is read.
- **Prepare the unstructured part.** Two sentences ready for each of the four standard openers.
- **Two sentences and a return question.** Turns an interrogation into a conversation.
- **Speak English aloud for ten minutes before the call.** Your first five minutes are otherwise your worst five.
- **Be ready fifteen minutes early:** audio, camera, lighting, water, advert open, notifications off, backup phone number.
- **Match the interviewer's register** — formal, casual or rushed. Mismatching reads as difficult.
- **Ask "what are the next steps and when will I hear?"** It gives you a follow-up date.
- **Say that you want the job.** Clear, not desperate, and usually omitted.
- **Ask them to repeat or slow down** when needed. Answering the wrong question is much worse.
