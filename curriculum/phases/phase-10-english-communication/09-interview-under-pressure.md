# Phase 10 — Chapter 9: Interview Under Pressure

> *"Everyone's mind goes blank. The difference is that some people have rehearsed what to do in the next ten seconds."*

---

## Chapter Overview

You will, at some point, be asked something you know the answer to and find nothing there. It is a physical event rather than an intellectual one — adrenaline narrows working memory, and working memory is where you retrieve things from.

This is worth preparing for specifically, because for you the stakes of that moment are higher than average. Three years of reaching for help within one second of friction means you have very little practice at pushing through the blank. The reflex is to escape, and in an interview there is nowhere to escape to, so the blankness compounds into panic.

The fix is not to be less nervous. It is to have a rehearsed procedure for the ten seconds after the blank, so that the moment has a script instead of a void.

## What is actually happening

When you are stressed, your body prepares for physical threat. Heart rate up, breathing shallow, attention narrowed. Working memory — the thing holding the problem, the approach and the language you are speaking — is the first thing to shrink.

Two useful consequences follow.

**It is temporary and it is physical.** It typically passes in thirty to ninety seconds if you do not feed it. It says nothing about your ability, and treating it as evidence about yourself is what turns a bad thirty seconds into a bad interview.

**It is worse in a second language.** Speaking English costs working memory that a native speaker does not spend. Under stress there is less to spend, which is why your English gets noticeably worse at exactly the moment you need it. This is normal, it is not a reflection of your English, and interviewers who work with foreign clients see it constantly.

## The ten-second procedure

Rehearse this until it is automatic. When the blank arrives, do these four things in order.

**1. Say something that buys time.** Out loud, immediately, so the silence does not begin:

> *"Let me think about that for a moment."*

That sentence is completely acceptable, it is used by senior engineers constantly, and it converts dead air into visible deliberation.

**2. Breathe out slowly, once.** Long exhale. It is the fastest available physiological brake and it takes three seconds.

**3. Say what you do know, out loud.** Not silently — out loud. Speech retrieves memory in a way that internal searching does not, and the interviewer hears reasoning rather than nothing.

> *"Right — this is about transaction isolation. I know read committed is the Postgres default, and I know serialisable is the strictest. What I'm reaching for is which one stops phantom reads."*

Nine times out of ten the answer arrives while you are saying this.

**4. If it does not arrive, name the gap and move.** Chapter 6 covers the structure. Do not sit in it.

## Recovering mid-answer

Sometimes it goes wrong after you have started.

**You lose the thread.** Say so and restart cleanly. *"Sorry, I've lost my thread — let me start that again."* Then restate the question and answer it in one shape. This is far better than trailing off into a sentence you cannot finish.

**You realise you are rambling.** Stop and summarise. *"Let me be more concise — the short answer is X."* Interviewers are relieved when a candidate does this.

**You answered the wrong question.** Say it. *"I think I answered a different question — were you asking about Y?"* Noticing this yourself is a positive signal.

**You said something wrong.** Correct it immediately and move on. *"Actually that's not right — let me redo that."* Self-correction is scored well.

## Recovering between questions

Once a round has gone badly, the risk is that the rest of it goes badly too because your confidence has gone. This is where most interviews are actually lost — not on the hard question, but on the four easy ones after it.

**Reset deliberately.** One breath, and one sentence to yourself: *that question is finished.* Then treat the next one as the first question of the interview.

**Do not audit while you are still in the room.** Replaying the last answer while the next question is being asked guarantees you handle the next one worse. The review happens afterwards, in writing, when it is useful.

**Assume it cost you less than you think.** Candidates consistently overestimate the damage of one weak answer. Interviewers are scoring across a whole conversation and they expect gaps. This assumption is usually accurate and it is always more useful than the alternative.

## Reducing the pressure in advance

**Do mock interviews with real people.** This is the single most effective intervention available and it is why the plan schedules five of them. The nervousness is largely a novelty response, and novelty is removed by repetition. A mock with a friend is not as sharp as the real thing and it still removes most of the effect.

**Interview at companies you do not care about first.** Deliberately schedule two or three lower-priority interviews before the ones you want. Live rounds are worth more than any amount of solo practice, and the cost of a bad one is nothing.

**Rehearse the openings.** Most of the adrenaline is concentrated in the first five minutes. If your introduction is automatic, you get through that window on rehearsal rather than improvisation, and you are noticeably calmer by the time the hard questions arrive.

**Warm up before the call.** Ten minutes speaking English aloud. Your first five minutes are otherwise your worst five.

**Sleep.** A poor night before an interview costs more than an evening of extra preparation gains. If it is a choice, sleep.

## The specific fear about being found out

Worth addressing directly, because it will be present in your early interviews and it changes how you behave.

You are carrying a belief that at some point in the conversation they will discover that you do not really know things, and that the interview is a process of exposure. That belief produces defensiveness, over-claiming, and a reluctance to say "I have not done that" — which are precisely the behaviours that damage an interview.

Two corrections.

**They are not trying to expose you.** They are trying to work out what you can do, so they can decide whether it matches what they need. A gap found is information, not a verdict. Interviewers ask until they find the limit of every candidate, including strong ones.

**By December you will not be bluffing about anything.** You will have built two applications, deployed them, measured them, and be able to discuss any part at depth. There is nothing to be found out. The fear is a residue of the position you are in now, not a description of the position you will be in when it matters — and noticing that difference is the point of doing the work first and interviewing after.

## Self Assessment

- Do I have a rehearsed sentence for the moment my mind goes blank, or would I go silent?
- Have I done a mock interview with a real person, or only practised alone?
- After a bad answer, do I reset, or do I replay it during the next question?
- Have I scheduled any low-stakes interviews before the ones I actually want?
- Am I going into interviews claiming things I could not defend? If so, remove them from the CV.

## Cheat Sheet

- **Going blank is physical, temporary, and worse in a second language.** It is not evidence about your ability.
- **The ten-second procedure:** say *"Let me think about that for a moment"* · breathe out once · **say what you do know, out loud** · name the gap and move.
- **Speaking retrieves memory. Silent searching does not.**
- **Mid-answer recovery:** *"Let me start that again."* · *"Let me be more concise."* · *"I think I answered a different question."* · *"Actually, let me correct that."*
- **Most interviews are lost on the four easy questions after the hard one.** Reset deliberately; do not audit in the room.
- **Mock interviews with real people are the highest-return preparation.** Nervousness is largely a novelty response.
- **Interview at low-priority companies first.** Live rounds beat solo practice.
- **Sleep beats an extra evening of preparation.**
- **They are not trying to expose you** — and by December there will be nothing to expose.
