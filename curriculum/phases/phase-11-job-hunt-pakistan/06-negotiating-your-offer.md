# Phase 11 — Chapter 6: Negotiating Your Offer

> *"The single most expensive sentence in your career is the one where you name your current salary first."*

---

## Chapter Overview

This chapter is worth more per hour of reading than anything else in the book. A negotiation lasts perhaps twenty minutes and routinely moves the outcome by thirty to sixty thousand rupees a month — which is more than the difference many people spend a year of study trying to earn, and it compounds, because your next salary will be anchored on this one.

It is also the chapter you are most likely to handle badly, for a specific reason: you are on 85,000, and the market you are entering pays three to five times that. When someone offers you 220,000 your instinct will be relief and immediate acceptance. That instinct will cost you.

Read this before your first screening call, not before your first offer. The most consequential moment in the negotiation happens twenty minutes into the very first conversation, weeks before any offer exists.

## The current salary problem

In Pakistan you will be asked your current salary, directly, often on the application form and almost always in the first call. This is a genuine structural disadvantage when your current salary is far below the band you are targeting, and it must be handled deliberately.

**Why it matters so much.** Salary offers are anchored. If a company learns you earn 85,000, an offer of 150,000 looks generous to them and feels enormous to you, and the conversation will settle somewhere near that — even at a company whose band for the role is 250,000 to 350,000. The number you say first sets the frame for everything after it.

**The tactic: answer a different question.** Do not lie — lying about salary is checkable through payslips at some companies and it destroys the offer if found. Instead, redirect to your expectation, which is the question that actually matters.

> **"I'd rather not anchor on my current number — my current role is at a small company and it isn't representative of the market. Based on the role and what I've researched for this level in Lahore, I'm looking at 280,000 to 320,000. Is that inside your band?"**

That answer does four things: declines without refusing, explains why in a way that is true and understandable, names your number, and hands the question back to them.

**When they insist.** Some recruiters will press, and some application forms will not submit without a number. Options, in order of preference:

- *"I'm not comfortable sharing that, but I'm happy to be completely transparent about my expectation, which is 280,000 to 320,000."* Most will accept this.
- If a form requires a number, enter your **expectation**, and clarify in the first call that the figure was your expected salary.
- If they absolutely require the current figure to proceed, give it, and immediately follow with the frame: *"That's 85,000, which reflects a small company rather than the market for this work. My expectation for this role is 280,000 to 320,000, based on the level and the stack."*

Even in the worst case, immediately reframing limits the damage. The recruiter now has two numbers rather than one, and the second is the one attached to your reasoning.

## Doing the research first

You cannot name a number you have not worked out. Do this in week 14, before offers arrive.

**Sources, in order of reliability:**

- **What recruiters tell you.** Ask every internal recruiter for the band in the first call. Many answer. This is the best data available to you and it is free.
- **Your referral contacts.** Someone inside the company can often tell you approximately what the level pays. Ask indirectly: "roughly what should I be expecting for this level there?"
- **Your own interview history.** By week 14 you will have had several conversations. What have people quoted?
- **Aggregate sites** — use them for shape rather than precision; the Pakistani data on most of them is thin and dated.

**Then write three numbers, before any conversation:**

| Number | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Walk-away** | Below this you decline and keep looking. For you, realistically around 180,000 — a doubling, and worth taking from a Tier 3 company if it is the only offer. |
| **Target** | What you expect to land. 250,000–300,000 at Tier 2. |
| **Ambitious** | What you ask for first. 300,000–350,000. |

You ask for the ambitious number. You expect to land near the target. You decline below the walk-away. Having these written down before the conversation is what stops you from improvising under pressure, which is where money is lost.

## How the conversation actually goes

**They name a number.** Whatever it is, do not accept in the same conversation. This is the single most important mechanical rule in the chapter.

> **"Thank you — I'm genuinely pleased, and I'm very interested in the role. Could I take a day to look at the whole package properly and come back to you tomorrow?"**

Nobody has ever lost an offer to that sentence. It is completely normal and professional. What it buys you is a conversation where you are not making decisions under adrenaline.

**The counter.** The next day, in writing or on a call. Structure: appreciation, then a specific number, then a reason, then silence.

> *"Thank you again for the offer, I'm excited about the team. I was hoping we could get to 300,000. I'm bringing three years of production experience, plus two applications I've built and deployed end to end on Node, React, PostgreSQL and AWS — including the performance and infrastructure work we discussed. Is there room to move?"*

**Then stop talking.** This is the technique, and it is the hard part. The silence after you name a number is uncomfortable and it is where the negotiation is won. Most people fill it — "but I understand if that's not possible", "I'm flexible" — and every one of those words gives back what the number just claimed. Say your sentence and wait, however long it takes.

**Expect a counter to your counter.** They will often come back with something between. That is a successful negotiation.

**Ask once. Accept graciously.** A single counter is expected and professional. A second round of haggling starts to cost you goodwill, and goodwill with your future manager is worth more than the last ten thousand.

## What else is negotiable

When the base is genuinely fixed — and sometimes it is, because bands exist — these are often more flexible:

- **Job title.** Costs the company nothing and changes what you can apply for next. "Senior" or "Software Engineer II" on your next CV is worth real money.
- **Joining bonus.** Often available from a different budget than salary.
- **Notice-period buyout** if you have a long notice and they want you sooner.
- **A written salary review at six months** with a stated figure. Get this in the offer letter, not as a verbal assurance — a verbal promise from a manager who leaves in April does not exist.
- **Annual leave, remote or hybrid days, equipment budget, training and certification budget.**
- **Start date** — useful if you want time between jobs.

Asking about these after a base-salary refusal is a good move: *"I understand the band is fixed. Is there flexibility on the title, or a review at six months?"*

## Reading the offer letter properly

Read every line before signing. Specifically:

**Gross versus net.** Pakistani offers are usually quoted gross. Know your net figure before you compare offers, and check whether the stated number includes allowances that are conditional.

**The structure.** Basic salary plus allowances. Some benefits — gratuity, provident fund, bonuses — are calculated on the basic component only, so two offers with the same total can differ materially.

**Provident fund and gratuity.** Whether the company contributes, and at what rate. This is real compensation.

**Bonus.** Guaranteed or discretionary? "Up to three months" that is entirely discretionary is not three months.

**Notice period on your side.** Two or three months in an offer letter constrains your next move.

**Bond or training agreement.** Common in this market at Tier 3. If you must commit to eighteen months with a financial penalty for leaving early, that is a genuine cost and it should change how you value the offer. Read the clause carefully, and be sceptical of a large bond attached to a modest salary.

**Probation terms.** Length, and what notice applies during it.

**Working model.** Days in office, and whether that is contractual or current practice.

If anything is unclear, ask before signing. Asking clear questions about a contract is what a professional does, and no reasonable employer objects to it.

## Multiple offers

The strongest position, and it is worth engineering deliberately by keeping several processes running in parallel rather than pausing when one goes well.

Handle it straightforwardly and without games:

> *"I want to be transparent — I have another offer at 290,000 that I need to respond to by Friday. Your team is genuinely my first choice. Is there room to close the gap?"*

**Never invent an offer.** Some companies ask to see it, and being caught ends the process immediately and permanently at that company.

If a company you prefer is behind in their process, tell them: *"I have an offer with a Friday deadline. I'd rather work with you — is there any way to accelerate?"* Processes move remarkably fast when there is a real deadline.

## When the offer is below your walk-away

It happens, and the answer is not always to decline.

**Consider accepting anyway if:** it is still a large improvement on 85,000, the engineering environment is genuinely good — real code review, senior engineers, modern stack — and you have no other live processes. Twelve months at a Tier 3 company with good practices, then interviewing again from a much stronger position, is a perfectly sound route and is far better than another year where you are.

**Decline if:** the environment looks like your current one, the bond is long, or you have other live processes with a realistic chance.

**How to decline well,** because you may want them next year:

> *"Thank you very much for the offer and for the time everyone spent. I've decided to go in a different direction, but I really appreciated the conversations and I'd be glad to stay in touch."*

No lengthy justification, no criticism.

## Interview Preparation

**"What are your salary expectations?"**

The redirection above. Practise it aloud until it is comfortable, because it will feel confrontational the first three times and it is not. *"Based on the role and my research for this level, I'm looking at 280,000 to 320,000. Is that inside your band?"*

**"That's above our range for this level."**

Do not immediately concede. *"I understand. What is the range for this level? And is there a level where that number would fit?"* Sometimes the answer is that you were being considered for the wrong level, and asking is the only way to find out.

**"Why do you think you're worth that much when you're currently on 85,000?"**

Occasionally asked, and it sounds hostile but is usually a genuine question. Answer on value rather than history. *"My current salary reflects a small company with no engineering organisation, not the market for this work. What I'm bringing is three years of production maintenance experience plus two applications I designed, built and deployed end to end — I can walk through the architecture and the performance work in detail. That's the basis for the number."*

**"Can you start immediately?"**

*"My notice period is one month and I intend to serve it properly."* Offering to break your notice signals how you would treat them on the way out.

## Self Assessment

- Have I written my three numbers — walk-away, target, ambitious — before any offer conversation?
- Can I deliver the current-salary redirection without hesitating?
- Have I asked every recruiter for their band?
- Can I stay silent for ten full seconds after naming a number? Practise it, timed.
- Do I know my contractual notice period, exactly?

## Cheat Sheet

- **Never name your current salary first.** Redirect to your expectation: *"I'd rather not anchor on that — my expectation for this role is X."*
- **Write three numbers before any conversation:** walk-away (~180k), target (250–300k), ambitious (300–350k).
- **Never accept in the conversation where the number is named.** *"Could I take a day and come back to you tomorrow?"*
- **Counter once, then stop talking.** The silence after your number is the technique.
- **Also negotiable:** title, joining bonus, a written six-month review, leave, remote days, equipment, start date.
- **Read the letter:** gross versus net, basic versus allowances, provident fund, bonus conditions, notice period, **bond clauses**, probation.
- **Never invent a competing offer.** Real ones are legitimate leverage; fabricated ones end processes.
- **Below your walk-away?** Accept anyway if the engineering environment is genuinely good and nothing else is live. Twelve months there beats another year where you are.
- **Ask once, accept graciously.** Goodwill with your future manager outlives the last ten thousand.
