# Phase 11 — Chapter 4: Referrals and Recruiters

> *"A cold application competes with three hundred others. A referred one competes with four."*

---

## Chapter Overview

A referral raises your reply rate from roughly seven per cent to somewhere between thirty and fifty. Nothing else available to you moves a number that far. Rewriting your CV might double the reply rate; a referral multiplies it by five.

And yet almost nobody does it, for a reason worth naming directly: asking feels like begging. It feels like using people, like admitting you cannot get in on merit, like imposing on someone who owes you nothing.

This chapter is about why that framing is wrong, and about the specific mechanics — who to ask, what to say, when, and what to do when they say no or say nothing. It is the highest-return chapter in this phase and it is the one most likely to be skipped.

## Why a referral works, mechanically

Understanding the mechanism removes most of the awkwardness, because it shows what you are actually asking for.

When someone refers you, three things change. Your CV arrives in a different queue — usually an internal referral pipeline that a recruiter reads within days rather than a portal that may be read by software only. It arrives with an implicit endorsement, which changes how it is read. And at most companies, **the referrer is paid a bonus if you are hired**, typically a meaningful amount.

That last fact reframes the entire interaction. You are not asking for charity. You are offering someone the opportunity to earn a bonus by introducing a candidate they think is decent. If they think you are not decent, they will not refer you, and that is fine and correct.

The favour you are actually asking is five minutes of their time and a small amount of their reputation. That is a real thing to ask for, and it is much smaller than it feels from your side.

## Who you can actually ask

Start by writing the list. Most people underestimate it by a factor of three or four.

**Former colleagues.** Anyone who has left your company in three years. This is your strongest category — they have worked with you and can speak to it honestly.

**University contacts.** Classmates, seniors, juniors. In the Pakistani market this network is unusually strong and unusually underused. Look through your graduating class on LinkedIn and see where people ended up; you will be surprised.

**Anyone you have ever worked with in any capacity.** Contractors, client-side developers, people from a shared project.

**People from your city or hometown at target companies.** A weaker connection but a real one, and often enough for a first conversation.

**Bootcamp, course and community contacts.** Anyone from a WhatsApp group, a Discord, a local meetup, a training programme.

**Second-degree connections.** People your contacts know. "Do you know anyone at Arbisoft?" is a much easier question to ask a friend than a referral request is to ask a stranger.

**Complete strangers at target companies.** The weakest and most numerous category. Success rate is low — perhaps one in ten will engage — but it is not zero, and one in ten of a large number is not nothing.

Build this list in week 3, one row per person: name, company, how you know them, last contact. Twenty names is a good target. It exists before you need it.

## How to ask

Four rules, then the templates.

**Ask for a conversation, not for a referral.** "Can you refer me?" puts the other person in a position where saying no is awkward and saying yes commits their reputation to someone they have not spoken to in two years. "Can I ask you a couple of questions about what it's like there?" costs them five minutes and lets the referral emerge naturally, which it usually does.

**Be specific about the role.** Send the link. Make it trivially easy for them to act.

**Make it easy to say no.** Add a line that gives them an exit. It sounds counter-intuitive and it measurably improves the response rate, because it removes the pressure that makes people simply not reply.

**Never attach your CV in the first message.** It converts a conversation into a request. Send it when they ask, which they will.

### Template — someone you actually know

> Hi Bilal — hope you're well. I saw you're at 10Pearls now.
>
> I'm looking to move on from my current place — three years on a legacy PHP/AngularJS product, and I've spent the last few months rebuilding into Node, React and PostgreSQL. I've shipped two applications; here's one: [link].
>
> I saw a Backend Engineer role open with you [link]. Would you have ten minutes some time to tell me what the engineering side is actually like there? No pressure at all if you're busy.

### Template — a weak connection or a stranger

> Hi Ayesha — I saw your post about the backend team at Arbisoft.
>
> I'm a backend engineer with three years' experience, currently working in Node, PostgreSQL and AWS — recent project here: [link]. I'm considering applying for the Backend Engineer role and wanted to ask one question: what does the team actually work on day to day?
>
> Completely understand if you don't have time — thanks either way.

### After a good conversation

Do not ask in the conversation. Ask afterwards, in writing:

> Thanks for the time today, that was genuinely useful. Based on what you described I think I'd fit well — would you be comfortable referring me for the Backend Engineer role? Totally fine if not, and I'll apply directly either way.

The last sentence matters. It gives them a clean exit and it tells them you are applying regardless, which removes the sense that their answer determines your fate.

## What to do when they say nothing

Most will not reply. This is the normal outcome and it is not about you — people are busy and LinkedIn messages are easy to miss.

Send exactly one follow-up after a week. Then stop, and leave the relationship undamaged for a future approach. Volume solves this: twenty approaches producing four conversations and two referrals is an excellent week, and it means sixteen people did not respond.

## Being someone worth referring

The other half of this, and the half that determines whether the asking works at all.

When someone considers referring you, they are asking themselves one question: *if this person is bad, how much does it cost me?* Your job is to make that question easy.

**Have something to point at.** A live URL is worth more than any description of your abilities. This is the strongest practical argument for shipping the first project by week 8: it turns every referral conversation from a request into a demonstration.

**Make your public presence consistent.** They will look at your LinkedIn and your GitHub. A LinkedIn that matches your CV and a GitHub with sixteen weeks of daily commits both do quiet work here.

**Be specific about what you want.** "Any opportunity" is hard to refer. "Backend or full-stack, Node and PostgreSQL, mid-level" is easy.

**Say what you are worth without inflating it.** People are more comfortable referring someone whose self-assessment seems accurate.

## Recruiters

A separate channel with different rules. Two types, and confusing them wastes time.

**Internal recruiters** work for the company. They are genuinely useful: they know the process, the team, the band, and they want you to succeed because filling the role is their job. Treat them as an ally. Ask them directly what the process is, how many stages, what the technical round covers, and what the band for the role is. Most will tell you, and knowing the band before the salary conversation is a large advantage.

**Agency recruiters** work for themselves and are paid a percentage when they place you. Some are excellent and know the market genuinely well. Many will submit you to anything with a matching keyword. Working with them productively:

- **Ask which company before agreeing to be submitted.** Never allow a blind submission. If two recruiters submit you to the same company, you can be disqualified entirely.
- **Ask what the band is.** A recruiter who will not say is not worth your time.
- **Do not tell them your current salary.** They will pass it on, and it will anchor everything downstream. The handling is in Chapter 6.
- **Keep a record of who submitted you where.** This prevents duplicate submissions, which are a genuine and avoidable way to lose a good opportunity.

### Making recruiters come to you

The LinkedIn profile does this work while you sleep, and it is worth thirty minutes of optimisation.

- **Headline:** the role you want plus your stack. *"Software Engineer · Node.js · React · PostgreSQL · AWS"*. Not "aspiring", not "seeking opportunities" — those read as weakness and they are not what recruiters search for.
- **Open to work,** set to recruiters only, with the job titles and locations filled in. This is the actual search filter recruiters use.
- **About section:** four short paragraphs — what you do, what you have built with links, your stack, and what you are looking for. Written as prose, not as a list of adjectives.
- **Featured section:** your two project links, pinned.
- **Skills:** the fifteen that match what you actually do. Recruiter searches run against this field, so an empty skills section makes you invisible regardless of the rest.
- **Activity:** post occasionally about what you are building. Even one short post a fortnight — "shipped the auth layer this week, here's what I learned about refresh token rotation" — puts you in feeds and reads as an engineer who is active.

## The weekly referral routine

Fifteen minutes, twice a week, inside the lunchtime job-hunt block. Sustainable, and over twelve weeks it produces more than any single burst of effort.

- **Monday:** three new approaches from the list.
- **Thursday:** follow up on last week's approaches; add three new names to the list.
- **Saturday review:** count referral conversations had and referrals secured. Target roughly a third of your applications carrying a referral by week eight.

## Interview Preparation

**"How did you hear about us?"**

If you were referred, say the name immediately — it is the entire point. *"Bilal Ahmed on the platform team; I spoke to him about the work a couple of weeks ago."* This changes how the rest of the conversation is weighted.

**"Tell me about your relationship with the person who referred you."**

Be accurate. Overstating a connection is easily checked and it damages both of you. *"We worked together for about a year at my current company before he left"* or *"We've not worked together — we connected on LinkedIn and he was generous enough to answer some questions about the team"* are both perfectly fine answers.

**"What do you know about our engineering team?"**

If you had a referral conversation, this question is free, and it is one of the most valuable secondary benefits of the whole exercise. You know what they work on, how they are organised, and what they struggle with, from someone inside. Use it.

## Self Assessment

- How many names are on my list? Under twenty means it is incomplete.
- How many approaches did I make this week? Target: three.
- What proportion of my applications carry a referral? Target: a third by week eight.
- Does my LinkedIn have a headline, an About section, skills, and my two projects featured?
- Do I have something to point at yet? If not, that is the week-8 project and it is why it matters so much.

## Cheat Sheet

- **Referrals multiply the reply rate by three to five.** Nothing else you can do moves a number that far.
- **The referrer usually earns a bonus.** You are offering an opportunity, not asking for charity.
- **Ask for a conversation, not a referral.** The referral emerges from the conversation.
- **Never attach the CV in the first message.** Always include a line that makes it easy to say no.
- **One follow-up after a week, then stop.** Most will not reply, and that is normal.
- **Have something to point at.** A live URL converts a request into a demonstration.
- **Internal recruiters are allies** — ask them the process and the band directly.
- **Agency recruiters: never allow a blind submission,** and never give them your current salary.
- **LinkedIn is a search index.** Headline, About, Skills and Open-to-work are the fields recruiters actually search.
- **Three approaches Monday, follow-ups Thursday, count on Saturday.**
