# Phase 11 — Chapter 1: The Pakistani Software Market

> *"Applying without knowing the tier you are applying to is how three hundred applications produce four replies."*

---

## Chapter Overview

Most job-hunting advice you will read online was written for the American market, where the hiring process, the salary structures, the role titles and the signals that matter are all different. Applying that advice here produces confusing results: you follow it carefully, and nothing happens.

This chapter is the map of the market you are actually in. Who hires, what they pay, what they screen for, how the process runs, and where a three-year engineer with your specific profile fits. It is the chapter to read before you send a single application, because targeting is the highest-leverage decision in the entire job hunt and it is made before any of the effort is spent.

The numbers in this chapter are approximate and they move. Treat them as a map, and correct them from what you actually see in your own interviews — by week eight you will have better data about your own market position than any chapter can give you.

## The four tiers

Pakistani software employers sort into four groups. The distinction that matters is not size or age; it is **who the customer is and how the company makes money**, because that determines both what they pay and what they need from you.

### Tier 4 — small shops and agencies

Ten to forty people. Local clients, small international clients found on freelancing platforms, or a single long-running contract. The technology is whatever the client asked for, which is why so many of them run PHP, WordPress, and older frameworks.

**Pay, three-year engineer:** PKR 50,000 – 120,000/month.

**Hiring:** often no technical interview at all, or a short conversation. Sometimes a small take-home task. The decision is frequently made by a non-technical owner.

**What it does to you:** this is where you are now, so you know. No code review, no senior engineers, no architecture, no standards. You ship, and you learn nothing about how to ship well. Three years here and three years at a Tier 2 company produce completely different engineers, which is the whole reason you are reading this book.

### Tier 3 — large service and outsourcing companies

Devsinc, Tkxel, Contour Software, NETSOL Technologies, Systems Ltd's larger delivery teams, and dozens of similar firms. Hundreds or thousands of engineers, delivering to foreign clients on a contract basis.

**Pay, three-year engineer:** PKR 120,000 – 250,000/month.

**Hiring:** a real process, usually three stages — a screening call, a technical interview with a live coding exercise of moderate difficulty, and a manager or client-fit round. They hire in volume, often continuously, and the bar is genuine but not high. Availability and English matter noticeably.

**What it does to you:** a large improvement on Tier 4. There are seniors, there is process, and there is usually code review. The limitation is that you are working on somebody else's product to somebody else's specification, so architectural decisions are rarely yours and the technology is chosen by the client.

**This is your floor.** If December arrives without a Tier 2 offer, a Tier 3 offer roughly doubles your salary and puts you in a materially better environment. Take it, and run the next plan from there.

### Tier 2 — strong service firms and product companies

Arbisoft, 10Pearls, VentureDive, Confiz, Folio3, Systems Ltd product teams, S&P Global's Pakistan engineering office, and similar. Some are service companies with unusually high standards; some build their own products; several do both.

**Pay, three-year engineer:** PKR 250,000 – 450,000/month.

**Hiring:** four to five stages. A recruiter screen, a technical screen, one or two deep technical rounds including system design, and a culture or manager round. The coding round is real. They will ask you to explain your own projects in detail and they will follow up on the answers.

**What they screen for, specifically:** fundamentals you can defend rather than technologies you can list. Whether you understand what your framework is doing underneath. Whether you can design a schema. Whether you can reason about a system you have not built. And whether you can communicate clearly, which at this tier is weighted heavily because much of the work involves foreign clients or distributed teams.

**This is the mission target,** and everything in this book is calibrated to it.

### Tier 1 — product companies and international engineering offices

Motive, Careem, Bazaar, Retailo, Educative, and the Pakistan engineering arms of foreign firms. They build their own products at scale, they compete for the same engineers as international companies, and they pay accordingly.

**Pay, three-year engineer:** PKR 400,000 – 800,000+/month, sometimes with equity.

**Hiring:** the full loop — a strong algorithmic bar, one or two system design rounds, deep language questions, and a high communication standard. Processes take four to ten weeks and rejection rates are high at every stage.

**Honest assessment for you:** reachable, but not by December. The algorithmic bar alone typically wants six to twelve months of dedicated practice, and spending your sixteen weeks chasing it means failing to build the things Tier 2 actually asks for. Apply to a few anyway — the interview experience is genuinely valuable, and occasionally a team hires for exactly the profile you have — but do not build the plan around it.

The realistic route to Tier 1 is: get to Tier 2 now, spend a year doing real engineering with real review, then interview at Tier 1 from a position of strength with a recognisable company on your CV. That is a two-year path, and it is much more likely to work than a sixteen-week assault.

## What a two-tier jump requires

You are attempting Tier 4 to Tier 2. That is a real jump, and it is worth being explicit about what actually bridges it, because it is not what most candidates think.

**It is not more technologies on the CV.** A list of fourteen technologies reads as a warning rather than a strength at Tier 2, because they know you cannot defend fourteen.

**It is not certifications alone.** Your AWS Cloud Practitioner is worth something only when attached to an architecture you built and can explain. On its own it signals that you can pass a multiple-choice exam.

**It is these four things, in order of weight:**

1. **Two real projects you built and deployed, that you can explain at any depth for twenty minutes.** This is the single biggest differentiator. Most candidates at your level have tutorial projects, or a work project they cannot discuss in detail because they did not design it.
2. **Fundamentals you can produce cold.** Writing a function from an empty file, explaining what happens underneath, designing a schema on a whiteboard.
3. **Database depth.** This is the most reliably underprepared area in the entire market. An engineer who can read a query plan and explain an index is remembered.
4. **Communication in English, under pressure.** At Tier 2 this is not a bonus. Their clients are foreign. A candidate who codes slightly worse but explains clearly beats one who codes slightly better and cannot.

Notice what is not on that list: years of experience, university, the specific technology your last job used. Those matter at the screening stage and almost nowhere else.

## How the process actually runs here

Five things about Pakistani hiring specifically, which differ from what international advice will tell you.

**Referrals dominate.** A referred application is read by a human, usually within days. A cold application on a careers portal may not be read at all. The multiplier is roughly three to five times on the reply rate, and it is the highest-return activity in your whole job hunt. This is why referral work is a scheduled weekly task rather than an optional extra.

**Salary is asked early and directly.** You will be asked your current salary, often in the first call, sometimes on the application form itself. This is a genuine disadvantage when your current salary is 85,000, and Chapter 6 is entirely about how to handle it. Read that chapter before your first screening call, not before your first offer.

**Notice periods are long.** One month is standard; two months is common; three months exists. Employers know this and factor it in, but it means an offer signed in December often starts in February. Plan for it, and know your own contractual notice period before you negotiate anything.

**Bonds and training agreements exist.** Some companies, particularly at Tier 3, ask you to sign an agreement committing you to stay for a period, with a financial penalty for leaving early. Read any such clause carefully before signing, and price it into your decision — a bonded 200,000 is not straightforwardly better than an unbonded 180,000.

**The process is faster than the American equivalent.** Two to four weeks from application to offer is normal at Tier 3, three to six at Tier 2. This is good news for a sixteen-week plan, and it is why applications opening in week 4 can still produce an offer by week 16.

## Cities, and remote work within Pakistan

**Lahore** has the largest concentration of engineering employers and the widest range of tiers. **Islamabad and Rawalpindi** host many product companies and foreign engineering offices, and typically pay slightly more for equivalent roles. **Karachi** has the fintech and commerce startups along with large service firms.

Remote and hybrid work within Pakistan became normal after 2020 and has partly receded since; many Tier 2 companies now expect two or three days in an office. If you are not in one of the three main cities, filter for genuinely remote roles and expect a smaller market — but not an empty one, and the compensation is usually close to the office-based band.

## The stack question

You are moving from PHP and AngularJS to Node, React, PostgreSQL and AWS. Two honest points about that decision.

**The market reality:** JavaScript and TypeScript across the stack is the largest single hiring surface in Pakistan, and it is where the Tier 2 volume is. .NET and Java are strong in enterprise and finance, and pay comparably, but both would mean starting a new language from nothing. Modern PHP with Laravel is a genuine market and would be the fastest possible switch, with a lower ceiling internationally.

**The three years are not wasted by the switch.** When an interviewer asks why you are moving, the honest answer is strong: you spent three years maintaining a legacy PHP and AngularJS system, you understand what makes a codebase expensive to maintain, and you moved to a stack with a larger market and a clearer future. That answer is better than a candidate who has only ever used one modern stack and has never seen what happens to code over three years.

Do not be defensive about the old stack. Legacy maintenance is real engineering work and everybody in the room has done some.

## What to expect in your first ten applications

So that the early weeks do not read as a verdict.

Of your first ten applications, expect zero to one replies. This is normal and it is not information about you. The funnel operates on volume and time: five applications a day, every weekday, means twenty-five a week, which means the first meaningful signal arrives somewhere around application forty in week five or six.

The single most common failure is stopping at thirty applications and concluding the market has rejected you. At thirty applications you have not yet run the experiment.

## Interview Preparation

**"Why are you leaving your current company?"**

This is asked in every screening call and it is the first place candidates damage themselves. Never criticise the employer — not because it is dishonest, but because the interviewer cannot verify it and it makes you sound like a future problem.

The strong version of your answer names what you want rather than what you are escaping. Something structurally like: *"I have spent three years on a legacy PHP and AngularJS product, mostly maintenance, working alone. I have learned a lot about what makes a codebase expensive to maintain, but there was no code review and no senior engineers, so I was not growing at the rate I wanted. I have spent the last few months rebuilding my fundamentals in Node, React and PostgreSQL and building two projects end to end, and I am looking for a team where the work is reviewed and the standards come from more than just me."*

That answer is honest, it is not bitter, it explains the gap in your experience before they have to ask, and it ends on what you have done about it. Practise it aloud until it is natural, because a rehearsed-sounding delivery is worse than a rougher genuine one.

**"You have three years of experience but this is mostly maintenance work. What have you actually built?"**

The question you are most afraid of, and the one you will be best prepared for by December. Do not deflect it. Answer it directly by moving immediately to the projects: what you built, why you made each decision, what broke, what you measured. Twenty minutes of detailed, confident discussion of something you genuinely built resolves this question more thoroughly than any amount of framing.

If you have no such project yet, that is what weeks 3 to 13 exist for. This is not a question you can talk your way past; it is one you answer with evidence.

**"What is your current salary?"**

Answer it with a redirection to your expectation rather than your history — the full handling, including what to do when they insist, is in Chapter 6. Read that chapter before your first screening call.

**"We use Java here, not Node. Why should we consider you?"**

A fair question and an easy one. You have now demonstrably learned a second stack from a standing start, in public, with commits to prove it. That is the actual evidence they want — not that you know their language, but that you can acquire one. Say that, and point at the repository.

## Self Assessment

- Which tier am I applying to, for each company on my list? Write the tier next to each one.
- For each of my top ten targets, do I know what they build, who their customers are, and what their stack is?
- Do I have a referral route into any of them? If none, that is this week's highest-priority task.
- What is my honest walk-away number, and what is my target?
- Can I answer "why are you leaving?" in under ninety seconds without criticising anyone?

- **You are T4. The target is T2. T3 at ~180k is the acceptable floor.** T1 is a two-year path, not a sixteen-week one.
- **What bridges the jump:** two explainable deployed projects · cold fundamentals · database depth · clear English under pressure.
- **Referrals convert three to five times better than cold applications.** Highest-return activity in the job hunt.
