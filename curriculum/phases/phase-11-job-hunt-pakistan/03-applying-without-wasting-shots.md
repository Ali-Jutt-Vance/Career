# Phase 11 — Chapter 3: Applying Without Wasting Shots

> *"You get one application per company per role. Spending it badly is not free."*

---

## Chapter Overview

From 28 September you send five applications every weekday for twelve weeks. That is roughly three hundred applications, and it sounds like a lot until you look at the conversion rates, at which point it becomes obvious that it is the minimum rather than the maximum.

This chapter is about making those three hundred shots count: where to find roles, how to decide whether to apply, how long each application should take, what to write in the message that accompanies it, and how to track the whole thing so that in week ten you can diagnose a problem instead of guessing at one.

The central discipline is this: **applying is a scheduled, bounded, daily activity, not something you do when you feel like it.** Thirty minutes at lunchtime, five applications, logged. Then you stop and go back to your day. Job hunting expands to fill whatever time it is given and produces no more results for the extra hours, so it gets a fixed box.

## The arithmetic, restated

| Stage | Rate | Over 300 applications |
|---|---|---|
| Cold application → reply | 5 – 12% | 20 – 35 replies |
| Referred application → reply | 30 – 50% | which is why referrals are separately scheduled |
| Reply → screening call | 50 – 70% | 15 – 25 screening calls |
| Screening → technical | 40 – 60% | 8 – 14 technical rounds |
| Technical → final | 30 – 50% | 4 – 7 finals |
| Final → offer | 30 – 50% | **2 – 4 offers** |

Two things follow from this table and they are the whole strategy.

**Volume is not optional.** At a 7% reply rate, thirty applications produce two replies, and two replies produce nothing. The plan needs three hundred because the funnel is that narrow, and the narrowness is arithmetic rather than a judgement about you.

**But volume alone fails too.** Three hundred badly targeted applications with a generic CV produce the same as thirty. The reply rate is the multiplier on everything, and it is determined by targeting and by the CV, which is why week 3 exists.

## Where the roles actually are

In rough order of how well they convert.

**Referrals — by far the best.** Covered fully in Chapter 4. Three to five times the reply rate. If you can reach someone inside the company, that route always beats the portal.

**LinkedIn Jobs.** The largest volume of Tier 2 and Tier 3 roles in Pakistan. Set up saved searches for "Software Engineer", "Backend Engineer", "Full Stack Engineer", "Node.js" filtered to Pakistan and to the last week. Check daily; posts get hundreds of applicants within days and being early measurably helps.

**Company careers pages directly.** For your target list, check the careers page weekly. Roles often appear there before they are posted anywhere else, and a direct application sometimes routes to a different, less crowded inbox.

**Rozee.pk and local boards.** Higher volume of Tier 3 and Tier 4 roles, lower signal. Worth a weekly pass rather than a daily one.

**LinkedIn posts, not job listings.** Engineering managers and recruiters frequently post "we're hiring" directly. These convert unusually well because far fewer people see them. Follow the engineering leads at your target companies, not just the company pages.

**Recruiters and agencies.** Mixed value; some are excellent, many will submit you to anything. Chapter 4 covers how to work with them.

## Deciding whether to apply

Apply when at least three of these are true:

- The role is at Tier 2 or Tier 3
- The stack overlaps yours substantially — Node, React, PostgreSQL, AWS, or generally JavaScript
- It asks for two to five years, or does not specify
- It is in a city you can reach, or is genuinely remote
- The company builds something you could describe to a friend

**Do not filter yourself out on the requirements list.** Job adverts are written as wish lists, and the expectation is that candidates meet perhaps 60% of them. If it asks for five years and you have three, apply. If it names one technology you have never used and four you have, apply. The list of things you cannot do is decided by the interviewer, not by you in advance.

**Do filter out** roles that are clearly a different discipline — data engineering, mobile, embedded, machine learning research — and Tier 1 roles at the very top of the market beyond a handful for the experience. Those are not shots you are wasting; they are shots that were never available.

## The four-minute application

Five applications in thirty minutes means about five minutes each, which is enough if you have a system.

1. **Read the advert properly, once.** Note the five technologies named and the one problem the role is clearly meant to solve. (60 seconds)
2. **Reorder the CV skills** so the named technologies appear first, and adjust the summary's middle sentence. Export the PDF. (90 seconds)
3. **Write the message** if there is a field for one, from the template below, with one genuinely specific sentence. (90 seconds)
4. **Submit, and log it.** Company, role, date, source, link, referral contact if any, status. (60 seconds)

If an application requires more than about ten minutes of forms, decide deliberately whether the company is worth it. Some are. Most are not, and the time is better spent on two more applications elsewhere.

## The message

Most applications have a cover letter field or allow a note. Most candidates either leave it blank or paste something generic. A short, specific message is a real advantage precisely because so few people write one.

Four sentences, and it should read like a person wrote it:

> Hello — I am applying for the Backend Engineer role.
>
> I have three years building and maintaining a PHP/AngularJS product as the sole developer, and over the last four months I have rebuilt into Node, React and PostgreSQL — including [SplitLedger](link), a full-stack expense-splitting application running on AWS with token authentication and a documented architecture.
>
> Your advert mentions query performance on a growing dataset; that is the part of my recent work I would most like to talk about — I have a written performance report with before-and-after query plans in the repository.
>
> Happy to walk through any of it. CV attached.

The third sentence is the one that works, and it is the one that cannot be templated. It proves you read the advert. Everything else is scaffolding.

**Never send the same message twice unmodified.** Not for moral reasons — because a generic message has close to a zero response rate, so sending it is functionally the same as not applying while still consuming the shot.

## Tracking

One spreadsheet, from day one of applications. Without it you cannot diagnose anything in week ten, and diagnosis is the whole point of the exercise.

| Column | Why |
|---|---|
| Company | |
| Tier | Lets you see whether you are aiming too high or too low |
| Role and link | You will need the advert again before the interview |
| Date applied | Drives follow-up timing |
| Source | LinkedIn, careers page, referral, recruiter — tells you which channel works |
| Referral contact | Who, if anyone |
| Status | Applied · Replied · Screening · Technical · Final · Offer · Rejected · Silent |
| Last action and next action | The two most important columns |
| Notes | Names, salary discussed, anything said on a call |

The **next action** column is what turns a list into a pipeline. Any live conversation with no next action is quietly dead. Reviewing that column takes two minutes and it is the most valuable part of the Saturday review.

## Follow-ups

**After five working days of silence:** one short message. To a named person if you have one; otherwise reply to your own application email.

> Hello — following up on my application for the Backend Engineer role sent on 2 October. Still very interested, and happy to send anything that would help. Thank you.

**After a further ten days:** mark it Silent and move on. Do not send a third.

**After a rejection:** reply once, briefly, thanking them and asking for one specific piece of feedback. Perhaps one in five will answer, and one honest answer is worth more than twenty guesses about why you were rejected.

**After an interview:** a short thank-you within twenty-four hours, referencing one specific thing that was discussed. It rarely changes a decision but it costs three minutes, and it occasionally does.

## When the numbers are bad

Diagnose from where the funnel stops, and fix that one thing rather than everything.

| What you see | The problem | This week's fix |
|---|---|---|
| 40+ applications, under 3 replies | The CV or the targeting | Rewrite the CV to outcomes. Check the tier — you may be applying too high or too low. |
| Replies, but no calls booked | Your reply speed or your first message | Answer within hours. Rewrite the LinkedIn About section. |
| Calls that go nowhere | The self-introduction, or English fluency | Rehearse the two-minute introduction daily until it is automatic. |
| Technical rounds failing | Cold generation and speaking while coding | More unaided problems, every one of them out loud. |
| Finals failing | Communication, or behavioural answers | Written STAR stories; mock interviews with real people. |

"I am not getting a job" is never the diagnosis. It is always a specific stage, and each stage has a different fix.

## The psychological part, stated plainly

Twelve weeks of mostly silence is genuinely hard, and it is worth knowing in advance what it does to people so that you can recognise it happening to you.

Rejections do not arrive as rejections. Most arrive as nothing at all, which is worse, because there is no closure and no information. Around week seven, with roughly a hundred and fifty applications sent and perhaps two interviews, the natural conclusion is that something is fundamentally wrong with you. That conclusion is not supported by the arithmetic — at a 7% reply rate, that outcome is close to expected.

Three things help concretely.

**Separate the number you control from the number you do not.** You control applications sent. You do not control replies. Score yourself weekly on the first one only.

**Keep the two tracks separate in your head.** Engineering progress is visible weekly — commits, projects, problems solved. The job hunt is invisible for weeks and then moves all at once. When the job hunt is silent, the engineering scoreboard is the evidence that the work is real.

**Remember that the funnel converts late.** Applications from weeks 4 to 8 produce offers in weeks 10 to 16. The silence in October is not a forecast of December; it is the pipeline filling.

## Interview Preparation

**"Why do you want to work here specifically?"**

Asked constantly, and a generic answer is noticed instantly. You need one real sentence about the company — what they build, who uses it, something recent — plus one honest sentence about what you want, which for you is genuinely strong: *"I have been the only developer for three years with nobody reviewing my code. I want to be somewhere with senior engineers and real review, because that is the thing I have not had."* Interviewers respond well to that, because it is true and because it describes someone who will use the environment.

**"How many places are you interviewing at?"**

Answer honestly but without numbers that sound either desperate or arrogant. *"I am in conversation with a few companies, at different stages."* If you have a live offer, say so — it is legitimate leverage and it also creates urgency in their process.

**"When can you start?"**

Know your contractual notice period before you are asked. *"My notice period is one month, so I could start by the first week of February."* Do not offer to break your notice period; it signals how you would treat them on the way out.

## Self Assessment

- Am I sending five every weekday, or bunching them and calling it the same thing?
- Is every application logged with a source and a next action?
- What percentage of my applications came through a referral? If under 20%, that is the fix.
- When did I last send a genuinely specific message rather than a template?
- Which row of the diagnosis table am I in right now?

## Cheat Sheet

- **Five applications every weekday, thirty minutes, logged.** A fixed box, not a mood.
- **Roughly 300 applications → 2–4 offers.** Volume is arithmetic, not desperation.
- **Do not filter yourself out on the requirements list.** 60% match is the normal expectation.
- **Four-minute application:** read the advert, reorder skills, one specific sentence, submit and log.
- **The one sentence that works** is the one proving you read the advert. Everything else is scaffolding.
- **Follow up once at five working days, then once more at fifteen, then stop.**
- **Ask every rejection for one specific piece of feedback.** One in five answers.
- **The "next action" column is what makes it a pipeline.** No next action means dead.
- **Score yourself on applications sent, never on replies received.**
- **Week 7 will feel like failure.** At a 7% reply rate it is close to expected. The funnel converts late.
