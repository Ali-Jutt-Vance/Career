# Phase 0 — Chapter 1: How to Use This Book

> *"You do not need to read this book. You need to run it."*

---

## Chapter Overview

This book is not a reference you consult when something breaks. It is a sixteen-week programme with a start date, an end date, and a task for every single morning. Used as intended it will take you from where you are now — three years in, unable to write code without help, on a salary that a good internship pays — to a signed offer at a company where the work will actually make you better.

Used the way people normally use technical books, which is to read the interesting parts and feel productive, it will do nothing at all. This chapter is about the difference.

It also explains how the material is written, because the writing style is deliberate and it will help you to know what to expect. Every chapter explains **why something exists before explaining what it does**, and names **where it breaks** rather than only the happy path. That is a slower way to write and a slower way to read. It is also the difference between knowing a fact and being able to defend a decision, and interviews test the second one.

## Start here if the whole thing looks intimidating

The book contains around one hundred and fifty chapters. You are not going to read one hundred and fifty chapters, and you should stop looking at that number immediately.

**You are going to read what the plan tells you to read, on the day it tells you.** That is roughly four chapters a week, of which one or two are read in full and the rest are read in specific sections. The remaining chapters are reference material — they exist so that when an interviewer asks about message queues in week 15, there is something to read that night. They are not homework.

Here is the only navigation instruction that matters:

> **Open the app. It opens on today. Do the three things it lists. Close the app.**

Everything else in this chapter is detail you can come back to.

## The four parts of the system

There is one folder on your computer, and it is one machine with four parts.

**The plan.** Phase 12, and the Plan tab in the app. One hundred and twelve dated days, three or four tasks each, in time slots. This is the part you actually use every day, and it is the part that makes the rest work. Without it you would be choosing what to study each morning, and choosing is where motivation goes to die.

**The book.** Phases 0 through 11, plus the appendix. The technical material, arranged by subject, written to be read from a standing start. Phase 12 tells you which parts to read and when.

**The app.** A reader with a Plan tab, a Chapters tab, a search box and a question-and-answer view. Progress is saved on your machine. It opens on today's plan every time you launch it.

**Your own repository.** `escape-plan` on GitHub, created on day one. A daily log, every exercise you write, and eventually both projects. This is the only one of the four parts that a hiring manager will ever see, and by December it is the strongest thing you own.

## How to read a chapter

Chapters follow a consistent shape, and knowing the shape lets you read selectively without missing anything important.

| Section | What it is | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **Chapter Overview** | Why the technology exists and what problem it solved | Always. It is the part that makes the rest stick. |
| **Beginner Theory** | The concept from nothing, no assumed knowledge | Always, first time. Skip on a revisit. |
| **Basic Examples** | Small, runnable code | Type it out. Do not copy and paste. |
| **Intermediate / Advanced** | Real-world usage and depth | On the day the plan schedules it |
| **Industry Usage** | How it is actually used in production | Skim, then reread before an interview |
| **Alternatives** | What else solves this and when to prefer it | This is where trade-off answers come from |
| **Security / Performance / Debugging** | Where it breaks and what it costs | Read properly. This is the senior material. |
| **Interview Preparation** | Full multi-paragraph answers | Read aloud, not silently. The highest-value section in every chapter. |
| **Practical Tasks / Projects** | What to build | This is the actual work |
| **Self Assessment** | Questions to answer honestly | Answer them in writing |
| **Cheat Sheet** | Compressed recall | Before an interview, not instead of the chapter |

Two of those deserve emphasis.

**Read the Interview Preparation sections out loud.** Not in your head. The gap between recognising a good answer and being able to produce one under pressure is enormous, and reading silently trains only the first. This applies from week one, months before your first real interview.

**Type the code, never paste it.** Typing is slower and that is the point — the slowness is where the learning happens. You have spent three years having code appear in your editor without passing through your hands. This is the correction.

## The daily loop

Every working day has the same shape. It never changes for sixteen weeks, which is what makes it survivable.

**05:00 — Deep Study, two hours.** Reading and understanding, before work, when nothing can take the time from you. This is the block the whole schedule is built around. Evening study is the first thing life cancels; five in the morning is not.

**13:00 — Job Hunt, thirty minutes.** Applications, referrals, follow-ups, interview preparation. Thirty minutes is enough because the work is repetitive and bounded. It is at lunchtime because it needs no deep focus.

**21:00 — Build, ninety minutes.** Writing code. Not reading, not watching, not researching. If you find yourself reading documentation at half past nine, the day has gone wrong — the reading was supposed to happen at dawn.

**Saturday** is eight hours in four blocks: a long build, study, an interview drill, and the weekly review. The weekly review is the last hour of the week and it is the highest-leverage hour in the whole system.

**Sunday is off.** No tasks at all. Not a suggestion — a scheduled, load-bearing part of the plan, and the streak counter treats it as neutral so resting never breaks anything.

## The rules

Seven, and they exist because each one corresponds to a specific way people fail at this.

**1. The dawn block is untouchable.** Two hours, five mornings a week. Everything else is negotiable; this is not.

**2. No AI writes code for you until 2 November.** Chapter 4 explains this properly and it is the single most important rule in the book. Documentation is allowed. Concept explanations are allowed after twenty minutes of genuine attempt. Generated code is not.

**3. Commit every working day.** A day with no commit did not happen. The contribution graph is the cheapest credibility signal available and it compounds for sixteen weeks.

**4. Twenty minutes before you look anything up.** Stuck means stuck for twenty minutes. Most of the time you will solve it in eight, and the tolerance you build is worth more than the time.

**5. Start applying before you feel ready.** Applications open on 28 September regardless of how prepared you feel, because the funnel takes four to eight weeks and you cannot buy that time back in December.

**6. Miss one day, fine. Miss two, diagnose.** Never try to catch up — catching up is how a one-day slip becomes a three-week collapse. Resume on today, and work out in the review what actually caused it.

**7. Milestones are pass or fail.** "Mostly did it" is a no. Be as strict with yourself as the interviewer will be, because the interviewer is the calibration.

## What to do when it goes wrong

It will, in one of four ways. All four are survivable and none of them means the plan has failed.

**You miss the dawn block.** Do the study at night instead and move the build to the following morning. Do not skip it and do not double up tomorrow. One block moved is a normal week; the dawn block disappearing for a fortnight is a different problem, and the review is where you catch it.

**You cannot do the work at all — genuinely exhausted, ill, a family emergency.** Take the day. Write one line in the log saying why. Resume tomorrow at today's task, not at the one you missed. The plan has no catch-up mechanism on purpose.

**You break the AI rule.** Record it in the log with the time and what you were stuck on. Do not restart the eight weeks and do not treat it as a moral failure. Three or four honest records will show you the pattern, and the pattern is almost always about tiredness rather than difficulty.

**You fall a week behind.** Do not compress two weeks into one. Drop the reference reading, keep the building and the job hunt, and carry on. Under pressure, cut breadth to preserve depth — that is the standing principle in this book, and it has already been applied once to build this version of the plan.

## An honest note on the word "beginner"

You may have arrived at this book believing you are a beginner starting from zero. You are not, and the distinction matters practically rather than emotionally.

A beginner does not know what a deployment is, has never seen a production bug, has never maintained code somebody else wrote, and has no idea how a real project actually goes wrong. You know all of that. What you are missing is a specific, nameable set of fundamentals — writing code cold, understanding what the framework is doing underneath, designing a schema, defending a decision out loud.

That distinction changes the plan. A genuine beginner would need twelve to eighteen months. You need sixteen weeks, because most of what takes a beginner a year is context, and you already paid for the context. You paid for it in a job that gave you very little else, which is precisely why you are leaving.

The book is written to be readable from a standing start, because the fundamentals genuinely do need to be built from the ground up. That is not the same as you being a beginner. It means the material is honest about where it starts.
