## Chapter Overview

Everything in the previous six chapters is a method. This chapter is where you check whether the methods work, using material written to the same specification as the real exam and marked with the same criteria.

Two honest notes before you start.

**First, this is original practice material, not a past paper.** It is written to match IELTS question types, text lengths, and difficulty, and the answer keys explain the reasoning behind every answer — which is something official papers rarely do. But for *timed, band-converted* practice you should still use the Cambridge IELTS series (books 15–20) and the free official tests at `ielts.org`, because only official material is calibrated. Use this chapter to learn the reasoning; use official papers to measure the score.

**Second, the Listening section here has no audio.** The workaround is genuinely effective: the transcripts are printed at the end of the section, and you have a partner read them aloud once at natural pace, or paste them into a text-to-speech tool at normal speed. Read the questions first, listen once, and do not pause. Play nothing twice.

The most valuable part of this chapter is the third section: the same Writing and Speaking tasks answered at three different bands, with commentary explaining exactly what changes between them. Seeing a Band 6 and a Band 8 response to the identical prompt, side by side, teaches the criteria faster than any amount of descriptor-reading.

**Topics:**
- The full-test simulation protocol
- Listening: Part 1 and Part 4 sets, with transcripts and explained answers
- Reading: a full-length academic passage with 14 questions and explained answers
- Writing Task 1: one task, answered at Band 5.5, 6.5, and 8.0, with commentary
- Writing Task 2: one task, answered at Band 6.0, 7.0, and 8.0, with commentary
- Speaking: a full test script with Band 6 and Band 8 answers compared
- Self-marking rubrics for Writing and Speaking
- Converting your raw score, and deciding what to do next

## How To Use This Chapter

```
FIRST PASS — untimed, learning mode
  Do each section without a clock. After each question, read the explanation
  even if you got it right — the reasoning matters more than the answer.
  Write your own answer to each Writing task BEFORE reading the models.

SECOND PASS — timed, measurement mode
  Listening: 30 minutes, one play only
  Reading:   20 minutes for the single passage (it is one of three in a real paper)
  Writing:   20 minutes Task 1, 40 minutes Task 2, no dictionary
  Speaking:  recorded, timed strictly: Part 1 (4 min) · Part 2 (1+2 min) · Part 3 (4 min)

THIRD PASS — two weeks later
  Redo the Reading passage and the Listening sets. Anything you get wrong the
  second time is a genuine knowledge gap, not a careless slip. Those go into
  your error log with priority.
```

### The Full-Test Simulation Protocol

Once a week from three weeks out, run a complete official test under these conditions. This is not optional if you want an accurate prediction of your band.

```

  A desk and a chair. Not a bed. Not a sofa.
  Water on the table. One toilet break allowed, timed against you (as in the exam).
  Same writing tool as the real test: pen and paper, or keyboard.

ORDER AND TIMING — no break between sections, exactly as on test day
  Listening   30 min audio + 10 min transfer (paper) / 2 min check (computer)

  Speaking    same day if you can arrange a partner; otherwise recorded solo

  Mark Listening and Reading. Convert to bands using the tables in Chapter 1.
  Log every wrong answer against the six error categories from Chapter 4.

NEXT DAY (60 minutes)
  Self-mark Writing with the rubric below, criterion by criterion.
  Listen to your Speaking recording once with the rubric in front of you.
  Write down ONE change per section for the coming week. Only one.
```

```
  Good afternoon. Today I want to trace the history of a material so ordinary
  that we look through it without noticing it exists: glass.
  The earliest glass objects we have found are beads dating from around three
  and a half thousand BC, in Mesopotamia and Egypt. What is striking is how
  precious the material was. Early glass was opaque, difficult to work, and
  produced in tiny quantities, and it was regarded as a luxury — as valuable,
  ounce for ounce, as gold. It was buried with kings.
  The transformation came with a Roman-era innovation: glass blowing, which
  appeared in Syria in the first century BC. Instead of laboriously shaping
  glass around a core, a craftsman could now inflate a bubble of molten glass
  and form a vessel in minutes. Production times collapsed, and with them
  prices. For the first time glass became cheap enough for everyday domestic
  use — storage jars, drinking cups, perfume bottles. The Romans were also the
  first civilisation to glaze windows, initially in bath houses where the
  combination of light and retained heat was particularly welcome.
  After the fall of Rome, the expertise survived unevenly, and by the
  thirteenth century production shifted decisively to Venice, which dominated
  European glassmaking for roughly three hundred years. In 1291 the furnaces
  were moved out of the city to the island of Murano. This is often described
  as an act of industrial secrecy, and it partly was — but the immediate
  reason was more mundane: the furnaces were a fire hazard in a city built
  largely of wood. Secrecy came next. Murano's craftsmen enjoyed extraordinary
  privileges, and were simultaneously forbidden from leaving the republic, on
  pain of severe penalty.
  Two modern developments are worth noting. The first is the float process,
  patented in 1959, in which molten glass is poured onto a bath of liquid tin.
  Because the surface of the tin is perfectly level, the glass sets perfectly
  flat, and for the first time large sheets could be made cheaply — which is
  why the buildings around you are made largely of glass. The second is
  optical fibre. Drawn into threads thinner than a human hair, glass now
  carries almost all of the world's long-distance data traffic, in pulses of
  light rather than electrical current. A material we first valued for its
  beauty turned out to be the best medium we have for moving information.
```

## Part 2 — Reading Practice

**Instructions.** 20 minutes. Read the passage and answer Questions 1–14.

### Passage: The Urban Heat Island

```
A
  Anyone who has walked from a park into a city centre on a summer evening has
  felt the effect, even without a name for it. Built-up areas are consistently
  warmer than the countryside around them, and the difference is not trivial:
  in large cities the gap between the urban core and the surrounding rural area
  commonly reaches four or five degrees Celsius on a still night, and
  differences of more than ten degrees have been recorded. The phenomenon has
  been known since 1818, when the amateur meteorologist Luke Howard published
  a set of temperature comparisons between central London and the fields
  outside it, and noted with some puzzlement that the city was warmer,
  particularly after dark.

B
  Howard could describe the pattern but not fully explain it. The modern
  account rests on four mechanisms, of which the most important is
  straightforward: the materials cities are built from are unusually good at
  absorbing and storing heat. Asphalt, brick, concrete and stone have a high
  thermal mass, meaning they take in solar energy through the day and release
  it slowly through the night. A field of grass, by contrast, sheds its heat
  within an hour or two of sunset. This is why the urban heat island is
  strongest at night and often almost undetectable at midday — a fact that
  surprises people who assume the effect is about direct sunshine.

C
  The second mechanism is the loss of evaporative cooling. Vegetation moves
  water from the soil into the air through its leaves, and that change of state
  consumes energy, drawing heat out of the surroundings. A mature tree can
  transpire hundreds of litres on a hot day, functioning, in effect, as a
  substantial air conditioner powered by sunlight. When a landscape is paved,
  rainfall is channelled into drains rather than held in soil and plants, and
  this cooling mechanism is largely eliminated. Third, urban geometry traps
  heat: narrow streets between tall buildings reduce the fraction of sky
  visible from ground level, and since radiation to the open sky is how
  surfaces lose heat at night, restricting that view slows the process
  considerably. Finally, cities generate their own heat — from vehicles,
  industry, air conditioning and human bodies. This last contribution is
  smaller than most people assume in temperate cities, though in dense
  business districts in summer it is far from negligible.

D
  The consequences are more serious than mere discomfort. Heat is among the
  deadliest of weather hazards, and its effects fall unevenly. During the
  European heatwave of 2003, mortality rose sharply in urban centres while
  remaining closer to normal in rural districts a short distance away, and
  subsequent analyses attributed a meaningful share of the difference to the
  heat island effect. The reason is partly physiological: the human body
  recovers from heat stress principally at night, and it is precisely the
  overnight temperature that cities fail to bring down. A daytime peak of
  thirty-eight degrees followed by a cool night is survivable for most people;
  the same peak followed by a night that never falls below twenty-eight is not.

E
  The burden is also distributed along social lines, and not by coincidence.
  Surveys in several countries have found that the hottest districts within a
  city are systematically the poorest, for a chain of reasons that reinforce
  one another. Lower-income neighbourhoods tend to have less tree cover, more
  paved surface, older housing with poorer insulation, and lower rates of air
  conditioning ownership. Residents are also more likely to work outdoors and
  less likely to be able to leave the city during a heatwave. In one widely
  cited American study, historical maps of discriminatory mortgage practices
  from the 1930s were found to predict present-day surface temperatures with
  uncomfortable accuracy — the districts denied investment then are the
  districts with the fewest trees now.

F
  What can be done divides into three approaches. The first is to increase
  vegetation, which addresses two mechanisms at once by providing shade and
  restoring evaporative cooling. Street trees are remarkably effective: a
  well-shaded pavement can be fifteen degrees cooler at the surface than an
  exposed one, and the cost per degree is low compared with almost any
  engineering alternative. The second approach is to change surfaces. Painting
  roofs white, or using pale paving materials, raises the fraction of sunlight
  reflected rather than absorbed. Trials in several cities have shown
  measurable reductions in indoor temperature and in cooling energy use,
  although the benefits at street level are less clear, and some researchers
  have raised the possibility that reflected light increases the heat load on
  pedestrians even as it cools the building above them.

G
  The third approach is the most difficult and the most durable: designing
  cities that do not create the problem in the first place. Orienting streets
  to catch prevailing winds, preserving corridors of open land that allow
  cooler air to flow inwards at night, requiring green roofs on new
  construction, and keeping water in the landscape rather than piping it away
  all belong to this category. None is dramatic in isolation, and all are
  cheaper to build in than to retrofit. The frustration for planners is
  familiar: the measures that would help most must be taken decades before the
  heat becomes intolerable, by administrations that will receive no credit for
  having prevented something that never happened.
```

### Reading — Answer Key and Explanations

```
MATCHING HEADINGS

1. Paragraph A → i  "A discovery that predates its explanation"
   The paragraph describes the effect and Howard's 1818 observation, ending
   with his puzzlement. Heading x (comparisons between cities) is the trap:
   the paragraph does compare city and countryside, but not cities in
   different climates.

2. Paragraph B → ii  "Why the effect peaks after sunset"
   The whole paragraph builds to "this is why the urban heat island is
   strongest at night". Note that thermal mass is the mechanism, but the
   paragraph's PURPOSE is explaining the night-time peak. Main idea beats
   detail.

3. Paragraph C → iv  "Three further contributing processes"
   Evaporative cooling loss, urban geometry, and waste heat — three
   mechanisms, following the one in Paragraph B. Heading v (air conditioning)
   is a DETAIL TRAP: air conditioning is mentioned in one clause.

4. Paragraph D → vii  "Why heat is dangerous at night"
   Mortality plus the physiological explanation that recovery happens
   overnight. The paragraph is about consequences and specifically about the
   night-time mechanism of harm.

5. Paragraph E → vi  "Unequal exposure within the same city"
   Explicitly about distribution along social lines within a city.

6. Paragraph F → viii  "Two interventions and their limits"
   Vegetation and reflective surfaces, plus the qualification that street-level
   benefits are unclear. Heading ix (the cost of street trees) is a KEYWORD
   TRAP — "cost" appears, but as one clause, not the paragraph's point.

7. Paragraph G → iii  "Measures that must be taken early to be effective"
   Design measures, "cheaper to build in than to retrofit", and the closing
   point about acting decades ahead.

Unused headings: v, ix, x. There are always more headings than paragraphs;
the unused ones are built from details and near-misses.

TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN

 8. FALSE
    Paragraph B: "the urban heat island is strongest at night and often almost
    undetectable at midday." This directly contradicts the statement, so FALSE
    rather than NOT GIVEN — you can point at the contradicting words.

 9. FALSE
    Paragraph A calls him "the amateur meteorologist Luke Howard". "Amateur"
    contradicts "employed as a professional scientist". Candidates often mark
    this NOT GIVEN because employment is not discussed directly; the single
    word "amateur" is sufficient contradiction.

10. FALSE
    Paragraph B states that thermal mass is "the most important" mechanism,
    and Paragraph C says waste heat "is smaller than most people assume".
    Two separate contradictions.

11. FALSE
    Paragraph F: "some researchers have raised the possibility that reflected
    light increases the heat load on pedestrians". "All researchers agree"
    is contradicted. Watch the absolute quantifier — but note this is FALSE,
    not NOT GIVEN, because the text explicitly reports disagreement.
    (If the passage had simply not mentioned disagreement, it would be NG.)

SENTENCE COMPLETION

12. evaporative cooling  (or: transpiration)
    Paragraph C: "the loss of evaporative cooling… that change of state
    consumes energy". "Evaporative cooling" is two words and fits the gap
    grammatically after "the process of".

13. sky
    Paragraph C: "reduce the fraction of sky visible from ground level".
    One word. "Open sky" would also be accepted.

14. mortgage practices
    Paragraph E: "historical maps of discriminatory mortgage practices from
    the 1930s". Two words exactly. "Mortgage" alone would not complete the
    sense after "discriminatory".

MARKING
  /14 on one passage. A full paper has three passages and 40 questions.
  Rough equivalence: 10–11 out of 14 on a passage of this difficulty is
  Band 7 territory; 12–13 is Band 8.

PARAPHRASE PAIRS TO LOG (do this for every passage you practise)
  question "most pronounced"        → passage "strongest"
  question "middle of the day"      → passage "at midday"
  question "professional scientist" → passage "amateur meteorologist"
  question "largest single cause"   → passage "the most important" / "smaller than"
  question "benefits people at street level" → passage "benefits at street
                                        level are less clear"
```

## Part 3 — Writing Task 1, Answered at Three Bands

### The Task

> The chart below shows the percentage of commuters in one city using four different modes of transport to travel to work in 2000 and 2020.
>
> Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words.

| Mode | 2000 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Private car | 62% | 41% |
| Bus | 20% | 24% |
| Metro / rail | 12% | 26% |
| Cycling / walking | 6% | 9% |

**Write your own answer before reading the models below.** Twenty minutes, no dictionary.

### Band 5.5 Response

```
The chart shows the percentage of commuters in one city using four different
modes of transport to travel to work in 2000 and 2020.

In 2000, the private car was 62% and in 2020 it was 41%. The bus was 20% in
2000 and it increased to 24% in 2020. The metro was 12% in 2000 and it went up
to 26% in 2020. Cycling and walking was 6% in 2000 and 9% in 2020.

The car was the most popular in both years. The metro increased a lot. The bus
also increased but only a little. Cycling and walking increased also.

Maybe people used the metro more because the government built new lines and
because the traffic became very bad in the city centre.

                                                              [~125 words]
```

```
EXAMINER COMMENTARY — BAND 5.5

Task Achievement 5
  No overview. This alone caps this criterion at 5.
  Under length (125 words against a 150 minimum) — a direct penalty.
  Includes speculation about causes in the final paragraph, which is off-task
  for Task 1 and cannot earn credit.
  The first sentence copies the prompt almost word for word; copied text is
  excluded from the word count, making the shortfall worse than it looks.

Coherence and Cohesion 5
  Data is listed rather than organised. The third paragraph repeats what the
  second has already said. Cohesive devices are limited to "and" and "also".

Lexical Resource 5
  "increased" appears four times; "a lot" and "a little" are imprecise and
  informal. No comparison language beyond "most popular".

Grammar 5
  Sentences are almost all the same simple pattern: X was N% and it was M%.
  "The private car was 62%" is inaccurate — the car was not 62%; 62% of
  commuters used it.
```

### Band 6.5 Response

```
The bar chart compares the proportion of people who travelled to work by four
different means of transport in a single city in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, the use of private cars fell considerably over the twenty-year
period, while all three alternatives became more popular. The most significant
change was the growth of metro and rail travel.

In 2000, the majority of commuters travelled by car, at 62%. By 2020 this had
dropped to 41%, a fall of 21 percentage points, although the car remained the
most widely used option. Metro and rail travel, by contrast, more than doubled
from 12% to 26%, making it the second most common mode by the end of the
period.

The changes for the other two categories were smaller. Bus use rose modestly
from 20% to 24%, and cycling and walking increased from 6% to 9%. Both
remained minority choices throughout, although together they accounted for
one third of journeys in 2020.

                                                              [~165 words]
```

```
EXAMINER COMMENTARY — BAND 6.5

Task Achievement 7
  Overview present and correct, with no figures — good.
  All key features selected: the fall in car use, the doubling of rail, and
  the smaller changes grouped together.
  One factual slip in the final sentence: bus plus cycling/walking is 33%, but
  the sentence implies bus and cycling/walking are "the other two categories"
  and then adds them differently — a reader has to work out what is meant.

Coherence and Cohesion 7
  Clear four-paragraph structure, appropriate linking ("by contrast",
  "although"), sensible grouping of the two smaller categories.

Lexical Resource 6
  Adequate but repetitive: "travelled", "travel", "travel" in close proximity;
  "rose modestly" and "increased" are fine but the range is narrow.
  "more popular" and "most widely used" are the only comparison structures.

Grammar 7
  Some complexity ("although the car remained…", "making it the second most
  common mode"), correct prepositions, correct past tense throughout.
  Sentence length is somewhat uniform.

WHAT WOULD MOVE THIS TO BAND 8
  1. More precise and varied lexis for change: "declined sharply", "a marked
     shift", "over the same period".
  2. A derived comparison rather than only reported figures — e.g. noting that
     the combined share of public transport overtook the car.
  3. More varied sentence structures, including one participle or relative
     clause.
  4. Removing the ambiguity in the last sentence.
```

```
Whether the length of a prison sentence is the most effective tool for
reducing crime is a question that divides both the public and criminologists.
While longer sentences clearly have a role for the most serious offences, I
would argue that measures addressing the causes of offending are generally
more effective.

Those who favour longer sentences advance two main arguments. The first is
deterrence: if the penalty is severe enough, potential offenders will conclude
that crime is not worth the risk. The second is simple incapacitation, since
an offender in custody cannot commit further offences against the public. This
argument is strongest in the case of violent and repeat offenders, where the
protection of others is a legitimate priority regardless of whether the
sentence changes the offender's behaviour.

However, the evidence for deterrence through sentence length is weaker than
many people assume. Research consistently indicates that offenders are
influenced far more by the perceived likelihood of being caught than by the
sentence they might eventually receive, largely because most crime is
opportunistic rather than carefully calculated. Moreover, prison is expensive
and frequently counterproductive: it removes people from employment and
family, exposes them to more experienced offenders, and returns them to
society with a criminal record that makes legitimate work harder to obtain.
High reoffending rates in countries with long average sentences suggest that
the mechanism is not working as intended.

In my view, therefore, the better strategy is to combine targeted custody
with investment in the conditions that produce crime in the first place —
improved policing that raises the chance of detection, education and training
for young people in deprived areas, and rehabilitation programmes within the
prison system itself. Longer sentences should be reserved for offenders who
present a genuine danger, rather than used as a general instrument of policy.
```

### Band 8.0 Response

```
Sentencing policy is unusual among political questions in that the intuitive
answer and the evidential one point in different directions: it feels obvious
that harsher punishment should deter crime, and yet the research record is
stubbornly unsupportive. Having weighed both positions, I believe longer
sentences are justified for a narrow category of dangerous offenders, while
broader reductions in crime depend on measures that operate long before
sentencing.

The case for lengthy custody rests on two distinct claims that are often
conflated. Deterrence assumes a calculating offender who weighs a penalty
against a gain. Incapacitation makes no such assumption: it simply observes
that someone in prison cannot burgle a house. The second claim is far more
robust than the first, and it is why the argument retains genuine force in
respect of violent and persistent offenders. A person who has seriously
harmed others three times is a reasonable candidate for a long sentence
irrespective of whether that sentence reforms him, because the immediate
protection of other people is a sufficient justification on its own.

Deterrence, however, depends on a model of offending that rarely matches
reality. Most acquisitive crime is opportunistic, committed by people who do
not expect to be caught at all, and a penalty that is never mentally
priced cannot influence behaviour. The variable that does appear to matter is
the perceived probability of detection, which is why a visible increase in
policing tends to reduce burglary in a neighbourhood within weeks, whereas a
statutory increase in maximum sentences typically produces no detectable
change. Custody also carries costs that work against its own purpose: it
interrupts employment, weakens family ties, places young offenders in the
company of experienced ones, and issues a criminal record that closes off the
legitimate options a person needs on release. Where sentences are long and
rehabilitation is minimal, reoffending rates are correspondingly high.

The more productive framing, in my view, is to ask what each instrument is
actually for. Prison is a tool for containing danger and should be used
generously for that purpose and sparingly for any other. Reducing the overall
volume of crime is a different problem, and it responds to different levers:
raising detection rates, intervening early with young people at risk, and
treating the addiction and educational failure that precede a substantial
share of offending. Treating sentence length as the primary dial is not merely
ineffective but expensive, since it consumes the budget that the more
effective measures require.

```

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: How many full practice tests should I do before the real exam?**
A: Between four and eight full four-section tests, with the last two in the final ten days. Fewer than four and you will not have trained the stamina needed for two hours and forty-five minutes of continuous concentration, which is a real and separate skill from answering questions well. More than eight tends to be wasted, because the marginal test teaches less than the review of a test you have already taken — the ratio that matters is roughly twenty minutes of error review per thirty minutes of practice. Between full tests, work on individual sections and individual question types, since that is where specific weaknesses are actually fixed. The final test should be at least three days before the exam, not the day before; fatigue at that point costs more than the practice gains.

**Q2: My self-marked Writing band is always higher than a tutor's. Why?**
A: Because self-marking is systematically biased in two specific ways. Candidates over-estimate Grammatical Range and Accuracy, since you cannot see errors you do not know are errors — that is what makes them your errors. And candidates under-estimate the strictness of Task Response, because you know what you meant, so an under-developed idea reads as complete to you and as an assertion to a stranger. The practical fix is to have at least two tasks marked by a qualified human early in your preparation, then use the discrepancy as a calibration offset: if a tutor marks you a band lower on grammar, assume that offset applies to all your self-marking. Counting error-free sentences as a percentage, as described in Chapter 5, is far more reliable than judging your grammar impressionistically.

**Q3: Should I write practice essays by hand or type them?**
A: Match your test format, because both have format-specific skills. If you are taking the paper test, handwrite: you need legible handwriting under time pressure, a reliable sense of how many lines equal 250 words, and the physical stamina for an hour of writing, which is unfamiliar to most people who type all day. If you are taking the computer-delivered test, type in a plain text editor with spell-check disabled, since the exam has no spell-check and practising with one trains you to ignore errors you will have to catch yourself. Mixing formats is the one thing to avoid — candidates who practise typing and then sit the paper test routinely under-write Task 2 because their word-count instinct is calibrated to a screen.

**Q4: Is it useful to memorise model answers like the ones in this chapter?**
A: Memorise the *architecture*, never the sentences. The four-paragraph Task 1 structure, the topic-explain-example-consequence paragraph pattern, and the Part 2 open-context-develop-climax-reflect shape are all worth internalising until they are automatic, because structure is what collapses first under time pressure. The sentences must be yours: memorised language is explicitly excluded from assessment in both Writing and Speaking, and in Speaking the change in rhythm when a candidate switches into recitation is audible on the recording. The productive use of these models is comparative — read the Band 6 and Band 8 versions of the same task, identify the specific differences, then rewrite one of your own old answers applying those differences. That exercise teaches the criteria far better than reproducing a model does.

**Q5: What should I do in the final week before the test?**
A: Taper. Do one full test early in the week, then stop taking full tests. Spend the remaining days on three things: your error log, so you are correcting known specific weaknesses rather than practising randomly; the cheat sheets at the end of each chapter in this phase, which is the highest-density revision material available to you; and ten minutes a day of spoken English out loud, because fluency is a motor skill that degrades quickly without use. Do no new material in the final two days — encountering an unfamiliar question type at that point damages confidence without improving anything. Sleep eight hours for three consecutive nights before the test, check your ID and venue, do a dry run of the journey, and accept that your band is already largely determined. The final week protects it; it does not change it.

2. Rewrite the Band 6.0 Task 2 essay into a Band 8.0 essay yourself, then compare with the model.
3. Rewrite the Band 5.5 Task 1 answer into a Band 8.0 answer, and annotate every change by criterion.
4. Have one Writing task from each simulation marked by a qualified tutor and compute your self-marking offset.
5. Record two full Speaking tests with different partners and mark both against the rubric.
6. Write a Task 2 essay that separates two conflated arguments, as the Band 8 model does with deterrence and incapacitation.
7. Take the four-angle technique and apply it to all six Part 3 questions in the script, recorded.
8. Build a per-question-type accuracy table across all your practice papers and identify your two weakest types.
9. Run a one-week clinic on those two types, then re-measure with a fresh official paper.
10. Two weeks before the test, produce a one-page personal revision sheet: your five grammar errors, your ten weakest vocabulary items, your two weakest question types, and your test-day timing plan.

## Cheat Sheet

```
HOW TO USE PRACTICE MATERIAL
  Learn from any material. MEASURE only with official material
  (Cambridge IELTS 15–20, ielts.org free tests).
  20 minutes of review per 30 minutes of practice. Non-negotiable.

SIMULATION CONDITIONS
  No phone, no dictionary, no pausing, no breaks between sections.
  Listening 30+10 · Reading 60 · Writing 60 → 2h45m continuous.
  Mark the same day. Log errors. Choose ONE change per section.

LISTENING SELF-CHECK AFTER MARKING
  Locate every missed answer in the transcript.
  Categorise: wrote-it-wrong · didn't-recognise · didn't-hear · still-writing ·
              fell-for-a-distractor

READING SELF-CHECK AFTER MARKING
  Categorise every error: couldn't locate · misread a qualifier · vocabulary ·
  inferred instead of read · ran out of time · careless
  Log the paraphrase pair for every answer.

WHAT MOVES WRITING TASK 1: 6.5 → 8.0
  1. a derived comparison no single bar shows
  2. precise varied lexis instead of repeated neutral verbs
  3. structural variety (participle + relative clauses, varied length)
  The four-paragraph architecture does NOT change.

WHAT MOVES WRITING TASK 2: 6.0 → 8.0
  position in the introduction → developed with mechanism AND example →
  counterargument bounded and evaluated → conclusion reframes the question
  Vocabulary: general → topic-specific → precise and idiomatic

WHAT MOVES SPEAKING: 6 → 8
  Part 1: 2–4 sentences with a reason and a detail, not one flat sentence
  Part 2: fill the full two minutes; four tenses; the "why" section is longest
  Part 3: four angles (economic · social · practical · generational),
          then hedge and reframe

SELF-MARKING BIAS TO CORRECT
  You over-estimate Grammar. You under-estimate the strictness of Task Response.
  Count error-free sentences as a percentage instead of judging impressionistically.

FINAL WEEK
  One full test early. Then: error log, cheat sheets, 10 min speaking daily.
  No new material in the last 48 hours. Sleep 8 hours × 3 nights.
```
