# Phase 10 — Chapter 4: IELTS Listening and Reading — Strategies for Band 7+

---

## Chapter Overview

Listening and Reading are objectively marked: each of the 40 questions is right or wrong, there is no partial credit, and there is no examiner judgement to appeal to. That sounds harsh, but it is the best news in the exam — because these are the two sections where technique produces the fastest, most reliable gains. You are not being asked to become a better English speaker. You are being asked to become a better retrieval machine under time pressure.

The numbers make the target concrete:

```
Band 7 needs   Listening         30 / 40      → you may get 10 answers WRONG
               Academic Reading  30 / 40      → you may get 10 answers WRONG
               GT Reading        34 / 40      → you may get 6 answers wrong

Band 8 needs   Listening         35 / 40
               Academic Reading  35 / 40
               GT Reading        37 / 40
```

Ten wrong answers is a lot of margin. Nobody needs to understand every word of an academic passage on Antarctic ice cores. You need to locate 30 specific pieces of information in 60 minutes and write them down without spelling errors.

**Topics:**
- Listening: the four parts, all seven question types, and the distractor patterns that cost most marks
- The prediction protocol — what to do in every pause
- Numbers, spelling, and word limits: the mechanical marks most candidates throw away
- Reading: Academic and General Training formats, and the skim-then-scan workflow
- All eleven Reading question types, each with a specific method
- True/False/Not Given worked in full, with a practice passage and explained answers
- Matching Headings, the type that costs 6.5-band candidates the most marks
- Time management, guessing strategy, and the last-three-minutes protocol
- Computer-delivered vs paper technique differences

## Part 1 — IELTS Listening

### Format

```
Total: 30 minutes of audio + 10 minutes transfer (paper) or 2 minutes check (computer)
       4 parts · 10 questions each · 40 questions total
       The audio plays ONCE. There is no rewind, no pause, no replay.

PART 1  Conversation, everyday/social context                     (easiest)
        e.g. booking accommodation, joining a gym, enquiring about a course
        Two speakers. Question types: form completion, note completion, table
        Content: names, dates, prices, phone numbers, addresses, times

PART 2  Monologue, everyday/social context
        e.g. a tour guide, a facilities briefing, a radio announcement
        One speaker. Question types: multiple choice, matching, map/plan labelling

PART 3  Conversation, educational/training context
        e.g. two students and a tutor discussing an assignment
        2–3 speakers. Question types: multiple choice, matching, flowchart
        Difficulty: speakers disagree, change their minds, and interrupt

PART 4  Monologue, academic lecture                                (hardest)
        e.g. a university lecture on urban planning or marine biology
        One speaker, ~5 minutes with NO break in the middle
        Question types: note completion, summary completion, sentence completion
        This is where most candidates lose 4–6 marks. Drill it disproportionately.
```

Accents across the four parts are deliberately mixed: British, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian, and American. Familiarising yourself with Australian and New Zealand vowels alone is worth practising, since South Asian learners typically have far more exposure to American English.

### The Prediction Protocol — What To Do In Every Pause

The single highest-value Listening habit. Before each part starts you get 20–30 seconds. Use it deliberately, in this order:

```
1. UNDERLINE the keywords in each question stem.
2. PREDICT THE ANSWER TYPE for every gap. Write a symbol next to it:
      #   a number, price, quantity
      D   a date or day
      T   a time
      N   a name (likely to be spelled out)
      PL  a place
      n   a noun (thing)
      adj an adjective
      V   a verb
3. PREDICT THE GRAMMAR: singular or plural? Does the gap follow "a" or "the"?
4. NOTE THE WORD LIMIT and write it at the top: "MAX 2".
5. IDENTIFY THE SIGNPOST for question 1 — the phrase in the question that will
   appear (paraphrased) in the audio just before the answer.

Example:
  Q1  The course begins on ____________.                    → D
  Q2  Students must bring their own ____________.            → n (plural? "own" + ?)
  Q3  The registration fee is £____________.                 → #
  Q4  The tutor's surname is ____________.                   → N — will be SPELLED
  Q5  The class is held in the ____________ Building.        → N/adj + capital letter
```

Predicting the answer type transforms listening from "understand everything" into "wait for a date". Your brain filters for a category, and categories are far easier to catch than meanings.

### Question Types and Methods

```
FORM / NOTE / TABLE / SUMMARY COMPLETION
  The answer must fit GRAMMATICALLY into the gap. If the gap needs a plural,
    a singular answer is wrong even if you heard the word.
  Word limit is absolute: "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" means a
    three-word answer scores zero.
  Hyphenated words ("well-known") count as one word. Numbers written as
    digits count as one word.
  Do NOT change the word form you hear. If the speaker says "cycling",
    write "cycling", not "cycle" — unless grammar in the gap demands otherwise.
  Answers come in the SAME ORDER as the audio. Always.

MULTIPLE CHOICE (one answer)
  Read all options BEFORE the audio. Options are long; reading them cold
    while listening is the main reason people fail this type.
  The correct option paraphrases the audio; it rarely repeats its words.
  Distractor pattern: all three options are mentioned. Only one answers
    the actual question.

MULTIPLE CHOICE (choose two/three from five)
  Order of your answers doesn't matter. Both/all must be right for the mark
    unless the paper says otherwise (usually each is worth one mark).
  Tick options as you hear them; several will be mentioned and rejected.

MATCHING
  Read the option list first and paraphrase each option in your head.
  Options may be used more than once, or not at all — check the instruction.
  Track which you have used, but do not assume each is used exactly once.

MAP / PLAN / DIAGRAM LABELLING
  Before the audio: locate the entrance, the "you are here" point, and any
    named landmark. Trace the route with your finger as the speaker moves.
  Direction language: opposite · adjacent to · alongside · at the far end ·
    on your left as you enter · beyond · just past · in the corner ·
    running parallel to · directly behind
  If you lose the thread, jump to the next labelled point rather than
    reconstructing the path.

SENTENCE COMPLETION
  Read the whole sentence, not just the gap. The words after the gap constrain
    the answer as much as the words before it.

FLOWCHART / PROCESS COMPLETION
  Common in Part 3. Follow the sequence markers: "first", "once you've...",
    "the next stage", "after that", "finally".

SHORT ANSWER QUESTIONS
  "Answer in no more than three words." Give the shortest complete answer.
  Do not write a sentence.
```

### The Six Distractor Patterns

Every IELTS Listening paper is built from the same traps. Learn to hear them coming.

```
1. SELF-CORRECTION — the most common trap of all
   "The session starts at four... sorry, no, they moved it to four-thirty."
   → Answer: 4.30. Always take the LAST value stated.
   Signals: sorry · actually · in fact · I mean · no wait · let me check ·
            hang on · that's wrong

2. NEGATION / REJECTION
   "We used to charge a deposit, but we don't any more."
   → "deposit" is NOT the answer to "what must you pay?"
   Signals: used to · no longer · not any more · we've stopped

3. TWO SPEAKERS DISAGREEING (Part 3 speciality)
   A: "I thought we'd focus on the questionnaire."
   B: "Hmm, wouldn't interviews give richer data?"
   A: "Actually, you're right, let's do interviews."
   → Answer: interviews. Follow the CONCLUSION, not the first proposal.

4. ALL OPTIONS MENTIONED (multiple choice speciality)
   The audio names the library, the sports centre, and the car park.
   Only one of them answers the question as asked.
   → Underline the exact question focus before listening.

5. SIMILAR-SOUNDING NUMBERS
   thirteen / thirty · fifteen / fifty · sixteen / sixty
   Difference: 'THIR-teen' stresses the end; 'THIR-ty' stresses the start.
   Also: "fifteen hundred" = 1500 · "one five oh" = 150 · "double four" = 44
         "oh" or "zero" for 0 · "point five" = .5

6. PARTIAL ANSWER
   Question asks for a STREET. Audio says "45 Chapel Road, Newtown."
   → Answer: Chapel Road. Not the number, not the town — read the gap's context.
```

### Numbers, Names, and Spelling — Free Marks You Must Not Give Away

```
SPELLED-OUT NAMES
  When a speaker says "that's B-E-A-U-M-O-N-T", the spelling IS the answer.
  Write letters as you hear them; do not try to recognise the word first.
  Confusable letter pairs in speech: B/V/P · G/J · M/N · S/F · A/R (in some
  accents) · I/E. If unsure, write both and decide in the transfer time.

BRITISH DATE AND NUMBER CONVENTIONS
  "the fourth of June" → 4 June / 4th June / June 4 — all accepted
  "nineteen ninety-eight" → 1998
  "oh-seven-nine-double-two" → 079 22
  Prices: "twelve pounds fifty" → £12.50
  "a quarter past six" → 6.15 · "half six" (informal BrE) → 6.30

SPELLING RULES THAT COST MARKS
  British or American spelling both accepted (organise / organize) —
    but be internally consistent.
  Plurals matter: if the gap needs "two tickets", writing "ticket" is wrong.
  Capital letters: never penalised for using capitals, so if you are unsure
    about a proper noun, WRITE IT IN CAPITALS. This is a genuinely useful
    exam hack for paper-based tests.
  Words most often misspelled in IELTS answers:
    accommodation · necessary · environment · receipt · questionnaire ·
    restaurant · Wednesday · February · library · government · separate ·
    definitely · maintenance · beginning · leisure · cemetery · February
```

### Note-Taking While Listening

```
Write abbreviated, decode during transfer time (paper-based):
  gov · env · uni · info · dept · yr · wk · hr · £ · # · → · ↑ · ↓ · ~
  Drop vowels for long words: accommodation → accmdtn

RULE 1  Never stop listening to finish writing. Abbreviate and move on.
RULE 2  If you miss one, abandon it instantly. The most expensive single
        error in Listening is losing questions 6, 7, and 8 while agonising
        over 5. Draw a dash and refocus on the NEXT question stem.
RULE 3  Always be reading one question ahead of the audio.
RULE 4  Write something for every question, even a guess, immediately.
        A blank on the answer sheet is a guaranteed zero; there is no penalty.
```

### Paper vs Computer Differences

```
PAPER-BASED
  You write on the question paper, then get 10 minutes to transfer.
  Use the transfer time for: spelling, plurals, word limits, capitals,
    and filling any blanks with guesses.
  Transfer errors are a real and avoidable loss — check the question numbers
    line up, especially after a section with a map.

COMPUTER-DELIVERED
  You type directly into the boxes. No transfer time — only 2 minutes to check.
  Advantages: no transcription errors, word count shown, easy editing.
  Practise: typing while listening, without looking at the keyboard.
    This is why touch typing matters — hunting for keys costs you answers.
  The audio plays through headphones with a volume check beforehand. Set it
    slightly louder than feels natural.
```

### The Listening Review Method — Where the Improvement Actually Happens

Doing more tests does not raise your Listening band. This does:

```
For EVERY question you got wrong:

  1. Find the exact moment in the audio where the answer was given.
     (Use the transcript at the back of the Cambridge book.)
  2. Play those 10 seconds again. Then play them a third time.
  3. Categorise the failure, honestly:
        HEARD IT, WROTE IT WRONG      → spelling / word limit / plural
        HEARD IT, DIDN'T RECOGNISE    → vocabulary gap: add the word to your list
        DIDN'T HEAR IT                → speed / accent / connected speech
        MISSED IT — was still writing  → note-taking discipline
        FELL FOR A DISTRACTOR         → which of the six patterns? name it
  4. Write the paraphrase pair in a notebook:
        question said "the maximum number of participants"
        audio said    "we can take up to twenty at a time"
     IELTS Listening is fundamentally a paraphrase-recognition test. Collecting
     paraphrase pairs is the highest-leverage vocabulary work you can do.

Then: re-listen to the ENTIRE part once with the transcript in front of you,
and once more without it. This is called intensive listening, and 20 minutes
of it is worth more than two new practice tests.
```

## Part 2 — IELTS Reading

### Format

```
ACADEMIC READING
  60 minutes · 3 passages · 40 questions · no extra transfer time
  Passages: 700–1,000 words each, from books, journals, magazines, newspapers
  Difficulty rises: Passage 1 descriptive → Passage 3 abstract/argumentative
  Topics: science, history, environment, psychology, technology, archaeology,
          business, education. No specialist knowledge is ever required.

GENERAL TRAINING READING
  60 minutes · 40 questions · three sections
  Section 1  two or three short factual texts (notices, adverts, timetables)
  Section 2  two workplace texts (contracts, staff handbooks, training material)
  Section 3  one longer general-interest text
  Easier texts, harsher band conversion: Band 7 = 34/40, Band 9 = 40/40.

BOTH: answers must be spelled correctly. There is no negative marking, so
      every blank should be filled with a guess before time runs out.
```

### The Workflow — Skim, Then Question-First Scanning

The most common self-inflicted wound in Reading is reading the whole passage carefully before looking at the questions. It costs 6–8 minutes per passage and it is unnecessary, because the questions only ever ask about a fraction of the text.

```
STEP 1 — SKIM FOR STRUCTURE (90 seconds, no more)
  Read: the title, any subheading, the first and last sentence of each paragraph.
  Goal: a one-line mental map. "Para A = the problem; B = early attempts;
        C = the 1970s breakthrough; D = criticism; E = current use."
  Do NOT read every word. Do NOT look up unknown words.

STEP 2 — READ THE QUESTIONS AND CLASSIFY (60 seconds)
  Identify the types you have. Note which ones follow passage order and which
  don't (see the order table below).
  Underline the keywords in each question — especially names, dates, numbers,
  and technical terms, because those are unparaphrasable anchors.

STEP 3 — DO THE ORDERED, ANCHORED TYPES FIRST
  Sentence completion, summary completion, and T/F/NG generally run in
  passage order, and locating question 1 tells you where question 2 begins.
  Working these first builds your map of the passage for free.

STEP 4 — SCAN, DON'T READ
  Move your eye down the text hunting for your keyword or its synonym.
  When you find the region, STOP scanning and read those two or three
  sentences carefully. The answer is almost always in one sentence.

STEP 5 — DO THE WHOLE-PASSAGE TYPES LAST
  Matching Headings and Matching Information require you to have seen the
  whole text, which you now have.
```

### Which Question Types Follow Passage Order

This table saves enormous time, because it tells you when you can search forward only.

| Question type | Follows passage order? |
|---|---|
| Sentence completion | Yes |
| Summary / note / table completion | Yes (within the section it covers) |
| Short answer questions | Yes |
| Multiple choice | Yes |
| True / False / Not Given | Yes |
| Yes / No / Not Given | Yes |
| Diagram label completion | Usually |
| Matching sentence endings | Yes |
| Matching headings | No — paragraph by paragraph |
| Matching information to paragraphs | **No** — scattered deliberately |
| Matching features (e.g. researchers to findings) | **No** |

The three "No" types are where time disappears. Do them last, and for Matching Information accept that you will have to re-scan.

### Question Type Methods

#### True / False / Not Given

The type that most often separates 6.5 from 7.5. The rules are mechanical, and candidates lose marks by reasoning like humans instead of like the rubric.

```
TRUE       The passage explicitly states or directly entails the statement.
FALSE      The passage states something that CONTRADICTS the statement.
NOT GIVEN  The passage neither confirms nor contradicts it — the information
           simply isn't there, or is there only partially.

THE DECISION PROCEDURE
  1. Find the relevant sentence(s) in the passage. If you cannot find them
     at all, the answer is very likely NOT GIVEN.
  2. Ask: does the passage say the OPPOSITE of this statement?
        Yes → FALSE
  3. Ask: does the passage say EXACTLY this, in different words?
        Yes → TRUE
  4. Neither → NOT GIVEN

WHAT MAKES IT FALSE, NOT NOT-GIVEN
  There must be a contradiction you can point to in the text. If you find
  yourself reasoning "well, it probably isn't true, because…", that is
  NOT GIVEN. FALSE requires evidence, not inference.

WATCH THE QUALIFIERS — they are usually where the answer hides
  all · some · most · always · never · only · the majority · a few
  Passage: "Some researchers argue that..."
  Statement: "Most researchers argue that..."  → NOT GIVEN (or FALSE if the
  passage explicitly says it is a minority view)

  Passage: "The technique was first used in Japan."
  Statement: "The technique was only ever used in Japan."  → NOT GIVEN

YES / NO / NOT GIVEN — the same procedure, but applied to the WRITER'S OPINION
  Used when the passage is argumentative. YES = the writer agrees with the
  statement; NO = the writer disagrees; NOT GIVEN = the writer's view on this
  is not expressed. Watch for the difference between what the writer believes
  and what the writer reports others believing.
```

**Worked practice.** Read this short passage and answer the six statements. Answers with explanations follow — cover them first.

```
PASSAGE

  The idea that a programmer's productivity can be measured by the number of
  lines of code they write has proved remarkably durable, despite being
  discredited almost as soon as it was proposed. In the 1970s, several large
  software firms adopted lines-per-day as a formal performance metric, and a
  handful of academic studies from the period appeared to support the practice.
  Within a decade, however, the approach had fallen out of favour among
  researchers, largely because of a simple observation: the most valuable
  contribution a skilled engineer makes is often the deletion of code that
  should never have been written. A system that achieves the same result in
  half the code is generally cheaper to maintain, easier to reason about, and
  less likely to fail.
  Curiously, the metric survives in industry long after its intellectual
  collapse, and it is not difficult to see why. Lines of code are trivially
  easy to count, whereas the qualities that actually matter — clarity,
  robustness, appropriate design — resist quantification almost entirely.
  Managers under pressure to demonstrate progress will tend to reach for
  whatever number is available, and an unreliable number often feels more
  defensible than an honest judgement.

STATEMENTS

  1. Lines-per-day was used as a performance measure by some large companies
     in the 1970s.
  2. All academic research in the 1970s supported counting lines of code.
  3. Researchers abandoned the metric within ten years of its introduction.
  4. Deleting code can be more valuable than adding it.
  5. Smaller codebases always contain fewer defects.
  6. The writer believes managers use the metric because they are lazy.
```

```
ANSWERS AND REASONING

1. TRUE
   "several large software firms adopted lines-per-day as a formal performance
   metric" in the 1970s. "Several large firms" = "some large companies".
   Direct match through paraphrase.

2. FALSE
   The passage says "a handful of academic studies... appeared to support the
   practice." "A handful" contradicts "all". This is a qualifier trap, and it
   is FALSE rather than NOT GIVEN because the text supplies a contradicting
   quantity.

3. TRUE
   "Within a decade, however, the approach had fallen out of favour among
   researchers." "Within a decade" = "within ten years"; "fallen out of favour
   among researchers" = "researchers abandoned".

4. TRUE
   "the most valuable contribution a skilled engineer makes is often the
   deletion of code that should never have been written."

5. NOT GIVEN
   The passage says a smaller system is "less likely to fail" — a probabilistic
   claim about failure. The statement says "always contain fewer defects", which
   is an absolute claim about defect counts. The passage neither asserts nor
   contradicts it. Tempting to mark FALSE because of "always"; but there is no
   contradicting statement, so NOT GIVEN is correct.

6. NOT GIVEN
   The writer says managers are "under pressure to demonstrate progress" and
   reach for available numbers. That is an explanation about incentives and
   measurability, not about laziness. Do not import a motive the writer never
   states. This is the classic Y/N/NG trap: a plausible inference is still
   NOT GIVEN.
```

Statements 5 and 6 are exactly where Band 6.5 candidates lose marks. The instinct is to reason about the world; the requirement is to reason only about the text.

#### Matching Headings

The highest-cost type for most candidates, because it looks like it needs the whole passage read carefully — and because there are always more headings than paragraphs.

```
METHOD
  1. Read the headings first and paraphrase each in three words in the margin.
     Group any that look similar — those are the deliberate near-misses.
  2. For each paragraph, read the FIRST and LAST sentences fully, then skim
     the middle. Ask one question only: "what is this paragraph mainly about?"
  3. Write your own three-word summary of the paragraph BEFORE looking at the
     heading list again. This prevents the list from biasing you.
  4. Match your summary to a heading. If two headings fit, ask which one
     covers the WHOLE paragraph rather than one detail in it.
  5. Do the paragraphs you are certain about first, then eliminate.

THE THREE TRAPS
  DETAIL TRAP     A heading matches one sentence in the paragraph but not its
                  overall point. The paragraph's main idea always wins.
  KEYWORD TRAP    A heading repeats a word from the paragraph. Word overlap is
                  usually bait; correct headings tend to be paraphrases.
  SCOPE TRAP      Two headings differ only in scope ("The causes of X" vs
                  "One cause of X"). Count how many causes the paragraph gives.

  Never leave a heading question blank. If you are down to two paragraphs and
  two headings, guess — you have a 50% chance for free.
```

#### Matching Information to Paragraphs

```
  "Which paragraph contains the following information?"
  NOT in passage order. Paragraphs may be used more than once, or not at all.

METHOD
  Do this type LAST, when you know the passage.
  Work from the QUESTION side: take the question's most unparaphrasable anchor
    (a name, a number, a date, a technical term) and scan for that.
  If the question has no anchor, use the paragraph map you built while skimming.
  Budget hard: this type can eat 15 minutes. Cap it, guess the remainder,
    and protect the rest of the paper.
```

#### Sentence and Summary Completion

```
  Answers come from the passage, usually word-for-word (check the instruction:
  "words from the passage" vs "from the list below").

METHOD
  1. Read the sentence and predict the answer's PART OF SPEECH and number.
  2. Identify the anchor word and scan for it or its synonym.
  3. Copy the word exactly. Spelling errors are marked wrong.
  4. Obey the word limit absolutely.

Common failure: answering with a grammatically impossible word.
  "The researchers were surprised by the ____________ of the results."
  → needs a NOUN ("consistency"), not an adjective ("consistent").
  If your answer doesn't fit the grammar, it is the wrong answer, even if
  the meaning feels right.
```

#### Multiple Choice, Matching Features, Sentence Endings

```
MULTIPLE CHOICE
  Read the STEM carefully and decide what is actually being asked before
    reading the options — the options are designed to be individually plausible.
  Eliminate rather than select: cross out the two you can disprove from the text.
  Options containing absolutes (always, never, all, none, only) are more often
    wrong, because academic passages hedge.
  The right answer paraphrases the text; a wrong option often quotes it.

MATCHING FEATURES (e.g. match findings to researchers)
  Names are unparaphrasable — scan for them, underline every occurrence first,
    then read around each one. This is fast once you stop reading linearly.

MATCHING SENTENCE ENDINGS
  Grammar first: eliminate endings that cannot grammatically follow the stem.
  This alone usually cuts the list in half.
```

#### Diagram Label Completion and Short Answer

```
DIAGRAM LABELS
  Usually drawn from one localised part of the text. Find that section first,
  then work through the labels in order, following the description's sequence.

SHORT ANSWER
  Give the minimum: "In no more than three words" — three words maximum, but
  two is fine. Never write a full sentence; extra words can invalidate a
  correct answer.
```

### Vocabulary Strategy: Paraphrase, Not Dictionary

```
IELTS Reading is a synonym-matching exercise in disguise. The question will
almost never use the passage's own words for the key concept.

  question: "young people"        passage: "adolescents" / "the under-25s"
  question: "cheap"              passage: "affordable" / "low-cost" / "economical"
  question: "damage"             passage: "detrimental effect" / "harm" / "impair"
  question: "increase"           passage: "a marked rise" / "escalation" / "surge"
  question: "problem"            passage: "obstacle" / "drawback" / "shortcoming"
  question: "important"          passage: "pivotal" / "of considerable significance"
  question: "start"              passage: "the outset" / "initially" / "originated"
  question: "worker"             passage: "employee" / "labour force" / "personnel"

BUILD YOUR OWN LIST. After every practice passage, write down the
question-word / passage-word pair for each question. Fifty pairs collected
this way is worth more than a thousand words of an academic word list,
because they are drawn from the exact register the exam uses.

Unknown words in the passage: do NOT stop. Three moves, in order:
  1. Is this word needed for any question? Usually not — skip it.
  2. Guess from morphology: prefix/suffix/root. ("un-", "-less", "hydro-")
  3. Guess from the sentence's logic: is the surrounding argument positive
     or negative? contrast or continuation?
```

### Time Management

```
ACADEMIC READING — 60 minutes, no transfer time
  Passage 1   16 minutes    (bank 4 minutes here — it is the easiest)
  Passage 2   19 minutes
  Passage 3   22 minutes
  Final       3 minutes     fill every blank, check answer numbering

GENERAL TRAINING READING — 60 minutes
  Section 1   15 minutes
  Section 2   18 minutes
  Section 3   24 minutes
  Final       3 minutes

HARD RULES
  Never spend more than 90 seconds on a single question. Mark it, guess,
    move on, and return only if you have time at the end.
  Never leave Passage 3 with fewer than 20 minutes remaining. If you are at
    minute 40 and still on Passage 2, guess the rest of Passage 2 immediately
    and move — the 13 marks in Passage 3 are worth more than 3 marks of
    perfectionism in Passage 2.
  Write answers directly on the answer sheet as you go (paper-based Reading
    has NO transfer time — this catches candidates who assume it works like
    Listening).

THE LAST THREE MINUTES
  1. Every blank gets a plausible answer. T/F/NG blanks: write NOT GIVEN
     (it is the most common single answer in most papers).
  2. Matching Headings blanks: use any unused heading.
  3. Check that your answer numbers align with the question numbers.
  4. Check word limits on completion answers.
```

### The Reading Review Method

```
For every wrong answer, write one line in your error log:

  Q#  type  |  what I answered  |  correct  |  WHY I was wrong

Categorise the WHY into one of six:
  1. Didn't find the right part of the passage      → scanning technique
  2. Found it but misread the qualifier            → all/some/only/most
  3. Vocabulary: didn't know the synonym            → add the paraphrase pair
  4. Inferred instead of reading                    → T/F/NG discipline
  5. Ran out of time                                → pacing
  6. Careless: word limit, spelling, wrong box      → final-check protocol

After five papers, count the categories. Whichever has the most entries is
your entire study plan for the next week. Most candidates discover that
one or two categories account for 70% of their losses — and both of those
are trainable in days, not months.
```

## Common Errors and Fixes

| Section | Error | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Still writing Q5 while Q6 is spoken | Abbreviate ruthlessly; abandon a missed answer instantly |
| Listening | Writing the first number heard | Wait for self-correction signals; take the last value |
| Listening | Exceeding the word limit | Write "MAX 2" at the top of every completion set |
| Listening | Singular/plural mismatch | Predict the grammar of the gap before the audio starts |
| Listening | Misspelling a heard word | Use CAPITALS for proper nouns; drill the top-20 spelling list |
| Listening | Not reading ahead in the pauses | Fixed protocol: underline, predict type, note the word limit |

## Interview Preparation

**Q1: What exactly is the difference between "False" and "Not Given"?**
A: False means the passage contains a statement that contradicts the claim; Not Given means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it. The test is whether you can point to the contradicting words. If a passage says "the technique was developed in 1995" and the statement says "the technique was developed in 1998", that is False, because the dates conflict. If the statement says "the technique was expensive to develop", that is Not Given, because cost is never mentioned — no matter how likely it seems. The most common error is marking something False on the basis of inference: "the passage says it was popular, so it can't have been a failure" is reasoning about the world, not reading the text. A useful discipline is to require yourself to underline the contradicting phrase before writing False; if you cannot underline anything, the answer is Not Given. Note also that Not Given is statistically the most common single answer in many papers, so if you are guessing at the end, it is the best guess.

**Q2: What is the fastest way to improve a Reading score?**
A: Three changes, in order of return. First, stop reading the passage before reading the questions: a 90-second structural skim followed by question-first scanning recovers six to eight minutes per passage, and time pressure is what causes most Reading losses. Second, enforce a 90-second-per-question cap, because a single hard Matching Information question can silently consume the time needed for eight easy ones later. Third, keep a paraphrase log: after every passage, write down the question's wording next to the passage's wording for each answer. IELTS Reading is fundamentally synonym recognition, and fifty locally collected paraphrase pairs are worth more than a generic vocabulary list. Beyond those, work by question type rather than by whole test — if Matching Headings is costing you five marks a paper, do thirty Matching Headings questions in isolation rather than three more full tests.

**Q3: How should I handle Listening Part 4, where most people lose marks?**
A: Part 4 is a five-minute academic monologue with no break, usually tested with note or summary completion, so the difficulty is sustained concentration plus dense academic vocabulary. Three specific tactics help. Use the preparation time to read all ten gaps and predict the part of speech and number for each — because the notes are usually a structured outline, the gaps will follow the lecture's own logical order, and knowing you are waiting for a plural noun narrows what you listen for enormously. Second, follow the lecture's signposting rather than trying to hear every word: "the first factor", "a second consideration", "by contrast", "the implication of this" tell you exactly which gap you are approaching. Third, if you miss a gap, mark it with a dash and move to the next stem immediately; the single most costly Part 4 habit is trying to recover one answer and losing three. In review, always re-listen to the whole of Part 4 with the transcript, then once without it.

**Q4: Should I take the paper or computer version for Listening and Reading?**
A: For most software engineers, computer-delivered is the better choice. Typing removes transcription errors entirely, the word count and highlighting tools reduce mechanical mistakes, and results arrive in three to five days rather than thirteen. The trade-offs are that Listening gives you only two minutes to check rather than ten minutes to transfer, and Reading requires you to use on-screen highlighting instead of annotating freely in the margin. Both are easily handled if you complete at least two official computer-delivered practice tests beforehand to internalise the interface. Choose paper only if you find on-screen reading of long passages genuinely tiring, or if your typing while listening is slow — which is itself worth fixing, since touch typing is a professional asset independently of IELTS.

**Q5: Is it worth memorising vocabulary lists for Listening and Reading?**
A: Partly. Generic lists like the Academic Word List have real value for Reading, because they cover the register academic passages are written in, and 570 word families is a manageable target over eight weeks. But the higher-return activity is collecting your own paraphrase pairs from practice material, because the exam does not test whether you know a word in isolation — it tests whether you recognise that "adolescents" in the passage answers a question about "young people". Every wrong answer in Reading and every missed answer in Listening should produce one line in a paraphrase log. After five papers you will have well over a hundred pairs drawn from exactly the register you are being tested on, and that list also feeds directly into your Writing and Speaking vocabulary, which a memorised word list rarely does.

## Practical Tasks

### Beginner (10 Tasks)
1. Complete one Listening part with the transcript open, following the audio word by word, to see how answers are signalled.
2. Do the prediction protocol on twenty completion gaps without listening: mark each with the expected answer type.
3. Drill numbers: have someone read twenty prices, dates, and phone numbers aloud in British format; write them down.
4. Practise spelled-out names: have someone spell fifteen surnames aloud at natural speed.
5. Learn the twenty most-misspelled IELTS answer words and test yourself by dictation.
6. Skim three passages for 90 seconds each and write a one-line summary of every paragraph.
7. Do twenty True/False/Not Given questions untimed, writing a one-line justification for each answer.
8. Do one Matching Headings set untimed, summarising each paragraph in three words before looking at the headings.
9. Time yourself on one Reading passage and compare against the 16/19/22-minute budget.
10. Build a paraphrase log with the first twenty question-word / passage-word pairs from your practice.

### Intermediate (10 Tasks)
1. Do one full timed Listening paper, then complete the full review method on every wrong answer.
2. Do one full timed Reading paper and log every error against the six WHY categories.
3. Drill Listening Part 4 alone, five times in a week, from five different tests.
4. Drill your worst Reading question type in isolation — thirty questions in one sitting.
5. Practise the last-three-minutes protocol: with three minutes left, fill every blank and check numbering.
6. Do one Reading paper where you deliberately cap every question at 90 seconds, guessing when the cap hits.
7. Collect and categorise ten examples of the six Listening distractor patterns from real audio.
8. Practise Matching Information under a hard eight-minute cap, guessing the remainder.
9. Do one Listening paper at 1.25× speed, then at normal speed, and note the difference in comprehension.
10. Reach one hundred entries in your paraphrase log.

### Advanced (10 Tasks)
1. Complete four full Listening and four full Reading papers in one week under strict exam conditions.
2. Build a per-question-type accuracy table across those papers and identify your two weakest types by percentage.
3. Run a one-week clinic on those two types only, sixty questions each.
4. Do a Reading paper in 50 minutes instead of 60 to build a time buffer, then return to 60.
5. Complete two official computer-delivered practice tests and time your on-screen highlight workflow.
6. Do intensive listening on one full Part 3 and one full Part 4: transcript-read, shadow, then listen clean.
7. Take a Part 4 transcript and rewrite the note-completion answers as full sentences, to train grammatical fit.
8. Practise with unfamiliar accents specifically: five Australian and five New Zealand Listening parts.
9. Sit a full Listening + Reading session back to back, as on test day, and check whether accuracy drops in the second hour.
10. Convert every practice score to a band using the tables in Chapter 1, and confirm you are consistently at or above your target with margin.

## Cheat Sheet

```
RAW SCORE TARGETS
  Band 7  Listening 30/40 · Academic Reading 30/40 · GT Reading 34/40
  Band 8  Listening 35/40 · Academic Reading 35/40 · GT Reading 37/40
  No negative marking. NEVER leave a blank.

LISTENING PARTS
  1 social conversation (forms, names, numbers)      easiest
  2 social monologue (map, multiple choice)
  3 academic discussion (matching, MCQ — speakers change their minds)
  4 academic lecture (note/summary completion)        hardest — drill this

PREDICTION PROTOCOL (every pause)
  underline keywords → predict answer TYPE (# D T N PL n adj V) →
  predict singular/plural → write the word limit → find the signpost

SIX DISTRACTOR PATTERNS
  1 self-correction (take the LAST value)     4 all options mentioned
  2 negation ("no longer", "used to")         5 similar numbers (13/30, 15/50)
  3 speakers disagreeing (follow the CONCLUSION) 6 partial answer (read the gap)

LISTENING MECHANICS
  Hyphenated = one word · digits = one word · CAPITALS are never penalised
  Missed one? Abandon it in one second. Protect the next three.
  Paper: 10 min transfer — check spelling, plurals, word limits, numbering
  Computer: 2 min only — type as you listen

READING WORKFLOW
  1 skim structure 90 sec  2 read + classify questions  3 ordered types first
  4 scan for keyword/synonym, then read 2–3 sentences  5 whole-passage types last

ORDER RULES
  Follow passage order: sentence/summary completion · short answer · MCQ · T/F/NG
  Do NOT follow order: matching headings · matching information · matching features

T / F / NG DECISION
  Can't find it at all              → NOT GIVEN
  Passage says the opposite         → FALSE  (you must be able to underline it)
  Passage says the same, reworded   → TRUE
  Plausible but unstated inference  → NOT GIVEN
  Watch: all · some · most · only · always · never

MATCHING HEADINGS
  Summarise the paragraph in 3 words YOURSELF, then match.
  Main idea beats detail · paraphrase beats keyword overlap · check scope.

TIME BUDGET (Reading)
  Academic  P1 16 min · P2 19 min · P3 22 min · 3 min final check
  GT        S1 15 min · S2 18 min · S3 24 min · 3 min final check
  90 seconds max per question. No transfer time in Reading.

LAST 3 MINUTES
  Fill every blank · T/F/NG guess = NOT GIVEN · unused heading for blanks ·
  check numbering · check word limits

THE TWO HABITS THAT MOVE BANDS
  1. Locate every missed Listening answer in the transcript.
  2. Log the paraphrase pair for every Reading answer. Target 100+ pairs.
```
